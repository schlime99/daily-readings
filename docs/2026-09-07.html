Twenty-third Week in Ordinary Time

Christ has been sacrificed — so clear out the old yeast and stretch out your hand.

First reading1 Corinthians 5:1-8
PsalmPsalm 5:5-6, 7, 12
GospelLuke 6:6-11

Two very different scenes today, and they do not belong to the same argument. Paul is writing to a church in moral crisis; Luke is showing us Jesus in a synagogue on a contested sabbath. These two readings are simply on their own tracks. But each one, taken seriously, is more than enough.

The first reading — 1 Corinthians 5:1-8

By the time Paul writes this, his relationship with the Corinthians is already complicated. First Corinthians is an early letter, probably from around 54 or 55 AD, and it is essentially Paul's attempt to deal with a community that has received the gospel and promptly made a mess of it. They are gifted, enthusiastic, and deeply confused about what following Christ actually requires of them. Chapter five opens a section on sexual ethics, and Paul is blunt in a way that must have landed like cold water.

The specific situation he names — a man living with his father's wife — would have been scandalous even by Roman standards, and Paul knows it. But what disturbs him almost as much as the sin is the community's attitude toward it. They are inflated with pride. The Greek behind that phrase suggests a kind of puffing up, a self-congratulatory smugness, and Paul finds it baffling. They seem to regard their tolerance as a sign of spiritual maturity, perhaps even of freedom in Christ. Paul will have none of it.

Then he reaches for an image every Jewish person in his audience would have felt in their bones: yeast at Passover. In the days before the great feast, Jewish households swept out every scrap of leavened bread. The purge was thorough and deliberate. Old yeast, if left behind, will work its way through everything — that is, after all, the point of yeast. Paul uses this as a picture of how unaddressed sin moves through a community. A little tolerance of serious wrongdoing does not stay contained; it shapes the moral atmosphere of the whole group.

And then comes one of those sudden pivots that make Paul so remarkable. He does not just land the warning and move on. He names the reason the community can afford to clear out the old leaven: our Paschal Lamb, Christ, has been sacrificed. The feast has begun. The reality that Passover pointed toward has arrived. To keep living in the old yeast, then, is not just a moral failure — it is a failure of comprehension. It is like someone who keeps eating the old bread while the feast is already on the table.

The psalm, Psalm 5, picks up the serious side of this: God does not delight in wickedness, and the arrogant cannot stand in his presence. But the psalm ends on a note Paul would recognize — those who take refuge in God will be glad, will exult, will be protected. The severity is not the last word.

The Gospel — Luke 6:6-11

Luke's scene is tightly observed. Jesus is teaching in a synagogue — the day is the sabbath — and there is a man present whose right hand is withered. Luke specifies the right hand, which is a detail that matters: in the ancient world, the right hand was the working hand, the hand for public life, for trades, for greeting. A withered right hand was a visible, daily limitation.

The scribes and Pharisees are also present, and Luke is careful to say they are watching closely. They are not there to learn. They want to catch Jesus in a violation so they can bring a charge. Jesus is fully aware of this — he realized their intentions — and his response is extraordinary in its directness. He does not work quietly or defensively. He calls the man forward, into the open, in front of everyone: Come up and stand before us.

This is not recklessness. It is a kind of clarity. Jesus poses a question that cuts to the heart of the dispute: is it lawful to do good on the sabbath, or to do evil? To save life or to destroy it? The question is not really about sabbath law in the technical sense. It is about what the sabbath is for. The day belongs to God, and God is not neutral about suffering. To pass by a person who can be helped, and to call that reverence, is its own kind of damage.

He tells the man to stretch out his hand. The man does, and the hand is restored. And the religious authorities respond with rage and begin conspiring about what to do with him. The healing that should have prompted wonder instead accelerates the opposition.

What the law cannot see

These two passages are not in dialogue with each other, but they both circle something similar: the gap between the form of faithfulness and its substance. The Corinthians look tolerant; they are actually negligent. The Pharisees look pious; they are actually indifferent to a suffering man in front of them. In both cases, something important has been missed — either the seriousness of sin, or the urgency of mercy — because the surface has been mistaken for the whole.

Paul's answer is to look at the Passover and understand what has actually happened. Luke's answer is to watch what Jesus does with his hands, and then notice what the man does with his.

To carry today

Think of a place in your life where you have been going through the motions of something — a habit of faith, a relationship, a kindness you keep meaning to show — without much of the substance behind it. Ask yourself honestly what it would look like to actually mean it today, rather than perform it. That is the clearing-out Paul is talking about, and the stretched hand Luke is describing.
