Friday · 28 August

Memorial of

Saint Augustine, Bishop and Doctor of the Church

The foolishness of God is wiser than anything we could have figured out on our own.

First reading1 Corinthians 1:17-25
PsalmPsalm 33:1-2, 4-5, 10-11
GospelMatthew 25:1-13

Today the Church remembers Augustine of Hippo, one of the most brilliant and restless minds in Christian history — a man who spent his early life looking for wisdom in every place it wasn't, and who finally found it in the very place he'd dismissed as beneath him: the crucified Christ. The readings for his memorial are, as a result, almost uncannily well chosen. Paul is writing about the scandal of a gospel that sounds foolish, and Jesus is telling a story about people who thought they had it figured out and didn't.

The first reading — 1 Corinthians 1:17-25

Paul is writing to Corinth, a city drunk on rhetoric and philosophy. The Greeks prized eloquence — a well-constructed argument was a kind of public spectacle, a form of status. The Jews, for their part, wanted proof: signs that God was acting, miracles that demonstrated divine power in recognizable, dramatic form. Into this world Paul walks with a message about a man who was executed, hung on a cross outside a city wall, dead and buried — and who is somehow the center of everything.

It is worth sitting with how offensive this actually was. A crucified messiah was, to Jewish ears, a contradiction in terms: the law itself said that a man hung on a tree was cursed by God, so how could such a person be the Anointed One? And to Greek ears, worshipping a condemned criminal was simply embarrassing — the kind of thing slaves and rubes did, not educated people. Paul does not soften this. He calls it a stumbling block and foolishness, because that is exactly what it was to its first hearers.

And yet. Paul's point is not that Christianity is irrational, but that the terms of rationality have been reset. If God chose to save the world through suffering and apparent failure rather than through conquest and impressive demonstration, then our intuitions about what power looks like and what wisdom looks like are simply wrong. Not inadequate — wrong. The plans of nations come to nothing, the psalm echoes back, but the design of God's heart stands through all generations. Psalm 33 is a perfect answer to Paul: the world's calculations fail, and the Lord's will holds. That is not a comfortable message for anyone who prefers to be in control of their own spiritual résumé.

Augustine knew this passage intimately, and he knew it from the inside. He was exactly the kind of person Paul describes — trained in rhetoric, admired for his eloquence, convinced that if God existed, the path to him ran through the finest minds of the age. He tried Manichaeism, he tried Neoplatonism, he tried living however he pleased. It was only when he stopped treating Christianity as something to be evaluated and submitted to it as something to be received that anything changed. The cross looked like foolishness to him for years. Then it looked like everything.

The Gospel — Matthew 25:1-13

The parable of the ten bridesmaids appears near the end of Matthew's Gospel, in a stretch of teaching about the end times and the return of the Son of Man. Jesus is not speaking casually here; he is preparing his disciples for a long wait and telling them what that wait will require.

The setup is familiar: ten young women wait for a bridegroom who is coming to take his bride to the wedding feast. Five bring extra oil for their lamps; five don't bother. The bridegroom is delayed — longer than anyone expected — and when he finally arrives at midnight, the unprepared five have run out of light. They rush off to buy more oil and miss the moment entirely. When they return, the door is shut, and the bridegroom says something devastating: "I do not know you."

The oil is the part of this parable that people have argued about for centuries, because Jesus doesn't explain it, and the wise bridesmaids are oddly unsentimental about sharing it. That refusal is often taken as cold, but the parable seems to be saying something specific: there is a kind of readiness that cannot be borrowed or transferred at the last minute. You can share money, you can lend someone your coat — but you cannot hand over the interior disposition that comes from years of showing up, of staying awake, of tending to the slow and unglamorous work of faith. The door closes not because God is cruel but because some things genuinely cannot be improvised at midnight.

The connection: wisdom that looks wrong until it doesn't

What Paul and Jesus are both circling is the question of what it means to be truly prepared — truly wise — in God's terms rather than the world's. Paul's Corinthians thought they knew what wisdom looked like. The foolish bridesmaids thought they were ready enough. Both were operating on assumptions that seemed reasonable and turned out to be catastrophically off. The cross looks like a defeat until you understand what victory actually is. The wedding feast looks like something you can scramble to catch up with until the door closes.

Augustine spent the first half of his life running exactly this kind of miscalculation. He was not a bad person so much as a person who was confident he understood the shape of things, and who discovered, slowly and painfully, that he did not. His feast day falls on readings that describe the very trap he finally escaped: the assumption that we are wise enough, prepared enough, clever enough to find our way without yielding to something that looks, from the outside, like foolishness.

To carry today

Think about where you are quietly confident that you have things under control — your faith, your relationships, your understanding of what you need. Ask honestly whether that confidence is based on something real and ongoing, or whether it is the assumption that you can always sort it out later when it matters more. The invitation is not to anxiety but to attentiveness: what does staying awake actually look like in your ordinary day today?
