Twentieth Week in Ordinary Time

God fires the shepherds who fed themselves and promises to tend the flock himself.

First readingEzekiel 34:1-11
PsalmPsalm 23:1-3a, 3b-4, 5, 6
GospelMatthew 20:1-16

Today's readings do not share a single story, but they share a single conviction: that God's generosity operates on a scale human beings keep finding alarming. Ezekiel watches the leaders of Israel exploit the people they were appointed to protect, and God responds not with a management correction but with a personal takeover. Matthew's landowner hands out the same full day's wage to someone who worked one hour as to someone who worked twelve, and the workers who were there first find this frankly offensive. Both readings trust that God means what he says — and both readings suggest that what he says is more than we were expecting.

The first reading — Ezekiel 34:1-11

Ezekiel is writing from Babylon, in exile. Jerusalem has fallen, or is about to, and the question hanging over everything is: how did this happen to the people of God? Ezekiel's answer, delivered with barely contained fury, is that it happened because the leaders — kings, priests, officials, anyone who held authority over others — turned their office into a feeding operation for themselves. The image of the shepherd was completely standard for rulers in the ancient Near East: a good king was one who gathered the flock, protected it, and brought it to good pasture. So when Ezekiel lists what these shepherds failed to do — strengthen the weak, heal the sick, bind up the injured, bring back the strayed, seek the lost — he is not being poetic. He is reading out the job description they abandoned. And what did they do instead? They ate the milk. They wore the wool. They slaughtered the fat ones. The very animals they were supposed to tend, they consumed.

The indictment is three layers deep: the shepherds fed themselves, the sheep suffered and scattered, and then the sheep became food for the wild beasts. Three times in this passage God says "my sheep" — not Israel's sheep, not the shepherds' sheep. My sheep. There is something almost fierce in that repetition, as if God is reclaiming ownership out loud before taking action. And the action he announces is exactly that: I am coming against these shepherds, I will remove the sheep from their care, and then — the pivot the whole passage turns on — "I myself will look after and tend my sheep."

This is where Psalm 23 arrives and earns its place with complete precision. The psalm is the fulfillment of the promise. Every image in it — verdant pastures, restful waters, the rod and the staff, the table spread in the sight of enemies — is a portrait of what God does when he becomes the shepherd himself. The early hearers of Ezekiel would have known this psalm by heart, and hearing the two together, they would have understood: the failure of every human shepherd only clears the way for the one who will not fail.

The Gospel — Matthew 20:1-16

This parable sits in the middle of a long section of Matthew where Jesus is teaching about what the kingdom operates like, which is apparently not like anything his disciples are expecting. The workers hired at dawn have a perfectly reasonable complaint by any fair labor standard: they worked longer, they bore the heat, and they received exactly the same wage as people who showed up at five in the afternoon and worked one hour before sundown. Nothing about the landowner's behavior is economically logical. He goes out five times — dawn, nine, noon, three, five — which is itself a bit excessive for a vineyard manager, as if he is looking for every possible person who hasn't been hired yet. When he finally asks the last group why they've been standing there all day, they say, simply: because no one hired us. And he sends them in too.

The grumbling at the end is utterly human and almost sympathetic. The workers who bore the heat are not wicked — they kept their agreement, they did their work, and they feel the sting of a comparison they did not ask for. But the landowner's question cuts straight to it: "Are you envious because I am generous?" The Greek word underneath that phrase has the sense of an evil eye, of looking at someone else's good with a kind of malevolent disappointment. The thing that has gone wrong is not the landowner's accounting; it is that the early workers have begun to measure their own good by what others received. The moment you do that, someone else's blessing starts to feel like your diminishment.

Where God's anger and God's extravagance converge

These two readings are not on separate tracks today, even though weekday pairings often are. Ezekiel's God is furious at shepherds who managed the flock for their own benefit, who kept careful account of what they could extract. Matthew's landowner keeps no such account at all — or rather, he keeps an account that nobody expected, one that tallies need and not hours. The same divine character underlies both: a God who is not interested in what he can get from the people, and who is frankly impatient with leaders — or workers, or anyone — who reduce relationship to transaction. Ezekiel's shepherds were calculating. The complaining workers at the end of the parable have caught the same disease: calculating what they deserve, measuring grace by labor, and finding the result insufficient. The remedy, in both cases, is the same God who announces he will show up personally and tend to things himself.

To carry today

Consider where you have been quietly keeping score — not maliciously, but with that low-grade arithmetic that measures what you have given against what others seem to receive. Ask whether that accounting is helping you, or whether it is making someone else's good feel like a grievance. The invitation today is not to stop noticing fairness altogether, but to loosen the grip a little and see if what looks like inequality might be someone else finally being found.
