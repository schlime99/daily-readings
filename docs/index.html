Thirtieth Week in Ordinary Time

Jesus refuses to flee, and names what he is walking toward with open eyes.

First readingEphesians 6:10-20
PsalmPsalm 144:1b, 2, 9-10
GospelLuke 13:31-35

There is a strange, almost stubborn courage running through today's readings. Paul is writing from prison and refusing to be diminished by it. Jesus is being warned that a king wants him dead and refuses to change his route. Neither man is pretending danger away. Both are walking straight into it, and the question both raise for the person sitting with coffee this morning is: what makes that kind of steadiness possible?

The first reading — Ephesians 6:10-20

We are near the very end of the letter to the Ephesians. Paul has spent five and a half chapters laying out what it means to live in Christ — how families and communities ought to work, how people who have been made new should carry themselves. Now he is closing, and his closing is not a gentle benediction. It is a call to arms.

The military imagery here is so familiar from years of hearing it read aloud that it can slide past without landing. But it is worth slowing down to notice what Paul is actually doing with it. He takes the standard equipment of a Roman soldier — the belt, the breastplate, the boots, the shield, the helmet, the sword — and reinterprets every piece. Truth belts you. Righteousness covers your chest. The Gospel of peace is what your boots are made for. Faith is the shield. Salvation is the helmet. The word of God is the one offensive weapon in the list, and it is not a blade for cutting people down but for cutting through confusion and despair.

What Paul is doing is imaginative and quite specific: he is telling people who have no power in the Roman world that they are not, in fact, unarmed. The "principalities and powers" he names are a shorthand in his letters for the forces — spiritual, structural, cultural — that bend human life toward darkness. He is not talking about individual bad actors; he is talking about the larger systems and spiritual pressures that distort things. His readers are mostly ordinary people, many of them slaves or freedmen, who feel the full weight of those systems. And Paul says: you are equipped for this fight, but the equipment is not what you think.

The psalm locks onto this directly. "Blessed be the Lord, my rock, who trains my hands for battle" — it is a warrior psalm, but the warrior's strength is acknowledged to be entirely borrowed. The singer is not self-made. Even the skill comes from somewhere beyond the self.

Then Paul adds something that stops the martial metaphor cold: he asks for prayer, not victory. He is in chains, he says, and he wants the boldness to keep speaking. That is what he needs. Not escape, not rescue in the conventional sense — just the courage to say what he must say.

The Gospel — Luke 13:31-35

Some Pharisees come to Jesus with a warning: Herod wants to kill you, get out. Whatever their motive — whether genuine concern or an attempt to redirect him away from Jerusalem — Jesus does not take the bait. He sends back a message for "that fox," which is a wonderful moment. It is not a compliment; a fox in that world meant a small, cunning, destructive animal, not a clever one. Jesus is not impressed by Herod's power.

His reply is almost rhythmic: today I cast out demons and heal, tomorrow I do the same, and on the third day I finish. The "third day" phrase here is not incidental — Luke's first readers would have heard the resonance immediately. Jesus is not saying he is safe from Herod; he is saying he knows exactly where this is going and he is going there anyway, because his work has a shape and a destination.

Then the lament over Jerusalem breaks open like something that has been held too long. "How many times I yearned to gather your children together as a hen gathers her brood under her wings." That image is worth sitting with — not a hawk, not an eagle, which would be more conventionally heroic, but a hen. A mother bird spreading herself over her chicks to cover them. It is a tender, almost helpless-looking gesture of protection. And the line that follows is devastating in its simplicity: but you were unwilling. The love was not the problem. The willingness was.

Steadiness that comes from knowing the destination

What joins the reading from Ephesians to this scene in Luke is not a shared theme so much as a shared posture: both Paul and Jesus are fully aware of the danger they are in, and neither changes course because of it. Paul's armor metaphor makes sense only if there is a real fight — he is not being metaphorical about difficulty. Jesus' lament makes sense only if the stakes are real — he is not performing courage.

The source of the steadiness, in both cases, is orientation. Paul knows what he is for: announcing the Gospel, even from a cell. Jesus knows where he is going: to Jerusalem, to the cross, to the thing he calls "accomplishing his purpose." The equipping Paul describes — truth, righteousness, faith, the word — is not a technique for avoiding suffering. It is what allows a person to remain themselves while passing through it.

To carry today

Think of some place in your life where the sensible move would be to go around something difficult rather than through it — a conversation you are avoiding, an obligation you are quietly rerouting. Ask yourself whether you have a clear enough sense of what you are for to let that clarity carry you forward rather than looking for the easier path.
