Wednesday · 11 November

Memorial of

Saint Martin of Tours, Bishop

One man turned back, and he was the one everyone had already written off.

First readingTitus 3:1-7
PsalmPsalm 23:1b-3a, 3bc-4, 5, 6
GospelLuke 17:11-19

Today the Church remembers Martin of Tours, the fourth-century soldier who cut his military cloak in half to cover a shivering beggar at the city gates — and that night dreamed of Christ wearing the half-cloak. He became a bishop, an evangelist of rural Gaul, and one of the most beloved saints of the Western church. The readings chosen for his memorial don't tell his story directly, but they illuminate the shape of a life like his: a man who had been remade by mercy, and who could not stop giving it away.

The first reading — Titus 3:1-7

The letter to Titus is one of the three "pastoral epistles," addressed not to a whole congregation but to a younger leader being coached in how to lead. Paul — or someone writing in Paul's name — has left Titus in Crete to organize the churches there, and what we hear today is advice about how Christians ought to carry themselves in public life: obedient to civic authority, peaceable, considerate, "exercising all graciousness toward everyone." It sounds at first like a kind of early Christian respectability code, the sort of thing designed to keep a minority community out of trouble with Roman authorities.

But then the passage pivots, and that pivot is everything. The reason Christians should be gracious toward everyone, it turns out, is that they themselves were once not gracious at all. Paul's list of what they used to be is not flattering: foolish, disobedient, deluded, enslaved to desires, living in malice, hateful and hating. He does not exempt himself — "we ourselves were once" — and there is something almost bracing about that candor. This is not a counsel to feel superior to the unkind people around you. It is a counsel to remember that you were them.

What changed was not moral improvement. It was the "kindness and generous love of God our savior" breaking in from outside — not earned, not merited, not a reward for righteous deeds, but sheer mercy. The Greek word behind "generous love" carries the warmth of a friend's affection; it is not cold beneficence but something closer to delight in the beloved. And this mercy arrived through baptism, described here as "the bath of rebirth and renewal by the Holy Spirit" — a rare, vivid image. The Spirit, Paul says, was poured out richly, not sparingly, like oil overflowing. The goal was not just forgiveness but inheritance: becoming heirs of a life that is still ahead.

Psalm 23 fits here almost perfectly, which is why the liturgy chose it. The shepherd who leads beside restful waters, who sets a table in the presence of enemies, whose goodness and kindness follow the psalmist all the days of his life — this is the same God Paul is describing. You were lost and now you are led. The table is spread not because you found your way there but because you were brought.

The Gospel — Luke 17:11-19

Ten lepers meet Jesus on the border of Samaria and Galilee — a geographic detail Luke uses deliberately, because the tension between Jews and Samaritans runs underneath everything that follows. They keep their distance, as the law required of people with this disease, and they cry out for mercy. Jesus sends them to the priests, which is the official procedure for certifying that a leper has been healed and can re-enter society. They go in faith, before they are healed, and "as they were going they were cleansed." The healing happens on the road, in obedience, before they see the evidence.

Nine of them keep going. One turns back. And Luke makes sure we know: he was a Samaritan, the one person in the group who, even healed, could not enter a Jewish Temple or be certified by a Jewish priest. He had the most reason to keep walking toward that official validation, and the least reason to expect it. Instead he returns, falls at Jesus's feet, and gives thanks loudly enough for everyone to hear.

Jesus's question is not quite a complaint — it carries something closer to wonder. "Ten were cleansed, were they not? Where are the other nine?" Nine healed people are somewhere ahead on the road, presumably doing exactly what they were told. They are not villains. But they received a gift and moved on, without pausing to register where it came from. The Samaritan, the outsider, the one who had nowhere to go with his official clean bill of health, was the one who turned around. And Jesus tells him something the others did not hear: "Your faith has saved you." The cleansing was physical; the salvation was something more.

What mercy does to its recipients

Here is where the first reading and the Gospel meet. Paul's argument is that Christians should be peaceable and gracious because they know themselves to have been radically undeserving recipients of a gift they did not earn. The Samaritan demonstrates exactly this in action: he is a man who knows he has been given something extraordinary, and that knowledge sends him back, prostrate, grateful. The nine who keep walking have received healing but have not yet been transformed by it. The one who returns has been.

Martin of Tours understood this. The soldier who gave half his cloak away was not performing philanthropy; he was responding to the logic of his own baptism. He had been given everything. The beggar at the gate was simply the next person in line to receive some of it.

To carry today

Think of a gift you have received that you never quite went back to thank anyone for — not necessarily a person, but a grace: a recovery, a reconciliation, a second chance you did not deserve. What would it look like to turn around and acknowledge it today, even now, even late?
