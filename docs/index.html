Twenty-eighth Week in Ordinary Time

The Spirit produces fruit; the law, on its own, produces only burdens.

First readingGalatians 5:18-25
PsalmPsalm 1:1-2, 3, 4, 6
GospelLuke 11:42-46

There is a particular kind of religious fatigue that comes not from too little faith but from too much performance — the exhausting work of maintaining an appearance of righteousness while the interior life quietly dries up. Today Paul and Jesus are addressing exactly that, from different angles, and between them they give a fairly complete picture of what goes wrong and what the alternative looks like.

The first reading — Galatians 5:18-25

Paul's letter to the Galatians is his most urgent and, at moments, his most exasperated. The community he had founded was being told by other teachers that following Jesus also required following the full Mosaic law — circumcision, dietary rules, the whole framework. Paul is beside himself, because he sees this as a fundamental misunderstanding of what Christ has done. This passage comes near the end of the letter, where Paul has moved from argument to practical description: what does life in the Spirit actually look like, versus life lived by striving to keep a legal code?

His list of "works of the flesh" is worth pausing over, because it is not simply a catalogue of personal vices. The word translated as "flesh" here does not mean the body in itself — Paul is not a Gnostic who thinks matter is evil. "Flesh" for him means the self turned in on itself, operating by its own strategies, without reference to God. And notice what turns up in that list alongside the more obviously physical sins: hatreds, rivalry, jealousy, dissensions, factions. These are not sins of appetite; they are sins of ego and tribalism — the kind that flourish just as easily in religious communities as anywhere else. A person can be scrupulously observant and absolutely riddled with factionalism and envy.

The fruit of the Spirit is something else entirely. Paul calls it fruit rather than "works," and that choice is deliberate. You do not manufacture fruit by effort; fruit grows from a living thing that is rooted in something life-giving. Love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, generosity, faithfulness, gentleness, self-control — these are not achievements you can perform your way into. They are the natural result of a life that is genuinely connected to the source. "Against such there is no law," Paul says, and there is almost a shrug in it, as if to say: you cannot legislate these into existence, and you don't need to forbid them either.

Psalm 1 fits here as snugly as a hand in a glove. It opens the whole Psalter with exactly this image: the person who is rooted in God's word is like a tree planted near running water — not striving, not straining, but drawing life from its source and bearing fruit in season. The wicked, by contrast, are like chaff: light, rootless, blown about by circumstance. The psalm's vision of flourishing is organic, not mechanical.

The Gospel — Luke 11:42-46

This is part of a longer "woe" speech that Jesus delivers to the Pharisees and legal scholars at a dinner — which is itself remarkable, since he has just been their guest, and this is how he responds to their hospitality. Luke does not soften the awkwardness. Jesus is not giving a seminar; he is confronting people he clearly thinks are doing serious harm.

The specific charge against the Pharisees is not that they are lawbreakers. They are obsessive rule-keepers — tithing down to the garden herbs, which is actually a level of scrupulosity the law does not require. The problem is inversion of priority: they have let the detailed observance crowd out "judgment and love for God." Jesus, pointedly, does not say the tithing is wrong. "These you should have done, without overlooking the others." The external practice is not the enemy; the substitution of external practice for the interior thing it was meant to serve is the problem.

The image he uses is quietly devastating: they are like unmarked graves that people walk over without knowing it. In Jewish purity law, contact with a grave made a person ritually unclean. Jesus is saying that these leaders, who present themselves as guardians of purity, are actually the source of pollution — but invisible, so no one knows to avoid them. It is a harsh image, and it is worth sitting with how harsh it is, because it clarifies that Jesus is not annoyed with the Pharisees. He is alarmed by what they are doing to the people in their care.

When a legal scholar objects that Jesus is insulting them too, the reply is brisk: the scholars load people with burdens they themselves do not help carry. Leadership that adds weight without offering strength is a specific kind of failure, and Jesus has a very short patience for it.

Fruit versus performance

These two readings are not on separate tracks — they are running the same direction. Paul is giving the theological account of what Jesus is diagnosing in practice. When the Spirit is not the animating principle, something else fills the space: ego, competition, the management of appearances, the relief of having performed correctly. And that something else, whatever face it wears, does not produce love, joy, peace, or patience. It produces factions, burdens, and unmarked graves.

The fruit of the Spirit is not a reward for better religious effort. It is what grows when the self is genuinely oriented toward God rather than toward its own performance.

To carry today

Consider whether any of the religious weight you carry — obligations, standards, habits of self-judgment — is actually feeding your interior life or has gradually become a substitute for it. The question is not whether the practice is good, but whether it is rooted in something living. Ask for the grace to know the difference.
