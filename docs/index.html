Memorial of

Saint Ignatius of Antioch, Bishop and Martyr

Ignatius walked toward Rome and death with his eyes wide open, calling it his joy.

First readingEphesians 1:15-23
PsalmPsalm 8:2-3, 4-5, 6-7
GospelLuke 12:8-12

Ignatius of Antioch was a bishop in Syria near the end of the first century, and when Rome finally came for him he was brought across the empire in chains to be killed in the arena. He wrote letters the whole way. They survive. What is striking — startling, really — is how little he sounds like a man being dragged to his death, and how much he sounds like someone trying to explain why he cannot wait to arrive. Today's readings sit alongside that fact and quietly illuminate it from both sides.

The first reading — Ephesians 1:15-23

We are in the opening chapter of Ephesians, in what amounts to an extended prayer that Paul offers on behalf of the community there. He has been giving thanks for their faith and their love, and now he makes his actual request of God: that they would receive a spirit of wisdom and revelation so that they might really know him — not just know about him. The phrase that follows is the one that earns attention: "may the eyes of your hearts be enlightened." The heart, in Paul's world, is not the seat of emotion in the way we mean it — it is more like the center of a person, the place where understanding and will and desire all live together. Eyes of the heart, then, would be the capacity to see what is real from that deepest part of yourself. Paul prays that those eyes would be opened to three things in particular: the hope of God's call, the inheritance awaiting the holy ones, and the surpassing greatness of God's power at work in those who believe.

That power, he says, is not an abstraction. It is precisely the power that raised Christ from the dead and seated him above every other authority or dominion that can be named, in this age or any age to come. Paul reaches for superlatives and then keeps going, as though language is struggling to contain what he is trying to say. The Christ who was executed is now head over all things — and not just over a general, cosmic all-things, but specifically over the Church, which Paul calls his Body, "the fullness of the one who fills all things in every way." It is a dense sentence and it is meant to be. The community of believers is not merely an organization Christ left behind; it is the continuing presence of the one who fills everything.

Psalm 8, which is a psalm of wonder at the scale of creation and the strange dignity of the human creature within it, answers this directly. "What is man that you should be mindful of him?" asks the psalmist, looking at the moon and stars. The refrain today reframes the question through the lens of Easter: "You have given your Son rule over the works of your hands." The ancient poem about humanity's dignity has become, in the light of the resurrection, a poem about Christ — and through him, about what humanity is being lifted into.

The Gospel — Luke 12:8-12

Jesus is speaking to his disciples about acknowledgment and denial. The stakes are named plainly: those who acknowledge him before others he will acknowledge before the angels of God, and those who deny him will be denied. This is a passage the early church lived with very literally — persecution was not a metaphor, and the choice to confess or deny Christ before a magistrate was a real choice people faced. Ignatius faced it and walked straight toward acknowledgment.

Then Jesus says something that has troubled readers for centuries: every sin against the Son of Man can be forgiven, but blasphemy against the Holy Spirit will not be. The best way to understand this is through what follows immediately — he tells the disciples that when they are brought before authorities, the Holy Spirit will teach them what to say. The unforgivable sin is not some obscure verbal transgression; it is the permanent, hardened refusal of the Spirit's witness, closing the very channel through which forgiveness comes. It is not a slip. It is a settled, deliberate shutting of the door from the inside. The passage is stern, but its pastoral point is almost the opposite of threatening: stop worrying about what to say. The Spirit will be there. Trust that.

The witness that requires no preparation

What holds the readings together is the question of what happens when a person's inner sight is actually opened to who Christ is. Paul prays for enlightened eyes of the heart. Jesus promises a Spirit who will supply the words. Ignatius is the man in whom both things appear to have actually happened. He did not need to prepare his defense because he was not defending a position — he was bearing witness to a person he knew, whose resurrection he had staked everything on. The hope Paul prays that the Ephesians would see clearly is the same hope that made Ignatius's chains feel, in his own words, like spiritual jewels.

These two texts are not on completely separate tracks, even though they come from different parts of the lectionary cycle. The reading from Ephesians and the Gospel are, in fact, two halves of the same promise: God opens the eyes, and God supplies the words. The saint whose memorial we keep today is what that promise looks like when it is actually believed.

To carry today

Think of something you know to be true about your faith but find hard to say out loud — in conversation, in the company of people who don't share it, or even to yourself without embarrassment. Ask for the grace that Paul describes: not just information, but genuine sight. The eyes of the heart can be opened. That is the prayer for today, and it is worth making it your own.
