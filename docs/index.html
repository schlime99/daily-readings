Twenty-ninth Sunday in Ordinary Time

God works through whoever he chooses, whether they know his name or not.

First readingIsaiah 45:1, 4-6
PsalmPsalm 96:1, 3, 4-5, 7-8, 9-10
Second reading1 Thessalonians 1:1-5b
GospelMatthew 22:15-21

A Persian king who never worshipped the God of Israel turns out to be an instrument of that God's purposes. A group of opponents come to Jesus with flattery in their mouths and a trap in their hands, and he walks out of it carrying the whole weight of the question with him. What holds these two moments together is a single conviction: God is not boxed in by the categories humans use to manage him.

The first reading — Isaiah 45:1, 4–6

This passage lands near the center of what scholars call the second part of Isaiah — chapters 40 through 55 — written to a people in exile in Babylon. The city of Jerusalem has fallen. The temple is rubble. The community that received God's promises has been scattered, and the questions hanging in the air are brutal: Where is God in this? Did he lose? Does he care?

The answer that comes is not what anyone expected. God announces that he has anointed Cyrus — the Persian king, the conqueror of Babylon — as his instrument of rescue. The word anointed here is the same word used for Israel's kings, and even, in its Greek form, for the Messiah. The author applies it without apology to a pagan emperor who does not know the name of the God who is speaking.

And that is the point. Isaiah wants the exiles to understand something that their suffering has obscured: God is not a tribal deity whose power ends at the borders of Judah. He is the Lord of all nations, which means he can work through all nations. Cyrus does not need to know he is being used. The LORD's purposes do not depend on human awareness of them.

The phrase that circles back twice — "though you knew me not" — is worth sitting with. It is not a reproach to Cyrus; it is a statement about God's freedom. He does not require prior acknowledgment. He acts first, and the recognition comes afterward, if it comes at all.

Psalm 96, which answers this reading directly, stretches the logic even further. It calls every nation to give the Lord glory, not because they already know him but because the news is going out. "Tell his glory among the nations," it says. The mission implied in the first reading becomes, in the psalm, an explicit summons.

The second reading — 1 Thessalonians 1:1–5b

This short passage is the opening of what is almost certainly the earliest piece of Christian writing we have — Paul's first letter to the Thessalonians, written perhaps twenty years after the resurrection, well before any of the Gospels. What is striking here is not any particular doctrine but the warmth of it, and the confidence. Paul writes to people he knows and loves, and he is certain about them: they were chosen, and the proof is what happened when the gospel arrived among them — not just words, but something with force and life behind it. He calls it power, the Holy Spirit, and conviction.

This section largely runs on its own track, separate from the Isaiah and Matthew readings. But it does carry one quiet resonance with the day: the gospel went to Thessalonica, a Gentile city, which means it crossed exactly the kind of border that Isaiah's oracle threw open. The God who called Cyrus without his knowing is the same God who sent Paul to people outside the covenant and made them, too, his own.

The Gospel — Matthew 22:15–21

The scene Matthew describes is a remarkable piece of political theater. The Pharisees and the Herodians make an odd pair — one group was defined by religious observance and deep unease with Roman rule; the other had made their peace with Rome and profited from it. They disagreed about almost everything, but they agreed that Jesus was a problem, and so they came together.

The question they bring — whether it is lawful to pay the census tax to Caesar — is a genuine trap. If Jesus says yes, he loses the crowd, who hated the tax as both a financial burden and a symbol of occupation. If he says no, he gives the Herodians grounds to report him to the Roman authorities as a revolutionary. There is no safe answer.

Jesus does not attempt a safe answer. He asks for the coin, and when they produce it, he has already made his point: they are carrying Caesar's currency. The coin bears Caesar's image and inscription, which means, in the logic of the ancient world, it belongs to Caesar's domain. Give it back to him if you must. But then there is the second half, which gets all the weight: give to God what belongs to God.

Jesus does not specify what belongs to God, and that silence is everything. His listeners knew their scriptures. They knew that human beings are made in the image and likeness of God — that the divine image is stamped not on a coin but on a person. If Caesar's image on a denarius means the coin belongs to Caesar, what does God's image on every human being mean? The question answers itself, and the answer is total.

The image that unlocks both readings

What Isaiah says about Cyrus and what Jesus says about the coin move in opposite directions but meet at the same place. Isaiah is telling the exiles that God's reach is wider than they imagined — he can claim what they assumed was outside his territory. Jesus is telling his questioners that their imagined escape hatch — the careful division between religious and civic life — is narrower than they thought. God is not confined to one corner of existence and Caesar to another. The image of God goes all the way down.

To carry today

Think today about the areas of your life you tend to keep in a separate column — the parts that feel too ordinary, too financial, too political, or too tangled to bring to prayer. Ask whether that compartment is really as sealed as it seems, and what it might mean that the God who armed a Persian king without his knowing is also present there, already working.
