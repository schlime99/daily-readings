Feast of

Saints Simon and Jude, Apostles

Jesus spent the night in prayer before he chose them — these two ordinary men among the Twelve.

First readingEphesians 2:19-22
PsalmPsalm 19:2-5
GospelLuke 6:12-16

Today the Church celebrates two apostles we know almost nothing about. Simon, called the Zealot, and Jude, son of James — they are names in a list, and that is essentially all we have. No letters, no famous conversions, no dramatic scenes in the Gospels. And yet the Church gives them a feast day, red vestments, and the full weight of apostolic dignity. There is something quietly encouraging in that.

The first reading — Ephesians 2:19-22

Paul's letter to the Ephesians is written to a community that was largely Gentile — people who had no claim on Israel's story, no ancestry in the covenant, no place at the table. The passage just before today's excerpt describes them as "far off," strangers to the promises. What Paul is doing in these verses, then, is announcing a reversal of that status. You are no longer strangers, he says. You are fellow citizens. You are household members.

The image he builds from there is architectural, and it is worth dwelling on. The community — the Church — is described as a building under construction. The foundation is the apostles and prophets. The capstone, the stone that locks an arch into place and holds the whole weight of the structure, is Christ himself. Through him, Paul says, "the whole structure is held together and grows into a temple sacred in the Lord."

That word grows is doing a lot of work. A temple growing is a strange thing — stone buildings do not grow. Paul is mixing his metaphors deliberately: this is a building, yes, but it is also a living thing, organic, still becoming what it is meant to be. The community of believers is both constructed and alive, both founded on something permanent and still in process.

The psalm appointed for today comes from Psalm 19, and the refrain — "Their message goes out through all the earth" — is applied in the liturgy to the apostles themselves. The psalm is actually about the heavens, about the sky declaring the glory of God without words, its testimony going to the ends of the world. The Church turns that image toward the Twelve: these men, like the sky, carried a message that needed no single language, a testimony that traveled far beyond what any of them could have imagined. Simon and Jude, whoever they were and wherever they went, are included in that reach.

The Gospel — Luke 6:12-16

Luke's account of the choosing of the Twelve is brief — nine verses — and it begins with something easy to read past. Before Jesus names anyone, he goes up a mountain and spends the entire night in prayer. The calling of the apostles is not a spontaneous moment. It is preceded by a night of conversation with God about which names will be spoken when morning comes.

Then day arrives, and he calls his disciples together and chooses twelve from among them. Luke gives us the list, and it is a strikingly mixed group. There is a tax collector and a Zealot — Matthew and Simon respectively — which means a man who collaborated with the Roman occupation and a man whose movement sought to end it by force were both named on the same morning. There are pairs of brothers. There is, at the end, Judas Iscariot, "who became a traitor," which Luke states flatly, without commentary.

Simon the Zealot appears tenth in the list. Jude — called here "Judas the son of James" to distinguish him from Iscariot — appears eleventh. They are interior names, not the ones the narrative lingers on. Peter opens the list. Judas Iscariot closes it with that bleak notation. Simon and Jude are in the middle, chosen, present, part of this.

What the foundation looks like from the inside

Paul calls the apostles the foundation of a living temple, and Luke's account shows us what that foundation is actually made of: twelve men chosen after a night of prayer, most of them unknown to history, one of them a future traitor. This is not a council of the distinguished. This is a deliberate choice made by Jesus in full knowledge of what each man was and would become.

The tradition holds that Simon and Jude eventually carried the Gospel together, working as a pair beyond the boundaries of Judea. Whether or not every detail of that tradition holds up to historical scrutiny, the image is worth keeping: these two obscure men, names in a list, traveling together, part of the message that went out through all the earth.

Paul's architecture is built on that kind of stone — ordinary, specific people who said yes when their name was called. The structure that grew from them is still growing. We are being built into it, Paul says, present tense, right now.

To carry today

Think of someone whose contribution to your life or your community has gone largely unrecorded — someone faithful and present but not prominent, not celebrated. Consider what it would mean to genuinely honor that kind of faithfulness, in your own mind if nowhere else. The foundation stones are rarely the ones you can see from the outside.
