Memorial of

Saint Elizabeth of Hungary, Religious

Jesus calls a name and a door opens — in Laodicea, in Jericho, in the heart.

First readingRevelation 3:1-6, 14-22
PsalmPsalm 15:2-3a, 3bc-4ab, 5
GospelLuke 19:1-10

Today's readings are, on the surface, about two very different kinds of spiritual failure — the congregation that has gone numb, and the man who has gone crooked. But what moves through both passages is the same astonishing thing: the Lord is still knocking. He has not given up on the lukewarm church or the tax collector up in his tree, and that patience is not a small thing.

The first reading — Revelation 3:1-6, 14-22

We are in the letters to the seven churches, the opening section of Revelation. Each letter follows a pattern: Christ introduces himself with one of his titles, says "I know your works," names what is wrong or right, and then offers both a warning and a promise to the one who perseveres. Today we get two of the seven churches — Sardis and Laodicea — and they are perhaps the two most uncomfortable ones for ordinary, comfortable Christians to hear.

Sardis has a reputation. People say it is a thriving church. Christ says it is dead. The gap between reputation and reality is the charge, and it lands quietly but hard. "Be watchful and strengthen what is left, which is going to die" — there is still something to save, but it is barely breathing. The image of soiled garments here is worth pausing on: in this book, white garments represent the righteousness of the saints, their faithfulness washed clean. Some in Sardis have kept theirs clean. Most, apparently, have not — not through great sin necessarily, but through drift, through letting the fire go out.

Laodicea gets the most famous passage in the whole chapter: the rebuke of lukewarmness. Laodicea was a wealthy city, known for its banking, its wool trade, and an eye salve its physicians produced. When Christ tells the church there to buy "ointment to smear on your eyes so that you may see," the first hearers would have felt the sting of that — you are in the city known for healing blind eyes, and you cannot see your own condition. The church has absorbed the city's self-satisfaction. "I am rich and have no need of anything" — and yet, Christ says, you are wretched, pitiable, poor, blind, and naked. The very things the city boasted of, Laodicea's church lacks in the only currency that matters.

Then comes the turn, and it is extraordinary. The passage does not end with the spitting out or the exposure of nakedness. It ends with this: "Behold, I stand at the door and knock." The letter that began with the most damning diagnosis in the whole sequence ends with an invitation to dinner. Christ is on the outside of a church that thinks it has everything — and he is still there, still knocking, still hoping someone will hear and open the door.

Psalm 15 gives the portrait of the one who is genuinely alive in God: someone whose integrity is not a performance but a habit of soul, who does justice and holds no grudges and lends without expecting something back in return. It is not an impossible portrait, but it is a demanding one, and it functions today as a quiet question: how much of this is true of me right now?

The Gospel — Luke 19:1-10

Jericho sits in the Jordan Valley, well below sea level, a lush oasis town that served as a customs hub. Tax collectors there had real work to do — goods moving up and down the roads — and a chief tax collector like Zacchaeus would have supervised others and taken a cut from their already-inflated collections. He would have been prosperous, socially isolated, and very well aware of exactly what people thought of him.

He wants to see Jesus, and the detail that he cannot — because he is short and the crowd is thick — is oddly endearing. This powerful, wealthy man cannot get a sightline. So he does something undignified: he runs ahead and climbs a tree. A chief tax collector, a man of some status, hiking up a sycamore like a child to catch a glimpse of a traveling preacher. Whatever he expected to happen, it was not what happened. Jesus looked up, said his name, and invited himself to dinner.

The crowd grumbles, and that grumble is not trivial — it represents the genuine religious logic of the day. You are what you associate with. For Jesus to eat with Zacchaeus is to endorse what Zacchaeus is. Jesus does not argue with this logic; he simply overrides it. He uses the word must — "I must stay at your house" — which in Luke's Gospel always signals something more than preference. This is not a social call. This is what the Son of Man came to do.

Zacchaeus's response is remarkable in its specificity. He does not say he will try harder or feel differently. He names what he will do: half his goods to the poor, fourfold repayment to anyone he has defrauded. He brings the precision of a tax collector to his own conversion.

Seeing clearly enough to open the door

What connects these two passages is vision — or the lack of it. The churches in Sardis and Laodicea cannot see what they actually are. Zacchaeus is a man who, despite everything, still wants to see. That wanting is not nothing. He has not closed down. And the moment he catches sight of Jesus — or rather, the moment Jesus catches sight of him — everything shifts.

Laodicea's Christ stands at the door and knocks. Zacchaeus opens his door and receives Jesus with joy. The same invitation, the same meal, the same word of life on the other side — and it costs only the willingness to come down from wherever we have climbed to protect ourselves, and to let someone in.

Saint Elizabeth of Hungary, whose memorial we keep today, was a woman who understood that faith without concrete action on behalf of the poor is precisely the kind of reputation-without-reality that Sardis was warned against. She gave away her wealth, served the sick with her own hands, and knew that salvation is meant to come to our house as well — and then go from it.

To carry today

Somewhere in your life right now there is probably something you have let go cool — a habit of prayer, a relationship, an honesty with yourself about how you are actually living. Ask for the clarity to name it, and then ask for the courage to open the door when the knock comes. That is all Zacchaeus did, and it was enough.
