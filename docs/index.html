Thirty-First Week in Ordinary Time

The dishonest steward acts fast when the reckoning comes — and Jesus calls that worth noticing.

First readingPhilippians 3:17–4:1
PsalmPsalm 122:1-2, 3-4ab, 4cd-5
GospelLuke 16:1-8

This is one of those Fridays where the two readings are largely running on separate tracks, and that is worth saying honestly. Paul is writing from prison about the shape of a life oriented toward heaven. Luke gives us one of the strangest parables in the Gospels — a rogue who gets praised for his roguery. Neither one softens easily into the other, but each one has something urgent to say on its own terms.

The first reading — Philippians 3:17–4:1

Philippians is a letter written from captivity, almost certainly from Rome, and it has an unusual warmth and intimacy throughout. Paul is not writing to correct a heresy or settle a dispute the way he does in Galatians or 1 Corinthians. He is writing to people he loves, and the affection is not performance — by the time he reaches the end of this passage, he has called the Philippians his "joy and crown," which is about as tender as Paul ever gets.

The passage comes near the end of a long section in which Paul has been sorting out what actually matters in a life. He has just finished cataloguing his own impressive religious credentials — circumcision, tribe of Benjamin, Pharisee, blameless by the law — and then declaring all of it garbage compared to knowing Christ. That is the buildup. Now, in chapter 3, he turns to those whose lives are ordered around the wrong things.

He describes them in striking terms. Their God is their stomach. Their glory is in their shame. Their minds are set on earthly things. These phrases are compact, almost brutal, and what they describe is not simply gluttony or immorality but something more structural: a life organized around appetite and comfort and the approval that comes from impressing people on the world's own terms. The word Paul uses for "shame" is pointed — he may be referring to something the people in question were actually boasting about, something that looked like status to them and looked like degradation to him.

Against this he sets the idea of citizenship. "Our citizenship is in heaven," he writes, and this would have landed differently in Philippi than it does for us. Philippi was a Roman colony, and its citizens had real, valued, legally protected status as Romans even though they lived far from Rome. Paul takes that civic pride and turns it. Yes, you have one citizenship that confers belonging and shapes how you live — but there is another one that matters more, and it is oriented not toward Rome but toward the Lord who is coming to transform even these lowly bodies. The psalm for today, a pilgrim song rejoicing to enter Jerusalem, echoes this same longing: there is a city we are made to go toward, and arrival in it is worth rejoicing over.

The closing line of the reading is almost a sigh: "Stand firm in the Lord, beloved." After everything — the warnings, the tears, the contrast between destruction and glory — Paul lands on that word. Beloved.

The Gospel — Luke 16:1-8

Here is where things get genuinely strange, and it is worth staying in that strangeness rather than rushing past it. A manager is caught mishandling his employer's assets and told to prepare for his dismissal. Before he is let go, he calls in several of the employer's debtors and reduces their bills — cutting one debt by half, another by twenty percent. He is essentially writing off money that is not his to write off. And then the master, when he hears what has happened, commends the man for acting prudently.

Readers have been unsettled by this for a long time, and understandably so. Jesus appears to be praising a cheat. Various explanations have been offered over the centuries — that the steward was simply removing his own commission from the debts, so he wasn't actually stealing, or that the master's commendation is worldly wisdom being described rather than endorsed. But Luke himself seems to want us to sit in the discomfort, because the punchline Jesus draws is not "wasn't that clever" but something more pointed: the children of this world, he says, are more shrewd in dealing with their own situation than the children of light.

The steward faced a crisis and he moved. He read the situation correctly — the reckoning was coming, there was no time to waste, and he acted decisively in the window he had left. He used what remained of his authority to build relationships that would sustain him after the crisis arrived. Jesus looks at that and says: children of light, take note. Not of the dishonesty, but of the urgency. Not of the method, but of the seriousness with which the man treated his situation.

What the shrewd steward and the heavenly citizen share

Paul and Jesus are both pressing on the same question from different angles: how seriously do you take the fact that the present arrangement is temporary? The steward knew his time was short and acted accordingly. Paul's people in Philippi are being asked to live as citizens of a city they cannot see yet, organizing their daily choices around a belonging that has not yet been fully revealed. Both readings assume that where you think you are going shapes how you act right now — and both suggest that most people, most of the time, live as though there is no reckoning coming, no transformation on the way, no city worth hurrying toward.

To carry today

Think about one area of your life where you are drifting — something you keep meaning to address, a relationship you have been slow to tend, a habit you have not taken seriously because there seems to be plenty of time. Ask honestly whether you are treating your situation with the shrewdness the steward showed, or whether you are waiting for a urgency that you half-hope will never arrive.
