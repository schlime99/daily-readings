Memorial of

Saint Leo the Great, Pope and Doctor of the Church

Duty done without scorekeeping is the shape of a life given over to God.

First readingTitus 2:1-8, 11-14
PsalmPsalm 37:3-4, 18, 23, 27, 29
GospelLuke 17:7-10

There is a strange freedom hidden in today's Gospel, though it doesn't look like freedom at first glance. A servant comes in from a full day's work in the field — plowing, tending sheep, earning his keep — and his master doesn't say thank you or pull out a chair. He says put on your apron and serve supper. And Jesus holds this up as the model. The disciples who have done everything asked of them should say: we are unprofitable servants. We have done only what we were obliged to do. This sounds hard, almost harsh, until you realize what it cuts away.

The first reading — Titus 2:1-8, 11-14

The letter to Titus is one of the pastoral epistles — letters written in Paul's name, addressed not to whole congregations but to younger leaders charged with organizing the life of a church. Titus has been left on the island of Crete to put things in order, and the letter is essentially a manual for that work. Today's passage is the household code section, where Paul works through the community almost by demographic: older men, older women, younger women, younger men, and finally Titus himself as the model.

Modern readers sometimes get snagged on specific phrases here — "under the control of their husbands," for instance — and it is worth acknowledging that the letter is writing within the social assumptions of a first-century Roman household rather than issuing timeless commands about marriage structure. What Paul is after is something that runs underneath all the particular instructions: the community's behavior is itself a kind of argument. If the people of this new faith are dignified, temperate, self-controlled, and good to one another, the word of God is not discredited. If they are chaotic, dissolute, or petty, they hand their opponents a weapon. The whole thing is oddly practical and genuinely demanding — not a list of spiritual heights to aspire to, but the texture of ordinary daily life brought under grace.

Then the passage opens out into something beautiful. After all the household management, Paul plants this: "the grace of God has appeared, saving all." The same grace that trains us toward temperance and goodness is the grace of the appearing of Christ himself, the one who gave himself to cleanse a people eager to do what is good. The shape of Christian life — dignified, controlled, honest — is not achieved by gritting teeth. It is a response to something that has already happened. We live this way because we have been claimed; we await the blessed hope because the great hope has already arrived once.

Psalm 37 picks this up almost verse by verse. Trust in the Lord and do good; take delight in the Lord. The psalm is a long meditation on the apparent unfairness of life, on the wicked who seem to prosper and the just who seem to get the short end. Its answer is not a quick fix but a posture: orient yourself toward the Lord, do what is good, and your steps will be made firm. The refrain the liturgy gives us — the salvation of the just comes from the Lord — is the psalm's whole argument in one line.

The Gospel — Luke 17:7-10

Luke places this tiny parable in the middle of a cluster of hard sayings. Jesus has just spoken about scandal, about forgiveness, and about faith the size of a mustard seed. Now this. The parable is deliberately mundane: a servant comes in from the fields, and no one expects the master to treat this as extraordinary. The servant does not get praised for the plowing. He does not get a banquet thrown for the sheep-tending. He finishes one task and moves to the next.

Jesus is not recommending that we treat other people as though their work doesn't count. He is describing something about our posture before God. The disciples have been given an astonishing gift — they have been called, taught, sent — and there is a temptation, as old as religion itself, to begin keeping score. To tally up what has been done and expect a corresponding return in divine gratitude or reward or recognition. The parable cuts that accounting off at the root. Everything we do in service of the Gospel is a response to grace already given. There is no surplus on our side of the ledger; there is only the ongoing astonishment that we were called at all.

The word translated "unprofitable" in some versions carries the sense of "unworthy" or "ordinary" — not that the servant's work is useless, but that it doesn't generate a debt on the master's side. We have done what we were obliged to do. This is not a counsel of despair or self-contempt. It is a release from the exhausting project of earning our place.

Where Titus and the Gospel converge: the life that doesn't keep score

These two readings are sitting on separate tracks in one sense — a letter about community order and a parable about servants — but they point toward the same interior disposition. Titus says: live well because grace has already appeared, not in order to make grace appear. Luke says: serve faithfully without expecting the account to come out in your favor. Both are describing a life freed from the anxious arithmetic of spiritual performance. We are dignified, temperate, and good not because we are building a case for ourselves, but because we have already been claimed. We serve without demanding gratitude because we know what it means to receive a gift we could never have earned.

Today the Church honors Leo the Great, the fifth-century bishop of Rome who held the city together during the Hunnic invasions and shaped the Church's theology of Christ for centuries. What Leo had was exactly this: a clear sense of who he was in relation to God, which freed him to act with authority and without panic. A man who knows he is an unprofitable servant can still do extraordinary things, because he is not doing them to prove something.

To carry today

Where in your life have you been keeping a quiet tally — doing good things but waiting, more than you realize, for the acknowledgment or reward that hasn't come? Ask for the grace of a genuinely unscored day: one act of patience, one piece of honest work, offered without calculating what it ought to earn you in return.
