Commemoration of

All the Faithful Departed (All Souls' Day)

Jesus says he will lose nothing of what the Father has given him — not one soul.

First readingWisdom 3:1-9
PsalmPsalm 23:1-3a, 3b-4, 5, 6
Second readingRomans 5:5-11
GospelJohn 6:37-40

Today the Church pauses to pray for everyone who has gone ahead of us — not the canonized saints celebrated yesterday, but the ordinary faithful dead: grandparents, parents, friends, the ones we lit candles for and the ones we hardly knew how to grieve. All Souls' Day is one of the most human things the Church does, because it takes seriously both the reality of death and the stubbornness of hope.

The first reading — Wisdom 3:1-9

The Book of Wisdom was written in Greek, probably in Alexandria, sometime in the century before Christ, for Jews living in a pagan world where death seemed like the final word. The author is thinking of people who died young, or who suffered before they died, or whose lives looked from the outside like defeat. The foolish — a word in this book that means not the intellectually slow but the morally blind, those who cannot see past the surface of things — look at the righteous dead and conclude that they lost. They suffered. They're gone. What did their faith get them?

The author's answer is not a consolation prize. It is a counter-claim about what was actually happening in those lives. They were being tried, the text says, as gold in the furnace — and the image is not of punishment but of refinement, the removal of what is not gold so that what is gold can be seen. Suffering in this reading is not evidence of God's absence; it is, at least in some cases, evidence of God's particular attention. That is a hard thing to say, and the author knows it. He is not saying that all suffering is purifying or that God sends illness as a lesson. He is saying that when the just suffer, something is happening that the world cannot read correctly.

And then the vision opens outward: these souls will shine, will dart like sparks through stubble — a vivid image of small bright things moving fast through dry grass, unexpected and alive. They will judge nations. The Lord will be their King forever. What looked like ending was arrival.

Psalm 23 is the natural answer to this. The valley of the shadow of death — the dark valley in this translation — is not the last stop. The shepherd leads through it, and what follows is a table, an anointing, an overflowing cup, and dwelling in the house of the Lord. The psalm was almost certainly sung at burials in ancient Israel, and hearing it today for all our dead is exactly right.

The second reading — Romans 5:5-11

Paul is writing to Rome, and this passage is doing something specific in his argument: he is building a case for confidence. Hope, he says, does not disappoint. Not "probably won't" — does not. The grounds for this confidence is not wishful thinking but the logic of what has already happened. If God reconciled us to himself while we were still enemies — while we were actively not on his side — how much more will he complete that reconciliation now that we are his? The argument runs from the harder thing to the easier: if he did the greater, the lesser is guaranteed.

The phrase "the love of God has been poured out into our hearts through the Holy Spirit" is one of Paul's most beautiful, and it is worth sitting with. Not distributed, not rationed, not made available — poured out, as if from a vessel that does not stop tilting. This passage runs on its own track from the Wisdom reading; it is not about death directly but about the certainty of love, which is the ground on which everything else in today's liturgy stands.

The God who does not lose anyone

The Gospel is four verses from the sixth chapter of John, in the long discourse Jesus gives after the feeding of the five thousand. Jesus is speaking about his relationship to the Father, and specifically about what the Father has entrusted to him. "I will not reject anyone who comes to me," he says — and then, twice, like a repeated promise to make sure it lands: I shall raise him on the last day.

The word that matters here is lose. "I should not lose anything of what he gave me." Jesus speaks of the souls the Father has given him the way you speak of something precious that has been placed in your keeping. The Father has entrusted them, and the Son will not let them slip. That is the promise underneath all of today's readings — not that death doesn't happen, not that grief isn't real, but that no one who has been given to Christ falls out of his hands on the way.

The Wisdom reading shows us souls that looked lost but were not. The psalm walks a shepherd's path through the darkest valley. Paul insists that what God began he will finish. And Jesus, in John, says it plainly: everyone the Father gives him, he will raise. These four passages are not saying the same thing in the same way, but they are facing the same direction, toward the same answer to the same terrible question.

To carry today

Think today of one person you have lost — someone whose death left a gap that is still there. Bring them to Mass in your mind, or simply hold their name in prayer at some point in the day. The promise being made today is not vague or general; it has a face, the face you are thinking of right now. Ask for the grace to trust it.
