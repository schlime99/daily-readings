Memorial of

Saints John de Brébeuf and Isaac Jogues, Priests, and Companions, Martyrs

We were dead, and God brought us to life — not because we earned it, but because he is rich in mercy.

First readingEphesians 2:1-10
PsalmPsalm 100:1b-2, 3, 4ab, 4c-5
GospelLuke 12:13-21

Today the Church in the United States honors the North American Martyrs — the Jesuit priests and laymen who carried the Gospel into the forests of what is now upstate New York and Canada in the seventeenth century, and who were tortured and killed for it. The readings assigned for this memorial are not special martyrology texts; they are the weekday readings in sequence, which means they arrive with their own logic. And it turns out that logic is not so far off. A letter about grace and a parable about the illusion of security fit these men more than you might expect.

The first reading — Ephesians 2:1-10

Ephesians is one of the great prison letters — written from captivity, probably in Rome, by Paul or a close disciple in his name. By the time we reach chapter two, the letter has already opened with a dense, magnificent hymn of praise. Now Paul shifts his register and speaks directly to his Gentile audience about what their lives looked like before: they were, he says, dead in their transgressions. Not sick, not weakened — dead. This is not rhetorical excess; it is a carefully chosen word. A dead person contributes nothing to their own resurrection.

What follows is one of the most compressed accounts of the Gospel anywhere in Paul's letters. God, who is rich in mercy — and the wealth of that phrase is deliberate, stacked against the poverty of human capacity — raised us with Christ and seated us with him in the heavens. This language of being seated in the heavens is startling. Paul does not mean it as a future reward; he means that by baptism something has already changed in what we are. We are not merely forgiven; we are repositioned. We belong, now, to a different order.

Then comes the line people sometimes argue about, and almost always misread: grace, not works, so no one may boast. Paul's point is not that deeds are irrelevant. He has been at pains throughout his letters to insist on the moral life. The point is that no one earns their way into this mercy. The initiative is entirely God's. We were dead; God moved first. And then — beautifully — we are called his handiwork, created in Christ for good works that God prepared in advance. The works follow from the gift; they do not purchase it.

Psalm 100 answers this reading with an almost embarrassing cheerfulness: "Know that the LORD is God; he made us, his we are." The psalm's refrain — "The Lord made us, we belong to him" — is precisely the corollary of Paul's argument. If we are handiwork, then we belong to the one who made us. There is a kind of relief in that.

For the North American Martyrs, this passage is not abstract. Men like Brébeuf had given up everything a seventeenth-century French Jesuit could have expected — comfort, safety, reputation, eventual old age — and gone into a world that was genuinely dangerous. They did not do this because they had earned God's favor. They did it because they had received it, and receiving it had changed what they were able to do. The grace came first.

The Gospel — Luke 12:13-21

Luke 12 is a long, dense chapter full of warnings about anxiety, hypocrisy, and misplaced priority. Someone interrupts Jesus from the crowd with what sounds like a perfectly reasonable complaint: tell my brother to share the inheritance with me. Jesus refuses the role of arbitrator, but he takes the question seriously enough to tell a parable about it.

The rich man in the story is not evil in any lurid sense. He has had a good harvest — a genuinely good harvest — and he is doing the sensible thing: planning ahead, securing the future, making sure the surplus is not wasted. Every instinct he follows would be praised in a business school. He tears down his barns and builds larger ones, and then he settles back into the imagined future: rest, eat, drink, be merry. He has, in his own mind, purchased a long life of ease.

God's response is blunt to the point of being darkly funny. "You fool." Not you sinner, not you greedy man — you fool. The charge is one of simple miscalculation. The man has oriented his entire life around an asset he does not actually own. His life will be demanded of him that very night, and the grain will belong to someone else entirely. The word demanded in Greek carries the sense of a debt being called in — his life was never fully his to keep. He stored up treasure for himself, Jesus says, but he is not rich in what matters to God.

What grace and greed reveal about ownership

Paul and Jesus are approaching the same territory from different directions. Paul says: you were dead, God raised you, the gift is entirely his, and now you are his handiwork. Jesus says: the man who thought he owned his future found out he did not. Both passages are asking where we think we stand in relation to what we have. Do we understand that life itself is received, not earned and not secured? The rich fool and the dead-in-transgressions Gentile are both in the same position: they have mistaken their situation. The difference is that one is corrected by mercy and the other by mortality.

The martyrs, in a strange way, had resolved this question before it was put to them. John de Brébeuf spent years among the Huron people, learned their language, endured extraordinary hardship, and ultimately died in captivity. He could not have done it while still clutching the self he had come to protect. That is not heroism from a superior character; it is what happens when Paul's logic has actually sunk in. You know you are his handiwork. You know your life has already been given back to you once. The barn burns down more easily after that.

To carry today

Where are you building larger barns — not necessarily with money, but with plans, habits, or self-sufficiency that quietly assume you are in charge of your own future? Sit with the question of what you are actually trying to secure, and whether you could loosen your grip on it, even slightly, today.
