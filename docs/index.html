Thanksgiving Day

One man turns back to say thank you, and Jesus calls it faith.

First readingSirach 50:22-24
PsalmPsalm 145:2-11
Second reading1 Corinthians 1:3-9
GospelLuke 17:11-19

There is a small, almost embarrassing gap at the center of today's Gospel — nine men who were healed and simply kept walking, and one who stopped, turned around, and came back. That gap is not a moral lesson about manners. It is a portrait of something that happens quietly in almost every life, and the Church hands it to us today because it is Thanksgiving, and it is worth looking at honestly.

The first reading — Sirach 50:22-24

Sirach is one of the deuterocanonical books — meaning it appears in Catholic and Orthodox Bibles but not in most Protestant ones, so it may be less familiar than other wisdom literature. It was written in Hebrew around 180 BC by a Jerusalem teacher named Ben Sira, then translated into Greek by his grandson. The book is fundamentally a manual for living wisely and gratefully in covenant with God, deeply rooted in the Temple worship of its era.

This short passage comes at the end of a long, magnificent poem praising the high priest Simon, who had just finished presiding over a feast-day liturgy. The whole chapter is almost cinematic — incense rising, the priestly vestments catching the light, the congregation prostrate in worship. And then, at the close, Ben Sira steps back from the spectacle and addresses the reader directly: Bless the God of all, who has done wondrous things on earth. It is a pivot from describing worship to practicing it, from watching the high priest give thanks to giving thanks yourself, right now, in your own life. The request at the end — that God grant gladness of heart and peace in our time — is not wishful thinking. It is petition rooted in memory, the kind of prayer that is possible only because the speaker has already been paying attention to what God has done.

The psalm assigned for today, Psalm 145, runs on the same frequency. It is one of the great praise psalms, an acrostic in the original Hebrew where each verse begins with a successive letter of the alphabet — a full-alphabet act of praise, as if to say that every word that exists belongs to this. The refrain the liturgy draws from it ("I will praise your name forever, my king and my God") is not a resolution for the future; it is a present-tense declaration that keeps renewing itself line by line.

The second reading — 1 Corinthians 1:3-9

Paul's opening to the Corinthians runs on its own track, somewhat independent of the other readings, though it carries the same posture of gratitude. Paul is writing to a community he founded, one he loves and also worries about constantly — Corinth was a port city, cosmopolitan and chaotic, and the church there had real problems. And yet he opens by giving thanks for them, specifically for the grace they have been given, the gifts of speech and knowledge, their confirmation in Christ. It is striking that Paul's gratitude here is not abstract but pointed: he is grateful for these people, with their particular gifts, in their particular city. He closes with the assurance that "God is faithful" — a phrase that in Paul's letters always carries weight, because it anchors present difficulty in something that does not waver.

The Gospel — Luke 17:11-19

Ten men with leprosy call out to Jesus from a distance — they had to keep their distance, by the law — and he tells them to go show themselves to the priests, which was the prescribed ritual for certifying that someone had been healed. They go, and on the way they are cleansed. All ten obey. All ten are healed. One, when he sees what has happened, turns back. Luke makes a point of noting that he is a Samaritan — an outsider twice over, unclean by disease and by ethnicity. He throws himself at Jesus' feet, praising God at the top of his voice. And Jesus asks, with what sounds like genuine puzzlement, where the other nine are. Then he says something unexpected: Your faith has saved you. Not just healed you — saved you. The other nine received healing. This man received something more, and it was connected to the act of turning back.

What gratitude actually does

The nine are not villains. They did exactly what Jesus told them to do. But there is something in the Samaritan's return that the Gospel wants us to notice — not because it was polite, but because it was an act of recognition. He saw what had happened, he knew where it came from, and he went back. That going back is what Luke calls faith. Gratitude here is not a feeling; it is a movement. It is the act of locating yourself correctly in relation to what you have received and who gave it to you, and letting that location change how you stand.

Sirach says the same thing with different imagery: bless God, who has done wondrous things on earth. The wonder is already accomplished. The blessing — the thankfulness — is what orients us to it properly.

To carry today

Think of one specific thing — not in general, but particular and concrete — that you have received this year that you did not earn or arrange for yourself. Sit with it long enough to feel its weight, and then, in whatever way is natural to you, say so. That turning back is what the day is for.
