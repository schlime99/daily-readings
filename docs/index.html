Thirtieth Week in Ordinary Time

The Kingdom begins small, hidden, and changes everything it touches.

First readingEphesians 5:21-33
PsalmPsalm 128:1-2, 3, 4-5
GospelLuke 13:18-21

Two very different kinds of teaching land on the table this morning. Paul is deep in the architecture of Christian marriage, working out what it means that husband and wife are an image of Christ and the Church. Jesus, in Luke, is doing something closer to wonder — asking his listeners to look at ordinary bread rising and a garden shrub throwing out branches, and to see in those small things the whole kingdom of God. These two are largely on separate tracks today. But taken together, they share a quiet insistence that what is holy tends to hide in the domestic, the everyday, the small.

The first reading — Ephesians 5:21-33

This passage has a complicated life in the modern Church, and it is worth being honest about that before anything else. Many people hear the word "subordinate" and stop there, which means they miss the most astonishing claim in the text. Paul is not primarily a household-management theorist; he is a theologian trying to say something almost too large to say — that Christian marriage is a living icon of the relationship between Christ and his Church.

The passage actually opens before the famous lines about wives: "Be subordinate to one another out of reverence for Christ." That mutual subordination is the frame. What follows is not a one-sided power arrangement but an attempt to describe the shape of love that flows from that frame, addressed to each partner in turn. When Paul turns to husbands — which he does at much greater length — the word that keeps returning is not "authority" but "love." And the love he demands is extravagant: the same love by which Christ handed himself over, gave himself up, emptied himself for the sake of the Church. That is not a comfortable position for the husband. It is a call to self-expenditure.

The word Paul uses for the Church's relationship to Christ — and that he applies analogically to the wife — is the same word used for the Church's orientation toward its Lord: a posture of trust, of being received, of belonging. It is not the same as erasure. Paul ends the whole passage by saying that a man who loves his wife loves himself, because the two have become one flesh. He quotes Genesis: "a man shall leave his father and his mother and be joined to his wife." Then he calls this a "great mystery" — and the Greek behind that word is the one the Latin tradition rendered sacramentum. Marriage, in Paul's theology, is not merely a civil arrangement or even a religious ceremony. It participates in something that exceeds it.

Psalm 128 answers the reading directly, and warmly. It is a psalm of domestic blessing — the fruitful vine, the olive plants around the table, the house held together by the fear of the Lord. That phrase, "fear of the Lord," does not mean terror; it means the orientation of a life toward something larger than itself, the posture of a creature who knows it lives in the presence of God. The psalm sees the ordinary household meal and the children at the table and says: here. The sacred is here.

The Gospel — Luke 13:18-21

Luke gives us two of Jesus' shortest parables, placed side by side, both asking the same question: what is the kingdom of God like? The mustard seed planted in a garden grows into something large enough for birds to nest in. The yeast mixed into three measures of flour — a large batch, a feast-sized quantity — works through the whole until everything is leavened.

Both parables insist on hiddenness and disproportionate result. The mustard seed is famously tiny; you can barely see it in your palm. The yeast disappears into the dough and works invisibly. Neither looks like what it produces. And that, Jesus seems to be saying, is exactly the point. The kingdom of God does not announce itself with the obvious grandeur people were hoping for. It does not arrive the way empires arrive. It works from the inside, from the small, from the hidden — and when you look up, it has already changed everything.

The Alleluia verse before the Gospel (from Matthew 11) is a small gift: "Blessed are you, Father, Lord of heaven and earth; you have revealed to little ones the mysteries of the Kingdom." The mysteries go to the small, the unhurried, the ones not looking for spectacle.

Hiddenness and fruitfulness

What these two readings share, even running on separate tracks, is a conviction that the most important things are not the most visible ones. The yeast is hidden in the flour. The mustard seed is in the ground. And Paul's great mystery — that two people becoming one flesh are somehow imaging the love of Christ for his Church — is hidden inside what looks, from the outside, like an ordinary Tuesday morning at the kitchen table.

The sacred tends to hide in the ordinary, and our work is learning to recognize it.

To carry today

Think of something in your life that has been quietly growing — a relationship, a practice, a change of heart — that you have not fully noticed because it has been slow and small. Ask whether it might deserve more attention and more trust than you have been giving it. The kingdom works on exactly that scale.
