Thirty-fourth Week in Ordinary Time

The last words of Scripture and the last words of Jesus in Luke pull in the same direction: stay awake.

First readingRevelation 22:1-7
PsalmPsalm 95:1-2, 3-5, 6-7ab
GospelLuke 21:34-36

This is the last Saturday of the liturgical year. Tomorrow, the First Sunday of Advent, everything resets — a new year, a new beginning, purple vestments and the long lean expectation of winter. But today we are still at the end, and the Church gives us two texts that know exactly where they are standing: at the edge of everything, looking forward.

The first reading — Revelation 22:1-7

We are at the very close of the Book of Revelation, which means we are at the very close of the entire Christian Bible. The vision John describes here is not an ending so much as a return — a restoration of what was always meant to be, now made permanent and unassailable. The river of life runs through the middle of the city. The tree of life grows on both banks. If those images sound familiar, they should: this is Eden, rebuilt and opened, the garden that was closed in Genesis now flung wide at the end of all things.

The tree of life is worth pausing over. In Genesis it appears briefly and then is put out of reach. Here it bears fruit every single month — twelve harvests a year, abundance that does not exhaust itself — and even its leaves do healing work, medicine for nations. The vision is not of private bliss but of restoration on a cosmic scale: all peoples, all the damage done through history, met with a remedy that grows freely and never runs out.

Then there is the face. "They will look upon his face," John writes, and that is easy to read past. But in the Hebrew tradition, to see the face of God was understood to be impossible for a mortal — the glory would be too much. Moses could only glimpse God's back. Even the seraphim in Isaiah covered their faces. Here, in the new creation, the servants of God look upon his face without being destroyed. The barrier is gone. The name written on their foreheads is not a brand but a belonging — they are known, fully and permanently, by the one they see.

The refrain of Psalm 95 chosen for today is the ancient Aramaic prayer Marana tha — "Come, Lord Jesus." It is one of the oldest fragments of Christian prayer we have, old enough that Paul quotes it without translating it in his first letter to the Corinthians, trusting that his Greek-speaking audience will recognize it anyway. Pairing it with this vision from Revelation turns the psalm into something yearning: the God who made the sea and the mountains, who shepherds the flock — this is the one the community is calling toward them, pulling him near with the simplest of requests.

The Gospel — Luke 21:34-36

Luke's Jesus is blunter, and that is a kindness. He does not want his disciples surprised. He has been warning them about the destruction of Jerusalem, about false prophets, about cosmic signs, and now he closes that long teaching with something almost domestic in its specificity. Watch out, he says, for carousing and drunkenness. Watch out for the anxieties of daily life.

The anxieties of daily life. That phrase lands differently than the rest. Drunkenness is an obvious way to go numb; the disciples can file it under dramatic temptations and feel reasonably safe. But anxiety is something else entirely, and Jesus places it right alongside dissipation as a thing that can dull the heart to sleep. The word Luke uses carries the sense of being weighed down, burdened, so distracted by the press of ordinary worries — money, work, illness, relationships, the low-grade hum of things unresolved — that you miss the moment when it arrives. This is not a warning about sinners. It is a warning about everyone.

The alternative Jesus proposes is not calm. He says to be vigilant and to pray — and prayer here is explicitly for strength, for the capacity to stand. He is not promising his disciples that vigilance will be easy or that the tribulations will be minor. He is saying: the resources are available, but you have to ask for them, and asking requires staying awake long enough to notice you need them.

What one vision and one warning share

The two readings are not about the same moment, but they are oriented toward the same person. Revelation shows the destination: a city full of light, a river, a tree, and a face you can finally look at. Luke's Jesus is still in the middle of the journey, warning his friends not to sleepwalk through it. The word "soon" in Revelation — I am coming soon — is the bridge. John's angel uses it, and Jesus in Luke implies it. The question both texts press is the same one: given that something momentous is on its way, what does a person do with their waiting?

The answer is not frantic productivity or spiritual performance. It is a kind of active receiving — staying porous enough to notice, light enough on the inside to be found.

To carry today

On the last day of this liturgical year, consider what has been making your heart heavy lately — not the dramatic failures, but the ordinary weight, the low background worry that keeps you half-elsewhere. Ask for the grace of one moment of genuine attentiveness today: to a person in front of you, to a prayer said without distraction, to a glimpse of the good that is already here. That is what it means to stay awake.
