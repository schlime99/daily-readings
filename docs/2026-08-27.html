Memorial of

Saint Monica

Monica prayed for her son for decades, faithfully, without knowing how it would end.

First reading1 Corinthians 1:1-9
PsalmPsalm 145:2-3, 4-5, 6-7
GospelMatthew 24:42-51

Today the Church remembers Monica, a fourth-century woman from North Africa whose primary claim on our memory is that she refused to stop praying for her son. She prayed for him through his wandering years, his infidelities, his philosophical detours, his long resistance to a faith she held with quiet fierceness. She is not remembered for dramatic action but for patient faithfulness sustained across decades. The two readings today, which are on their own separate tracks from each other and from her feast, each light up a different facet of what that fidelity actually looks like.

The first reading — 1 Corinthians 1:1-9 — This is the very opening of Paul's first letter to the Corinthians, which means we are at the threshold, before any of the thorny problems get named. And Corinth has thorny problems — factions, scandals, disputes about spiritual gifts and idol meat and resurrection. Paul knows all this as he writes. What is striking is that he leads not with correction but with gratitude. He tells a community that is, frankly, a mess that he gives thanks always on their account, that they have been enriched in every way, that they are not lacking in any spiritual gift. He is not flattering them into compliance; he is seeing them truly — seeing what God has already placed in them, before getting to what they have done with it.

The sentence that anchors everything is near the end: God is faithful. The Greek word behind "faithful" carries the sense of trustworthiness, reliability, the quality of a person who does what they said they would do. Paul is not making a general theological point. He is saying something very specific: God called you into fellowship with his Son, and that call will hold. You will be kept firm to the end. The one doing the keeping is not you.

Psalm 145 answers this directly. It is a hymn of unbroken praise — "every day will I bless you," generation after generation handing the praise forward. The psalm's confidence is not cheerful optimism; it is grounded in what God has already done, in works that have already proved the claim.

The Gospel — Matthew 24:42-51 — This comes from Jesus's discourse on the last things, late in Matthew, when he has entered Jerusalem and the confrontation with the authorities is near. He has been speaking in sustained images about watchfulness, and this passage gives us two servants, each of whom must decide what the master's absence means. The first one keeps doing his job — distributing food at the proper time, managing the household — and is found at it when the master returns unexpectedly. The second one sees the delay as permission: the master is a long time coming, so let the constraints loosen, let the cruelty toward the other servants begin, let the drinking and the company-keeping with those the master would not approve begin. The second servant is not a villain from the start; he is a servant who let the master's absence become an excuse.

Jesus's word for the second servant is "hypocrite" — someone whose outward role and inward life have separated. He was still called a servant. He was still in the house. The problem is not that he stopped believing the master existed, but that he started living as though the master's return did not matter today.

What Monica and the faithful servant share — The connection between these two readings is not thematic in an obvious way — Paul is writing about grace, Jesus is warning about vigilance — but Monica's life sits exactly at the crossroads. She did not know when her son would be found. She could not have predicted that Augustine's conversion would come in a Milanese garden after years of resistance, or that he would become one of the most formidable minds the Church has ever produced. She simply kept doing what a faithful person does: she prayed, she wept, she stayed near a bishop who kept telling her the child of those tears could not be lost. That is the faithful servant's posture — not doing something spectacular while waiting, but continuing to do the good thing in front of her, convinced the master was coming even when the hour was unknown.

Paul's "God is faithful" is the theological ground of Monica's endurance. She was not sustained by seeing results. She was sustained by a conviction about the character of the one she was praying to. And the Gospel's warning about the wicked servant applies here too: the temptation in a long wait is always to let the waiting become a reason to stop. To decide the answer is not coming, and to rearrange your life around that conclusion.

Monica did not do that. The patience required was not passive; it took real work to stay open across years of silence and seeming failure. The feast is not sentimental. It is an honest picture of what fidelity actually costs.

To carry today — Think of the prayer you have been praying for a long time without visible result — the person you are interceding for, the situation that has not moved, the hope you have almost talked yourself out of. Ask honestly whether you are still in it, or whether you have quietly arranged yourself around the assumption that it will not be answered. The invitation is not to manufacture new energy, but to notice that the one you are praying to has not changed.
