Monday of the

Twenty-eighth Week in Ordinary Time

The outsiders repented; the insiders demanded more proof. Jesus notices.

First readingGalatians 4:22-24, 26-27, 31–5:1
PsalmPsalm 113:1b-2, 3-4, 5a, 6-7
GospelLuke 11:29-32

There is a particular kind of spiritual restlessness that dresses itself up as rigor — the endless need for another sign, another confirmation, another reason to finally believe. Jesus has seen it before, and in today's Gospel he names it with unusual bluntness. Meanwhile, Paul is finishing up a long, heated argument about what it actually means to be free. These two texts are on somewhat separate tracks, but each, in its own way, is asking: what are you actually waiting for?

The first reading — Galatians 4:22-24, 26-27, 31–5:1

By the time we reach chapter four of Galatians, Paul is writing with the urgency of someone who cannot quite believe he has to say this again. The Galatian Christians had received the gospel with joy, but certain teachers had since arrived and told them they needed to take on the full obligations of the Mosaic Law — circumcision first among them — to be truly part of God's people. Paul has been arguing against this for chapters, and here he reaches for one of his most ambitious moves: an allegory drawn from the story of Abraham's two sons.

Most readers know the basics. Abraham had a son, Ishmael, by Hagar, his wife Sarah's slave, when Sarah seemed unable to bear children. Then, miraculously, Sarah herself conceived and bore Isaac. Paul reads this as a picture of two different ways of relating to God. Hagar and her son represent the covenant of Sinai — the Law given to Moses — which Paul characterizes as a kind of bondage, not because the Law is bad, but because it cannot by itself bring the freedom it points toward. Sarah and Isaac represent the new covenant, the one that runs through promise rather than through human effort or legal obligation.

The audacity of this reading would have landed hard on Jewish ears, and Paul knew it. He is essentially saying that Gentile Christians, who have received the promise through faith, stand in the line of Isaac — while those who insist on the Law as the path to belonging stand in Hagar's line, even if they are physically descended from Abraham. This is not a reading you can arrive at by ordinary logic; it is a reading Paul arrives at by working backward from the resurrection, asking what the whole story must have been about all along.

The passage ends with a line so compressed it is easy to miss: "For freedom Christ set us free; so stand firm and do not submit again to the yoke of slavery." The freedom Paul is describing is not freedom from moral seriousness, but freedom from the anxious, contractual striving that turns religion into a performance of adequacy. You are already in. Do not climb back into the cage.

Psalm 113 fits here beautifully. It is a psalm of reversal — the Lord "raises up the lowly from the dust" and "lifts up the poor" from obscurity. The barren woman who suddenly has more children than the one with a husband, quoted in the first reading from Isaiah, is exactly this kind of reversal. God's economy keeps working backwards from what anyone would expect.

The Gospel — Luke 11:29-32

This is a hard saying, and Jesus delivers it to a swelling crowd, which makes it harder. People are pressing in around him, presumably hoping to see something remarkable, and he tells them flatly that no sign is coming — except the sign of Jonah.

Jonah is worth a moment. The book of Jonah is one of the stranger texts in the Hebrew Bible: a reluctant prophet who runs from his mission, gets swallowed by a great fish, survives, delivers the world's most minimal sermon to Nineveh ("Forty days and Nineveh will be overthrown"), and then sulks when the city repents. The Ninevites were not Israel. They were the enemy — Assyrians, the people who would eventually destroy the northern kingdom. They had no law, no covenant, no prepared ground. And yet at the briefest word from the most reluctant prophet in scripture, they repented in sackcloth and ashes.

Jesus sets them against his own generation like a mirror. The queen of the south — the Queen of Sheba, who traveled an enormous distance to hear Solomon's wisdom — would rise at judgment and condemn people who had Solomon himself standing in front of them and still wanted more. The Ninevites would condemn a generation that had Jonah's fulfillment right there in the flesh, and still held out.

The sign of Jonah, in Luke, seems to be Jonah himself — the preacher, the word, the call to turn around. There is no elaborate proof. There is a person speaking, and the question is whether you will hear him.

The willingness the outsiders had that the insiders lacked

What connects these two readings is the problem of prior commitment. The Galatians who wanted to return to the Law, and the crowd demanding a sign, are both in some sense protecting themselves from the thing that is right in front of them. The Ninevites had no investment in Jonah being wrong. The queen of the south had no reputation to protect. They could simply hear. The people Jesus is addressing have a framework, and the framework is sitting on top of the message.

Paul's word for the Galatians is: you are already free, stop auditioning for a slavery you have been released from. Jesus's word to the crowd is something similar: you already have what the Ninevites were given. The question is whether you will receive it.

To carry today

Think of something in your life where you are quietly waiting for more certainty before you act — in faith, in forgiveness, in a decision you already know the shape of. Ask yourself honestly whether more evidence would actually change anything, or whether the waiting has become its own kind of protection. What would it look like to simply respond to what is already in front of you?
