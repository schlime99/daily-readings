Twenty-seventh Sunday in Ordinary Time

God planted the vineyard with everything it needed; the question is what we have done with it.

First readingIsaiah 5:1-7
PsalmPsalm 80:9, 12, 13-14, 15-16, 19-20
Second readingPhilippians 4:6-9
GospelMatthew 21:33-43

The image of a vineyard runs from the first reading all the way through the Gospel today, which is unusual — the liturgy rarely hands us a thread this clean. Two vineyard songs, centuries apart, one the original and one a variation on it. It is worth sitting with them together, because Jesus is clearly counting on his audience to know Isaiah's version, and the shock of his parable depends on it.

The first reading — Isaiah 5:1-7

The Book of Isaiah opens in grand, prosecutorial fashion, and by chapter five the prophet is ready for the centerpiece of his early preaching. He introduces this passage with a small, strange move: he calls it the song of his friend, which shifts the register away from prophecy and into something more intimate before God himself takes over as speaker. The vineyard is set up in loving detail — a fertile hillside, cleared of stones, planted with the best vines, a watchtower, a wine press cut into rock. Every reasonable preparation has been made. And then, when harvest comes, the vine produces wild grapes. The Hebrew word for those grapes suggests something foul-smelling, bitter, inedible — not just a disappointment but a kind of insult.

God then turns to the people of Jerusalem and Judah and does something unexpected: he asks them to judge. What more could he have done? The question hangs there, and it is meant to. The people are being asked to render a verdict on themselves before they realize they are the vineyard. The punishment he announces — the hedge removed, the walls broken down, no pruning, no rain — is not so much a violent act as a withdrawal. He stops protecting it. He stops tending it. The vineyard becomes what it would always have been without him.

The last line of Isaiah is the interpretive key: "he looked for judgment, but see, bloodshed! for justice, but hark, the outcry!" In the Hebrew there is a wordplay — the words for judgment and bloodshed, for justice and outcry, sound startlingly similar. What God planted and cultivated was meant to yield justice; what grew instead was its ruinous opposite.

Psalm 80 answers this directly, picking up the vineyard image but shifting from accusation to lament. The same vine, the same metaphor — but now the people are crying out from inside the ruin, asking God to look again, to tend the vine once more, to restore them. It is one of scripture's most honest movements: from sentence to prayer, from verdict to petition. The psalm does not dispute that the vineyard failed. It just refuses to stop asking.

The second reading — Philippians 4:6-9

This passage runs on its own track, unconnected to the vineyard theme, and there is no point in pretending otherwise. Paul is writing from prison — the Letter to the Philippians carries that shadow throughout, though you would not always know it from the warmth of his tone. This closing section is among the most practically useful things he ever wrote: do not be anxious, but bring everything to God by prayer; and in the meantime, train your mind deliberately on whatever is true, honorable, just, pure, lovely, and worthy of praise. The peace that results, he says, surpasses all understanding — it is not the peace that comes from having things sorted out, but something that arrives and holds you even when nothing is sorted out. That distinction matters more than it might seem on a quiet Sunday morning.

The Gospel — Matthew 21:33-43

Jesus is in the Temple, in his final week, and the people he is speaking to are the chief priests and the elders. He has just told them the parable of the two sons; now he gives them another one, and the deliberate echo of Isaiah 5 is so close — vineyard, hedge, wine press, tower — that the audience would have caught it immediately. He is doing what Isaiah did: asking them to judge.

The parable escalates in ways that become almost surreal. The landowner sends servants to collect his share of the harvest, and the tenants beat them, kill them, stone them. He sends more. The same. Finally — and this is where the story tips into something strange — he sends his son, reasoning that the tenants will respect him. They do not. They see the son coming and decide that if they kill the heir, the inheritance will fall to them. Their logic is not irrational so much as it is catastrophically shortsighted: they have confused possession with ownership.

Jesus asks his listeners what the landowner will do. They answer their own question: he will destroy those tenants and lease the vineyard to others who will hand over the harvest. And then he quotes Psalm 118 — the stone the builders rejected has become the cornerstone — and tells them plainly that the kingdom will be given to people who produce its fruit.

Ownership mistaken for possession

What joins Isaiah's song and Jesus's parable is not just the image of the vineyard but the single error at the heart of both: the assumption that what has been given is now owned. Israel mistook covenant for entitlement. The tenants in the parable mistake stewardship for title. The vineyard was always someone else's; the fruit was always owed. Both stories are really about what happens when that fact is forgotten — not just forgotten abstractly, but forgotten in the practical, daily choices about what justice looks like and who deserves it.

Neither story is comfortable to read from the outside, much less from the inside. The question neither passage lets us avoid is the quiet one: what is the fruit we are expected to be producing, and what do we actually have to show?

To carry today

Think of something — a talent, a relationship, an opportunity, a stretch of good fortune — that you have quietly begun to treat as yours by right rather than by gift. What would it look like to hold it a little more loosely today, with some acknowledgment of where it came from and what it is actually for? That small shift in posture is closer to what both readings are asking for than any grand religious gesture.
