Twenty-fourth Week in Ordinary Time

A woman bathes the feet of Jesus with her tears, and he sends her away at peace.

First reading1 Corinthians 15:1-11
PsalmPsalm 118:1b-2, 16ab-17, 28
GospelLuke 7:36-50

Today's readings sit on separate tracks, as weekday Ordinary Time readings so often do — Paul is deep in an argument about the resurrection, while the Gospel has Jesus at a dinner party that goes unexpectedly sideways. But each text, on its own, is extraordinary. Let Paul have his say, then let Luke have his, and by the end something quietly rhymes between them.

The first reading — 1 Corinthians 15:1-11

This passage opens the fifteenth chapter of First Corinthians, which is Paul's long, sustained defense of the resurrection — one of the most carefully constructed arguments in the New Testament. By the time he gets here, the Corinthian community has apparently developed some doubts about whether the dead actually rise. Paul's response is to go back to the beginning, to what he himself received and passed on. The formula he quotes — Christ died for our sins, was buried, was raised on the third day, and appeared — is strikingly old, older than the letter itself. Most scholars believe Paul received it within a few years of the crucifixion, which would make it among the earliest Christian writing we have. The list of witnesses he offers is almost legal in its precision: Cephas, then the Twelve, then five hundred at once, then James, then all the apostles.

And then Paul stops and looks at himself honestly. "Last of all," he writes, "as to one born abnormally, he appeared to me." The phrase he uses — the one translated as "born abnormally" — carried a flavor of something untimely, even monstrous, as if Paul's apostleship were a strange and irregular birth. He does not argue against this. He says: yes, I persecuted the Church. I am the least. And then he says something worth sitting with: "By the grace of God I am what I am, and his grace to me has not been ineffective." He is not minimizing his past. He is being clear-eyed about both what he did and what was done for him. The grace did not pretend the persecution never happened; it worked through someone who knew exactly who he had been.

Psalm 118 answers this with a voice that knows both the depth and the rescue: I shall not die, but live, and declare the works of the Lord. This psalm was written in the shadow of genuine danger, probably for a king or leader returning from battle, and its exultation is not cheap. It comes from someone who has been through something. Paul's testimony and the psalm are both the voice of a person changed in a way they did not manufacture themselves.

The Gospel — Luke 7:36-50

This dinner scene is one of Luke's most cinematic moments. A Pharisee named Simon has invited Jesus to recline at table with him — a notable thing; Pharisees are often in conflict with Jesus in the Gospels, and this one seems to be in some posture between curiosity and scrutiny. At meals like this, the doors would have been open to the community; it was not unusual for people to drift in and stand along the walls. What was unusual was what this woman did.

Luke calls her simply "a sinful woman in the city" — a phrase that, in this context, almost certainly means a prostitute. She enters, goes to where Jesus reclines, and begins to weep. His feet would have been extended behind him on the couch, and she bends down and bathes them with her tears, dries them with her hair, and anoints them with expensive oil from an alabaster jar she has brought. It is extravagant, and it is lavish, and it is utterly disregarding of what anyone in the room must be thinking.

Simon thinks it, anyway: if Jesus were truly a prophet, he would know what kind of woman this is. Jesus, of course, knows exactly who she is — and also who Simon is, which is the point. He tells the little parable of two debtors, one owing ten times the other, both forgiven entirely, and asks Simon which one will love the creditor more. Simon gives the obvious answer. Then Jesus does something sharp and almost uncomfortable: he turns it into a comparison between the woman and his host. Simon offered none of the basic gestures of hospitality — no water for the feet, no greeting kiss, no oil for the head. The woman, who has no standing in this room and no claim on anyone's courtesy, has done all of it, with tears, with her hair, with the most expensive thing she apparently owned.

Jesus does not say she is forgiven because she loved. He says her sins are forgiven, and therefore she has loved much. The order matters. The love is the fruit of the mercy, not the payment for it.

What the two readings share: the shape of grace

These two texts are not connected by theme or by liturgical season — they are simply what the Church offers today. But they share a shape. Paul's testimony and the woman's gesture are both portraits of someone who knows what they cost, knows who they were, and has been met anyway. Paul names this "grace." Jesus names it "forgiveness." In both cases, the one who receives it becomes someone different — not by forgetting, but by being freed.

To carry today

Think of something in your past that you still carry some quiet shame about — something you have made peace with on the surface but that still has weight underneath. Paul says the grace was not ineffective; the woman goes home in peace. Ask, honestly, whether you have received that peace for yourself, or whether you are still paying a debt that has already been cancelled.
