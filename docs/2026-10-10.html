Twenty-seventh Week in Ordinary Time

A woman praises Jesus' mother; he opens the praise wide enough for everyone.

First readingGalatians 3:22-29
PsalmPsalm 105:2-3, 4-5, 6-7
GospelLuke 11:27-28

Two verses. That is all the Gospel gives us today — a woman's cry from the crowd and Jesus' answer. It would be easy to read past it quickly, especially alongside Paul's grand architecture of law, faith, and inheritance in Galatians. But the two readings are not on separate tracks. They are saying, with different tools, the same arresting thing: that the circle of God's family is larger than anyone expected, and that the door into it is not bloodline but hearing and doing.

The first reading — Galatians 3:22-29

By the time Paul writes these lines, he is deep into one of the most urgent arguments of his career. The communities in Galatia were being told by some teachers that Gentile converts needed to take on the Jewish law — circumcision and the whole structure of observance — in order to be fully included in God's covenant people. Paul pushes back hard, and this passage is close to the heart of his counterargument.

His key image here is the paidagogos, translated as "disciplinarian." In the ancient world, this was not a schoolteacher; it was the household slave assigned to walk a child to school and keep him out of trouble, supervising behavior until he reached maturity. The law, Paul says, played this role in Israel's life — it was not bad, it was prior, a guardian for the time before the full inheritance arrived. But now that faith in Christ has come, the disciplinarian has been released from duty. The child has come into the estate.

What is the estate? Here Paul says something that must have made jaws drop: in Christ, there is neither Jew nor Greek, slave nor free, male nor female. This is not a claim that differences disappear — Paul is not naive about the world — but a claim about standing before God. Every one of those categories in the ancient world carried enormous weight about who was inside and who was outside, who counted and who counted for less. Paul dismantles all of it. The criterion is not ethnicity, legal status, or sex; it is belonging to Christ. And belonging to Christ, he adds, makes you a descendant of Abraham — an heir of the oldest promise God ever made.

Psalm 105 sweeps in underneath this with exactly the right response: The Lord remembers his covenant for ever. The psalm addresses "descendants of Abraham, his servants, sons of Jacob, his chosen ones" — and Paul has just told his Gentile readers that this address belongs to them now as well. Singing this psalm after Galatians 3 is not mere liturgical decoration; it is the inheritance being formally handed over.

The Gospel — Luke 11:27-28

The setting in Luke is that Jesus has just finished teaching, and the crowd is pressing around him. A woman — unnamed, unimportant by the standards of the day — calls out in what is clearly a gesture of blessing and praise. "Blessed is the womb that carried you and the breasts at which you nursed." It is a beautiful, earthy exclamation, and it is also the most natural form of praise available to her: to honor a great man by honoring his mother.

Jesus does not dismiss her or correct her sharply. What he does is more interesting: he redirects. Rather, he says — not instead, but rather, which is something like more to the point — blessed are those who hear the word of God and observe it. He is not diminishing his mother. He is refusing to let the circle close around a single womb, a single family, a single bloodline. The blessedness his mother carries, he says, comes from exactly what you can also have: hearing and doing. She is not blessed because she bore him biologically. She is blessed because she received the word, believed it, and said yes. That, he tells the woman in the crowd, is available to her. It is available to everyone listening.

What Paul and Jesus are each refusing

Both readings are pushing against the same human habit: drawing the family of God small, keeping it defined by categories that not everyone can enter. Paul's opponents wanted to keep it within the boundaries of Torah observance. The woman's natural impulse was to locate blessedness in a body, a mother, a lineage — the way greatness was always anchored in the ancient world. Jesus and Paul both decline. Not because belonging matters little, but because belonging is now open by a different door: faith that acts, a word that is heard and kept.

There is something quietly radical about the Alleluia verse repeating Jesus' own words from the Gospel — Blessed are those who hear the word of God and observe it — as the congregation's response before the Gospel is even proclaimed. The assembly is already, in singing it, demonstrating exactly what it describes.

To carry today

Paul lists the old dividing lines — ethnicity, status, sex — and says none of them determine your standing before God. We have our own versions of those lines, the ones we use to sort people into those who belong and those who don't. Spend a moment today noticing where you have drawn one of those lines in your own thinking, and ask what it would look like to believe, practically and not just theoretically, that the circle is as wide as Christ is.
