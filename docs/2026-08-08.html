Memorial of

Saint Dominic, Priest

The just man, because of his faith, shall live — and faith the size of a mustard seed moves mountains.

First readingHabakkuk 1:12—2:4
PsalmPsalm 9:8-9, 10-11, 12-13
GospelMatthew 17:14-20

Today the Church remembers Dominic de Guzmán, the Spanish priest who founded the Order of Preachers in the early thirteenth century — a man who gave his life to proclaiming the Gospel in an age of confusion and heresy, and who reportedly spent his nights in prayer so fervent that his brothers could hear him weeping for sinners. The readings the Church places alongside his feast were not chosen with him in mind — these are the ordinary weekday readings, cycling through Habakkuk and Matthew on their own schedule — but they fit the spirit of a man who knew how to wait on God and how to act when the moment came.

The first reading — Habakkuk 1:12—2:4

Habakkuk is one of the shorter and stranger prophets, and it helps to know what kind of book it is before wading in. Unlike most prophets, Habakkuk does not spend much time delivering God's word to Israel. Instead, he argues with God. The whole book is structured as a complaint and a reply, a confrontation between a man who cannot make sense of what he sees and a God who answers — not always in the way the man expects.

What Habakkuk sees is the Babylonian empire on the march, devouring smaller nations, crushing the just and the innocent, and apparently unstoppable. And Habakkuk finds himself in an almost unbearable position: he believes in a God who is holy and cannot look on evil, and yet he watches evil succeed. The fishing imagery in this passage is vivid and dark — the Babylonians drag entire peoples in nets the way a fisherman hauls in a catch, and then they sacrifice to those nets, worshipping the instruments of their own power. It is a striking image of empire turning brute force into religion.

So Habakkuk climbs his guard post and waits. That image is worth sitting with: he does not walk away from God in disgust, and he does not perform a false peace he does not feel. He stations himself and watches. This is a different kind of faith — not the absence of complaint, but the willingness to keep the argument going, to stay in relationship even when God seems silent.

And God answers. Not by explaining the suffering, but by telling Habakkuk to write down the vision, because the vision has its own time and will not be late. Then comes the line that would echo for centuries: "the just man, because of his faith, shall live." In Hebrew, that word for faith carries the sense of steadiness, of holding firm — not certainty that everything will be explained, but a refusal to abandon the post. Psalm 9 catches this exactly in its refrain: "You forsake not those who seek you, O Lord." The psalm is addressed to a God enthroned, a God who sees, a God who has not forgotten the cry of the poor — even when appearances suggest otherwise.

The Gospel — Matthew 17:14-20

This episode in Matthew sits just after the Transfiguration, which is worth knowing because the contrast is stark. Three disciples have just been on the mountain with Jesus, watching him shine with light, hearing the voice from the cloud, falling on their faces in terror. And then they come down the mountain to find the other nine disciples standing uselessly around a suffering boy, having failed to help him at all.

The father's words carry exhausted grief: "I brought him to your disciples, but they could not cure him." Jesus's response — "O faithless and perverse generation, how long will I be with you?" — is one of the more unsettling lines in the Gospels, not least because it is aimed at his own disciples. It is not a warm scolding. It sounds genuinely weary.

He heals the boy, and afterward the disciples ask their quiet, necessary question: why couldn't we? And the answer is faith — or rather, the near-total absence of it. Even a mustard seed's worth would have been enough. A mustard seed, for the record, is tiny: barely visible between two fingers. The point is not that they needed enormous faith, but that they had effectively none. They had the words, the commission, the example — and when it came to the actual moment of prayer, something failed to ignite.

Where Habakkuk and the disciples meet

The word that holds these two readings together is waiting and watching — or rather, the difference between waiting actively in faith and waiting passively in doubt. Habakkuk climbs his watchtower and stays there, expecting an answer even when he cannot imagine what it will be. The disciples, by contrast, seem to have done nothing wrong from the outside but failed at exactly the interior point: they reached for something they did not really believe they had. Faith is not the performance of the right words. It is the conviction, however small and frightened, that the power to act belongs to God and has actually been placed in your hands.

Dominic understood this. He preached fearlessly not because he had no doubts but because he had learned — night by night, on his knees — to trust a God who does not forsake those who seek him.

To carry today

Is there a place in your life right now where you are going through the motions — praying the words, doing the thing — but without any real expectation that God will move? Not a crisis of belief, just a quiet dulling of trust. Spend a moment today naming it honestly, and ask for the faith that does not need to be large, only real.
