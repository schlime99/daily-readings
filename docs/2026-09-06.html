Twenty-third Sunday in Ordinary Time

Three readings, one question: when someone near you is heading toward harm, do you speak?

First readingEzekiel 33:7-9
PsalmPsalm 95:1-2, 6-7, 8-9
Second readingRomans 13:8-10
GospelMatthew 18:15-20

There is a kind of love that says nothing, that keeps the peace at any cost, that would rather let someone walk toward a wall than risk the awkwardness of speaking. Today's readings look that kind of love in the eye and gently, firmly, name it as something less than love. What the three texts share is a picture of a community that takes one another seriously enough to tell the truth.

The first reading — Ezekiel 33:7-9

Ezekiel is writing during one of the worst periods in Israel's history — the Babylonian exile, roughly the sixth century before Christ. Jerusalem has fallen or is about to. The people are scattered, stunned, and tempted toward despair or toward the comfortable lie that things are not so bad. Into this situation God gives Ezekiel a particular role, and the word for it is watchman — the person posted on the city wall whose whole job is to spot danger and shout a warning.

The imagery carries enormous weight. A watchman who sees the enemy coming and stays silent out of fear, or laziness, or some calculation about the social cost of raising the alarm — that watchman has not simply failed at a task. He has become complicit in what follows. The text is blunt about this: if the wicked person dies and no warning was given, God will hold the silent watchman responsible. This is not a soft metaphor; it is meant to press.

But notice equally what the text does not say. If the watchman warns and the person refuses to turn, that person dies for his own guilt, and the watchman is free. The passage does not make Ezekiel responsible for outcomes, only for the act of speaking. This distinction matters enormously — the text is asking for courage, not for control.

Psalm 95 answers this reading beautifully. "If today you hear his voice, harden not your hearts." The psalm is a call to worship, joyful and sweeping — come, let us bow down, let us sing — but its refrain keeps pulling back to this one urgent fact: God is speaking now, and the question is whether we will let it land. The Meribah reference is to the famous episode in the desert where Israel grumbled and tested God despite having seen miracle after miracle. The psalm uses it as a kind of cautionary echo: hardness of heart is not a dramatic falling away; it is often just a slow habit of not quite responding.

The second reading — Romans 13:8-10

Paul's letter to the Romans is his longest and most systematic, and by chapter thirteen he is in the practical section — how do Christians actually live in a Roman city, with neighbors, with debts, with obligations? This little passage runs on its own track, somewhat separate from the Ezekiel and the Gospel, though it ends up illuminating both. Paul makes a remarkable claim: all the commandments can be gathered into the single instruction to love your neighbor as yourself. Love, he says, does no evil to the neighbor — and so love is the fulfillment of everything the law was reaching toward.

What this passage does for today is quietly reframe the other two. Speaking a hard truth, going to your brother about a fault — these are not violations of love or acts of aggression dressed up in spiritual language. They are what love looks like when it takes the other person seriously. Love that never risks anything, never says a difficult word, never agrees to be uncomfortable on someone else's behalf, has quietly collapsed into something more like indifference.

The Gospel — Matthew 18:15-20

This passage from Matthew is one of the most practically detailed in the Gospels. Jesus is giving the community a process — not a vague spiritual posture, but an actual sequence of steps for what to do when someone wrongs you or when you see a brother or sister going wrong. Start privately, just the two of you. If that fails, bring one or two others. If that fails, bring it to the whole church. And if even that fails, the passage ends in something that sounds hard: treat them as a Gentile or a tax collector.

That last phrase is worth pausing on. It sounds like rejection — write them off, cut them loose. But in Matthew's Gospel, tax collectors are not simply written off; they sit at Jesus's table. Matthew himself was a tax collector. The phrase may be less about exclusion and more about starting over: treat them the way you would treat someone not yet inside — with patience, with evangelization, with the hope that the door is still open.

The passage closes with the remarkable promise that where two or three gather in Christ's name, he is in the midst of them. After a section about conflict and correction, this feels like the ground under everything: the community's ability to do any of this hard work depends on the presence of someone larger than the dispute.

What Ezekiel and Matthew share: the weight of not speaking

Both texts are asking what it costs to stay silent. Ezekiel frames it as a structural role — the watchman has a position of responsibility. Jesus frames it as ordinary community life — your brother, just the two of you. But in both cases, the assumption is the same: love obliges you to say something. Not to fix, not to control the outcome, not to win. Just to speak, and then to trust what follows to God.

To carry today

Think of someone in your life — a friend, a family member, someone you see regularly — about whom you have had a quiet, persistent worry that you have never voiced. Not a judgment, just a concern. Ask yourself honestly whether your silence is kindness or avoidance, and whether the person you love deserves the small courage of a gentle word.
