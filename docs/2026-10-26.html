Thirtieth Week in Ordinary Time

A woman bent double for eighteen years stands upright in a moment, and glorifies God.

First readingEphesians 4:32–5:8
PsalmPsalm 1:1-2, 3, 4, 6
GospelLuke 13:10-17

There is a before and an after in this Gospel, and they are separated by the laying on of hands. Before: a woman who has not seen the horizon in eighteen years, whose whole world has been the ground beneath her. After: she stands, and the first thing she does is glorify God. The reading from Ephesians is working on a different track entirely, but both pieces share a conviction that something has genuinely changed — that what we were and what we are now are not the same thing.

The first reading — Ephesians 4:32–5:8

We are in the second half of the letter to the Ephesians, past the great doctrinal opening about God's plan to gather all things in Christ, and into the practical section: now that you know this, here is how to live. Paul — or the writer in Paul's name, as scholars sometimes note — has been cataloguing the old life and the new life in contrast, and this passage is the climax of that contrast. The list of what to avoid is not squeamishness; it is clarity about what belongs to which world. Greed, Paul says, is a form of idolatry — not a throwaway comparison, but a precise one. Whatever commands your deepest attention and trust is what you actually worship, and accumulation does that to people as surely as any statue.

But the positive charge is the astonishing one: be imitators of God, as beloved children. Paul does not say "try to improve" or "follow the rules more carefully." He says: imitate your Father, the way a child naturally picks up the gestures and habits of the parent they love and watch closely. The model for that imitation is immediate — Christ, who handed himself over as an offering. The word for "fragrant aroma" there is temple language, the language of sacrifice accepted by God, and Paul is using it deliberately. The whole sacrificial system has been fulfilled in one act of love, and we are now called to live inside that logic.

Then comes the pivotal line, and it deserves a moment: "You were once darkness, but now you are light in the Lord. Live as children of light." Not "you were in the dark" but "you were darkness" — and not "you are now enlightened" but "you are light." The change is not cosmetic. Something has been altered at the level of what you are.

Psalm 1 is the answer a Jewish congregation would have recognized: the person rooted in God's word is like a tree by running water, fruitful in season, green when everything else has gone dry. The psalm is less about effort than about source — where are your roots drawing from?

The Gospel — Luke 13:10-17

Luke places this story in Jesus's long journey toward Jerusalem, a journey that is as much about who he is as where he is going. He is teaching in a synagogue — the normal place, the normal day, the normal setting. And then he sees her. The text says he called to her, which is worth pausing on. She did not push through the crowd. She did not call out to him. He saw her and he acted.

She has been bent over for eighteen years, Luke says, "bound by a spirit." Ancient medicine did not always separate physical and spiritual causes the way we do, and Luke is not making a claim about her moral state — Jesus nowhere suggests she has done anything wrong. What the language captures is that her condition has had a grip on her, a hold, a weight. And it has had it for a long time. Eighteen years is long enough to stop expecting anything different.

The synagogue leader's objection is not ridiculous on its own terms. The sabbath rest was serious, commanded, honored — a genuine gift of freedom built into the week. His mistake is not that he cares about the sabbath; it is that he has confused the sign with the thing it points to. The sabbath was given as a day of liberation, of rest from servitude, of restoration to right order. And Jesus, with what reads as barely-contained exasperation, points this out: you untie your animals on the sabbath so they can drink. This woman has been tied up for eighteen years. What exactly is the argument against untying her today, on the day made for exactly this?

He calls her "daughter of Abraham" — a designation of full belonging, of covenant membership, of dignity. She is not a problem. She is family.

What freedom is actually for

The Ephesians reading and the Gospel are not obviously connected, but they share a structure: something that had a hold on you no longer does, and that changes what you are supposed to do next. Paul says the old life of darkness is over — live as what you now are, children of light. Jesus says this woman's bondage is over — she stands up and glorifies God. In both cases, freedom is not the endpoint. It is the beginning of something.

The synagogue leader treats the sabbath as a fence around a prohibition. Jesus treats it as the shape of restoration. Paul treats the Christian life not as a tighter set of rules but as the natural expression of a new nature. Freedom, in both cases, opens toward something rather than simply away from something.

To carry today

Think of something that has had a hold on you for a long time — a habit, a resentment, a way of seeing yourself that bends you inward. Ask honestly whether you have stopped expecting it to change. Sit with the possibility that the word "daughter" or "son" applies to you, and that being named that way changes what comes next.
