Wednesday · 16 September

Memorial of

Saints Cornelius, Pope, and Cyprian, Bishop, Martyrs

Two bishops who argued fiercely, died faithfully, and are remembered together.

First reading1 Corinthians 12:31–13:13
PsalmPsalm 33:2-3, 4-5, 12, 22
GospelLuke 7:31-35

Today the Church remembers two men who did not always agree with each other — Cornelius, bishop of Rome in the early 250s, and Cyprian, bishop of Carthage — but who shared both a commitment to the unity of the Church and, eventually, martyrdom under the Emperor Valerian. They are an unexpected pair to honor with Paul's great hymn to love, and yet the pairing is not accidental. What held these two difficult, strong-willed leaders together was precisely what Paul names as the thing that outlasts every gift.

The first reading — 1 Corinthians 12:31–13:13

Paul is writing to a community that has become, frankly, a mess. The Corinthians are proud of their spiritual gifts — tongues, prophecy, knowledge — and they are using those gifts as a kind of social currency, ranking themselves against one another, showing off in worship, treating the assembly as an arena. The whole of chapter 12 is Paul's patient attempt to explain that every gift comes from one Spirit, serves one body, and exists for the common good rather than personal glory. This passage is its climax.

What Paul does here is genuinely startling. He does not say that love is the greatest gift, as if it were simply first on a ranked list. He says it is something different in kind — "a still more excellent way." Gifts, even the most spectacular ones, are instruments. Love is the point. Without it, eloquence becomes noise, prophecy becomes empty, and even self-sacrifice — even handing your body over — gains you nothing. That last one should stop us cold. Paul allows for the possibility of a martyrdom that means nothing, if it is not rooted in love. The martyrs Cornelius and Cyprian, then, are not honored simply for dying. They are honored for dying as the culmination of lives spent in love for the Church and for God.

The portrait of love that follows is probably the most familiar passage in the New Testament, which is a pity in a way, because familiarity makes it easy to read it as a warm sentiment rather than as the demanding thing it is. "It is not quick-tempered, it does not brood over injury" — this is not describing an emotion but a discipline, a set of choices made again and again. The love Paul describes here looks a great deal like the work of two embattled bishops in the third century, navigating schism and persecution and theological controversy, choosing again and again not to write the other off.

Paul closes with the image of the mirror — "at present we see indistinctly, as in a mirror" — and the promise of the face-to-face encounter that is coming. Love is the only thing that survives into that moment, because love is what God is. Faith will have become sight. Hope will have become possession. But love — love will simply continue, because it was always already participation in what is permanent.

Psalm 33 responds by singing of a God whose word is trustworthy, whose earth is full of kindness. It is a psalm of confidence that what God has promised, God will do. After Paul's dizzying height, the psalm is a kind of landing — yes, that love comes from somewhere, and we can trust its source.

The Gospel — Luke 7:31-35

These two readings are on separate tracks today, and it is worth being honest about that. Paul's hymn and Luke's scene share almost no vocabulary, no imagery, no obvious theme. But Luke's passage is worth sitting with on its own terms, because it is one of the more grimly funny moments in the Gospels.

Jesus is frustrated. He has just been contrasting John the Baptist with "the Son of Man" — himself — and he is trying to describe what it feels like to deal with a crowd that has made up its mind in advance. He reaches for a children's game: children in the marketplace calling to one another, complaining that no one played along. We played the flute and you wouldn't dance. We sang a funeral song and you wouldn't weep. The crowd finds fault either way — John fasted, so they called him possessed; Jesus ate and drank, so they called him a drunk and a glutton. The standard keeps moving because the standard is not actually the point. The point is to avoid being changed.

This is a form of bad faith that every reader will recognize, because we practice it ourselves. When we have decided that something cannot be true, or that a person cannot be worth listening to, we find reasons to dismiss whatever they do. The ascetic is crazy. The sociable one is worldly. Neither can win, because neither was ever being evaluated fairly.

The last line — "wisdom is vindicated by all her children" — is the quiet punchline. Sophia, wisdom, is a figure in the Jewish tradition, and Jesus seems to be aligning both John and himself as her messengers. The vindication will not come from this crowd's approval. It will come from the lives of those who actually listened and were changed.

These two readings run on separate tracks

The first reading carries the weight of the day: Paul's vision of love as the animating center of every gift and every life, offered on the feast of two men who lived it imperfectly and fully. The Gospel adds a kind of edge — a reminder that the wisdom these martyrs embodied will always be available to be dismissed by those who have decided, in advance, not to see it.

To carry today

Think of someone whose manner you have used as a reason not to hear what they are actually saying — too intense, too casual, too strict, too easy. Ask whether you are honestly evaluating them or looking for an exit. The crowd in the Gospel was doing the latter, and it cost them everything they might have received.
