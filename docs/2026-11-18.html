Thirty-third Week in Ordinary Time

God gives his servants real work to do, and he will return to see what they made of it.

First readingRevelation 4:1-11
PsalmPsalm 150:1b-2, 3-4, 5-6
GospelLuke 19:11-28

We are near the end of the liturgical year, and the Church knows it. The readings these last weeks of Ordinary Time have a particular urgency to them — an eye on the horizon, an ear cocked for a trumpet. Today that urgency takes two very different forms: John in Revelation sees the throne room of heaven and falls into dazzled praise, while Jesus tells a parable that is frankly unsettling, about a king who goes away and comes back and wants to know what his servants did while he was gone.

The first reading — Revelation 4:1-11

The Book of Revelation is not, first of all, a code to be cracked for predictions about the future. It is a vision — a piece of apocalyptic literature written to persecuted Christians in the late first century, using vivid symbolic imagery drawn from the Hebrew prophets, especially Ezekiel and Isaiah. When John hears "Come up here," he is given a glimpse behind the curtain of ordinary reality, into what is always and already true: God is on the throne, and heaven is full of unceasing worship.

The description of that throne room is worth reading slowly. There is a figure of such blazing splendor that John can only reach for gemstones — jasper, carnelian, emerald. There are four living creatures covered with eyes, which is not meant to be disturbing so much as overwhelming: they see everything, in every direction, all the time, and what they see drives them to one continuous cry — "Holy, holy, holy." This is the Trisagion, the thrice-holy, which the Church has drawn into every Mass. We say it too, without perhaps always feeling the full strangeness of what we are joining.

The twenty-four elders are likely a symbolic number representing the full company of God's people — twelve tribes of Israel, twelve apostles — and what they do is striking: they lay their crowns before the throne. Whatever authority or dignity they hold, they offer it back. The praise at the end of the chapter gets at why: "because of your will they came to be and were created." Everything exists by an act of divine generosity. The only fitting response to that is exactly this — to lay down what you have and say: it was always yours.

Psalm 150 follows without missing a beat. It is the last psalm in the Psalter, the great final burst of praise, and it essentially asks every instrument and every breathing creature to do what the living creatures never stop doing. The two texts set the same scene from different angles: the heavenly assembly and the whole created world, both erupting into the same song.

The Gospel — Luke 19:11-28

Jesus tells this parable just outside Jerusalem, in the last days before his death. Luke is careful to explain why: the crowd thinks the Kingdom of God is about to appear in some sudden, complete, unmistakable way. Jesus tells the parable to correct that expectation, and the correction is pointed.

A nobleman goes away to receive a kingdom and then return. While he is gone, he leaves money — in the original Greek, minas, a substantial sum — with his servants and tells them to put it to work. The language matters: he doesn't say "guard this carefully." He says engage in trade. He expects increase. When he comes back, the first two servants have doubled and quintupled what they were given, and they are entrusted with more. The third servant returns the original coin untouched, explaining that he was afraid, that his master is severe, and that he didn't want to risk losing what he had.

The master's response is sharp and not entirely comfortable: he doesn't dispute the description of himself as demanding, he uses it. If you knew I was demanding, why did you do nothing? The coin is taken and given to the one who already has the most, and when the crowd protests the apparent unfairness, Jesus states plainly that this is how it works: faithfulness increases capacity, and paralysis diminishes it.

There is also a harsher subplot — the enemies who sent word that they wanted no part of this king — and the ending is stark. Luke doesn't soften it. The parable isn't a story about how everyone turns out fine. It is a story about the return of a king and what that moment reveals about what each person did with what they were given.

What the throne room and the parable share

The first reading and the Gospel are, on the surface, as different as two texts could be: ecstatic vision on one hand, economic parable on the other. But they are circling the same truth from opposite directions. Revelation shows us that God is the source of everything — creation exists because of his will, every crown belongs to him. The parable shows us that this God is not simply to be admired from a safe distance. He has distributed his goods into human hands and gone away, and he expects those hands to work.

The servant who hid his coin thought he was being cautious and reverent. What he was actually doing was treating the gift as though it were a threat — holding it at arm's length, refusing the relationship it implied. The living creatures don't hold anything back; they pour out their praise without stopping, day and night. The two images together suggest that the right response to God's abundance is not hoarding or caution but generous use.

To carry today

Think about one gift — a talent, a relationship, a responsibility — that you have been holding very carefully, perhaps out of fear of getting it wrong. Ask honestly whether that caution is wisdom or whether it is a way of avoiding the relationship that the gift implies. God has entrusted something real to you, not for safekeeping, but for use.
