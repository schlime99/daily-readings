Twenty-Ninth Week in Ordinary Time

Jesus refuses to let disaster settle the question of who is guilty.

First readingEphesians 4:7-16
PsalmPsalm 122:1-2, 3-4ab, 4cd-5
GospelLuke 13:1-9

Today holds two very different gifts in tension. Paul, writing from prison, is in a mood of high architecture — fitting every part of the Church together into a single, growing Body. Jesus, meanwhile, is out in the crowd, fielding a raw question about blood and rubble and what God must have meant by it. Neither reading is soft. Both are, in their own way, deeply kind.

The first reading — Ephesians 4:7-16

Ephesians is a late letter in Paul's collection, almost certainly written while he was imprisoned in Rome, and its tone is elevated throughout — more like a homily than a quick note to a troubled community. By chapter four, Paul has already spent three chapters on theology and has turned now to the practical question: given all that God has done, what does the Church actually look like from the inside?

He opens with a quote from Psalm 68, a victory psalm about a king returning in triumph, hauling captives behind him and distributing plunder to the people. Paul takes that image and reads Christ's resurrection and ascension into it — the one who descended into death is the same one who now fills heaven, and in his victory he has distributed gifts. The gifts are not, interestingly, dramatic signs or spiritual experiences. They are people: apostles, prophets, evangelists, pastors, teachers. The gift God gave the Church is not a system but a set of human beings charged with building others up.

What Paul is arguing against is the opposite of community: the state of being "tossed by waves" — his phrase — swept along by whatever the latest persuasive teaching happens to be. He calls it "human trickery," and there is a knowing edge to his language; he is watching the young Church get pulled in too many directions by too many confident voices. His prescription is not suspicion, though. It is growth. The way you stop being blown around is by becoming more fully yourself as part of the Body, each ligament doing its actual work, each part contributing to the whole's growth in love. The image is almost biological — a Body that builds itself up from within.

Psalm 122 answers this naturally, even if it was written about a different city. "Let us go rejoicing to the house of the Lord" — Jerusalem, built with compact unity, to which all the tribes go up. For Paul's community, that gathering place is the Body of Christ itself, the unified company of the baptized. The psalm gives the first reading a visual: people on the road, converging, not scattered.

The Gospel — Luke 13:1-9

These two passages are on separate tracks. Paul is building a cathedral; Jesus is answering people who have just heard something terrible.

Someone reports to Jesus that Pilate — the same Pilate who will later send him to the cross — has massacred a group of Galilean pilgrims in the Temple, mixing their blood with the blood of their sacrifices. It is a brutal, desecrating act, and the people telling Jesus about it are clearly waiting for his reaction. There is an unspoken assumption behind the report: these people must have done something to deserve it. Tragedy in the ancient world was routinely read as punishment, and frankly, that instinct has not entirely left the modern world either.

Jesus refuses it directly. "Do you think that because these Galileans suffered in this way they were greater sinners?" No. He adds a second case: eighteen people killed when a tower collapsed at Siloam, a reservoir area south of Jerusalem. Same question, same answer. Accidents are not indictments. The deaths of others do not reveal their guilt.

But Jesus does not stop at correcting the theology of retribution. He turns the question around entirely: "If you do not repent, you will all perish as they did." This sounds alarming until you realize he is removing the self-congratulation that comes with watching someone else suffer. The questioners were implicitly reassuring themselves — those people died, so we must be fine. Jesus says that reasoning is precisely the danger. Not because catastrophe is coming for you specifically, but because the posture of watching other people's disasters as evidence of their sin prevents you from looking honestly at your own life.

The parable that follows softens nothing and yet is somehow tender. A fig tree has produced nothing for three years. The owner wants it cut down — reasonable. The gardener asks for one more season, promises to dig around it, fertilize it, give it every possible help. "It may bear fruit in the future. If not, you can cut it down." The gardener is not naive about what the tree is. He just is not done with it yet.

Patience and urgency, held together

What links the story of the dead Galileans and the story of the unfruitful fig tree is the same refusal to write anyone off on present evidence. Jesus declines to read the dead as guilty; the gardener declines to read the living tree as hopeless. Both moves require the same thing: enough humility to admit that you do not yet have the full picture, and enough seriousness to know that there is a real difference between fruit and no fruit.

The Alleluia verse from Ezekiel says it plainly: "I take no pleasure in the death of the wicked man, but rather in his conversion, that he may live." That is the logic behind the gardener's extra season. It is not indulgence; it is desire.

To carry today

Think of a situation in your life where you have been tempted to treat someone else's difficulty as evidence that they had it coming — whether you've said so aloud or just quietly thought it. Ask yourself what it would mean to hold that judgment a little more loosely, and what honest look at your own life you might have been avoiding in the meantime.
