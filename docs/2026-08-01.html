Saturday · 1 August

Memorial of

Saint Alphonsus Liguori, Bishop and Doctor of the Church

Two prophets stand before power and tell the truth anyway. One lives; one does not.

First readingJeremiah 26:11-16, 24
PsalmPsalm 69:15-16, 30-31, 33-34
GospelMatthew 14:1-12

Today the Church remembers Alphonsus Liguori, an eighteenth-century bishop who spent his life insisting that God's mercy is larger than human fear — that people who feel trapped in their sins are not beyond reach. It is a fitting day for readings about men who tell the truth to dangerous people and pay the price, or nearly pay the price. Both Jeremiah and John the Baptist stand before rulers who have the power to destroy them, and both tell the truth anyway. The shape of their stories is almost identical. The outcomes are not.

The first reading — Jeremiah 26:11-16, 24

We are in the temple courts of Jerusalem, which is the last place you might expect to see a mob forming — but here we are. Jeremiah has just delivered what scholars call the "Temple Sermon," a blistering address in which he warned that God would destroy this very temple if the people did not change their ways. That was not what anyone wanted to hear, especially the priests and the professional prophets whose livelihood and prestige depended on the temple standing forever and God being reliably pleased with his people. So they turned on him.

The accusation is stark: this man deserves death. Blasphemy, or something very close to it — he has prophesied disaster for the holy city, and in their view that makes him an enemy of God rather than a servant of one. They are certain they are right. That certainty is part of what makes this scene so uncomfortable, because they are not wicked people calculating how to silence a critic; they genuinely believe they are defending the faith.

What Jeremiah does in response is remarkable. He does not argue strategy, does not try to negotiate, does not recant. He simply states the facts: God sent him, the message is real, and if they kill him, they will be killing an innocent man — which means the blood will be on their heads, not his. And then, in what may be the quietest act of courage in the whole book, he says: as for me, I am in your hands. Not resignation, and not a death wish — it reads more like a man who has simply decided that the truth is worth whatever comes next.

The crowd turns. Whether from conscience or political calculation or both, the princes conclude that Jeremiah has been speaking in God's name, and that executing prophets who speak in God's name is a path they do not want to start down. A well-placed official named Ahikam shelters him, and he lives.

Psalm 69 is Jeremiah's interior experience set to music — the mire, the flood-waters, the pit about to close. The psalmist cries out from exactly that place of danger, holding on to the conviction that the Lord hears the poor, and his own who are in bonds he spurns not. Prayed after this reading, it sounds like what Jeremiah might have whispered to himself in the courtyard while waiting to find out whether he would live or die.

The Gospel — Matthew 14:1-12

The Gospel tells a story that ends very differently. John the Baptist has been arrested by Herod Antipas, a client-king ruling a portion of what had been his father's territory, on account of a similarly inconvenient truth: John had said publicly that Herod's marriage to Herodias, his brother's wife, was unlawful. He was right, and Herod knew it, which is why Herod feared him and would not simply have him killed — the people regarded John as a prophet, and prophets had a way of mattering to crowds.

The birthday banquet is the mechanism of catastrophe. The daughter of Herodias dances, Herod makes a reckless oath in front of his guests, and Herodias sees her moment. She has her daughter ask for the Baptist's head on a platter. Matthew notes that Herod was distressed — and that detail is important, because this is not a monster at ease with what he is about to do. He is a weak man, trapped by his own boasting and his fear of looking weak in front of people who are watching. He orders the execution anyway.

That is what makes this episode so specific and so grim. Herod knows better. His distress is a moral signal he overrides. The machinery of vanity and peer pressure and political calculation does what it does, and John dies in prison, and his disciples bury him and go tell Jesus.

What mercy looks like under pressure

These two readings are on separate tracks in the sense that they were chosen independently — the Jeremiah passage runs through a lectionary cycle while the Gospel is assigned to this week in Matthew. But they arrive at the same place. In both, a man speaks truth to power. In both, the one in power has a choice. Jeremiah's judges choose rightly, if narrowly. Herod does not. The difference is not intelligence or even goodwill — Herod seems to have had more of both than the priests who wanted Jeremiah dead. The difference is whether a man can bear to be seen doing what he knows is right when it costs him something publicly.

Alphonsus Liguori spent his life in moral theology, which is the Church's ongoing effort to think carefully about exactly these human moments — the pressure, the oath, the crowd, the distress that gets overridden. He was particularly interested in people who felt unworthy of mercy, and he kept insisting that mercy was not a reward for the strong. The readings today, in their way, show him what he was working with.

To carry today

Think of a moment recently when you knew the right thing and the crowd — or the room, or the mood of the evening — pulled you the other way. Not a dramatic choice like Herod's, but the ordinary version: the true thing left unsaid, the correction quietly swallowed. Ask for the honesty to name it to yourself, and the trust that naming it honestly is already the beginning of something better.
