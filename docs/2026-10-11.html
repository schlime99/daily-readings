Twenty-eighth Sunday in Ordinary Time

God prepares a feast, sends servants into the streets, and fills the hall with strangers.

First readingIsaiah 25:6-10a
PsalmPsalm 23:1-3a, 3b-4, 5, 6
Second readingPhilippians 4:12-14, 19-20
GospelMatthew 22:1-14

There is a banquet in the middle of today's readings, and it runs from beginning to end. Isaiah sees it on a mountaintop, Psalm 23 sits at a table spread before enemies, and Matthew's parable empties the palace guest list and refills it from the street. The feast is not a metaphor for something else. It is the thing — abundance, presence, the end of grief, the end of death — offered outright to anyone willing to come in and sit down.

The first reading — Isaiah 25:6-10a

Isaiah 25 belongs to what scholars call the Isaiah Apocalypse, chapters 24–27, a strange and visionary section in the middle of the book that zooms out from specific historical crises to the end of everything. By the time we reach chapter 25, the prophet has been describing the shaking of the whole earth — nations laid low, proud cities broken. And then, without warning, the scene shifts to a mountain, and there is food. Not ordinary food: "juicy, rich food and pure, choice wines," the text says, and the doubling is deliberate — this is a feast so good it has to be said twice. The setting is Mount Zion, the holy mountain, which in the ancient imagination is the place where heaven and earth touch, where God is most present.

But the feast is for all peoples, not just Israel. That phrase would have been startling to first hearers, and it is worth pausing over. God does not merely invite the nations in as observers; he sets the table for them. And what else does he do there? He destroys the veil — better translated as the shroud, the cloth draped over the nations, the fabric of grief and unknowing. And he destroys death. The word in the Hebrew is blunt: he swallows it up. The same death that seemed so permanent, the same grief that seemed to have the last word, is consumed.

Then comes the quiet line that follows the announcement: "Behold our God, to whom we looked to save us." This is not a triumphant shout but something closer to relief — the recognition of a face you have been waiting to see. There he is. There is the one we were hoping for. Psalm 23 answers this directly: the table spread before me, the cup overflowing, the goodness and kindness that follow all the days of my life. The shepherd of Psalm 23 and the host of Isaiah 25 are the same figure — the one who provides, who protects through the dark valley, who wipes the tears away.

The second reading — Philippians 4:12-14, 19-20

Paul is writing from prison, or under house arrest — his situation is genuinely precarious — and his letter to the Philippians is one of the warmest he ever wrote. This passage runs on its own track, mostly disconnected from the banquet imagery of the other two readings. But it is not irrelevant. Paul says he has learned to be content in every state, well-fed or hungry, with much or with little. The word "learned" matters: this is not a natural disposition, not an easy temperament, but something acquired through practice and failure and grace. "I can do all things in him who strengthens me" is one of the most quoted lines in Paul, often stripped from context and applied to athletic feats. In context it means something more specific: I can endure both feast and famine without losing my footing, because my stability does not rest on which one I am currently living through. That is a harder and more interesting claim. And his closing — "My God will fully supply whatever you need, in accord with his glorious riches in Christ Jesus" — is, quietly, its own small banquet promise.

The Gospel — Matthew 22:1-14

Matthew 22 opens with a confrontation. Jesus is in the Temple, the chief priests and elders pressing him on his authority, and he answers them in parables — which is to say he answers indirectly, letting the story do the work the argument cannot. This parable of the wedding feast comes right after the parable of the wicked tenants, and the audience knows who they are in these stories. The first guests, the ones who were invited and refused to come, represent those who had every preparation and every reason — and still found their farm or their business more pressing. Some go even further and kill the king's servants, which Matthew's first readers would almost certainly have heard as a reference to the prophets.

The king's response — destroying those murderers and burning their city — is violent, and the parable does not soften it. Matthew is writing after the fall of Jerusalem in 70 AD, and his community heard that detail in a particular way. What matters for us is what happens next: the doors open completely. Good and bad alike are brought in from the main roads. The hall fills.

Then comes the strange, uncomfortable coda: the man without a wedding garment. He does not answer when questioned; he is simply silent. The usual explanation is that guests would have been given garments at the door, so refusing the garment was refusing the feast on the host's terms even while physically present in the room. He came in but did not actually accept what was being offered. Being present is not the same as receiving.

The feast that costs nothing and asks everything

Isaiah's table and Matthew's banquet are the same image from two angles. Isaiah shows the feast as pure gift — God provides, God destroys the shroud, God wipes the tears. Matthew shows that the gift still requires a response. The doors are as wide as the roads allow; the guest list has been thrown open to everyone the servants could find. But walking in and sitting down are not the same as receiving the garment, accepting the terms, letting the feast actually reach you. The silence of the man without a garment is one of the saddest moments in the Gospels — not defiance, just an inability to receive.

To carry today

Consider where in your own life you receive what God offers only on your own conditions — present at the feast but keeping something back, unwilling to put on what is handed at the door. Think about what it would feel like to simply accept that the table has already been set, the cup already filled, the grief already scheduled for its end. Ask for the grace to actually sit down.
