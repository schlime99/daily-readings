Twenty-eighth Week in Ordinary Time

Paul says freedom is the whole point; Jesus says the inside of the cup is what needs cleaning.

First readingGalatians 5:1-6
PsalmPsalm 119:41, 43, 44, 45, 47, 48
GospelLuke 11:37-41

There is a particular kind of weariness that comes from trying to earn what has already been given. Paul knows that weariness, and he is writing to people who have been talked back into it. Jesus, at a dinner table in Luke, is pressing the same wound from a different angle — not the law of Moses this time, but the human habit of making the outside presentable while leaving the inside untouched. The two readings run on separate tracks today, as weekday pairings often do, but they end up pointing at the same interior room.

The first reading — Galatians 5:1-6

By chapter five, Paul's letter to the Galatians has reached something close to a cry of alarm. The Galatians are Gentile converts who came to faith through Paul's preaching, and now teachers have arrived behind him telling them that faith in Christ is not quite enough — that the men, at minimum, also need to be circumcised and to observe the Mosaic law. To modern ears this can sound like an obscure ancient argument, but the stakes as Paul understands them are enormous: if you can complete or secure your standing before God by a ritual act, then what Christ did on the cross is demoted to a starting point rather than the whole thing.

Paul's language is blunt in a way that can startle us. He says that if you accept circumcision as a means of justification, "Christ will be of no benefit to you." He does not mean that the rite itself is evil — elsewhere he says circumcision counts for nothing, and uncircumcision counts for nothing either. What counts for nothing is using either one as a lever on God. The moment you make your standing before God a legal transaction you can execute, you have stepped out of the economy of grace and into a different system entirely. And you cannot be half in each. "You have fallen from grace," he says — not as a punishment, but as a description of how the logic works.

The phrase he ends on is worth sitting with: "faith working through love." That is what replaces the legal framework, and it is not easier — it is more demanding, because love cannot be completed and checked off. But it is also free, because you are not doing it to secure something. You are doing it from within something already given.

Psalm 119, the great hymn to God's law, might seem like an odd pairing with Paul's fierce anti-law argument, but look at how the psalmist actually speaks about the law: "I will walk at liberty, because I seek your precepts." The law the psalmist loves is not a transaction but a relationship, not a cage but the shape of a life oriented toward someone. That is not so far from Paul as it first appears.

The Gospel — Luke 11:37-41

The scene Luke sets is quietly rich. A Pharisee invites Jesus to dinner — this is not automatically hostile; some Pharisees are genuinely curious about him — and Jesus, without ceremony or apology, simply sits down without performing the ritual hand-washing that a pious Jew would have done. The Pharisee notices and is amazed, which in Luke's Greek carries more bewilderment than outrage.

Jesus does not explain himself. He pivots and names what he sees. The Pharisees, he says, make the outside of the cup gleam while the inside holds "plunder and evil" — and that word plunder is worth pausing over, because it suggests not just private sin but something taken from others, accumulated through unjust practices. The outside-cleaning becomes a way of not having to look inside, of performing righteousness while the interior life goes unexamined.

Then comes the line that should stop us: "Did not the maker of the outside also make the inside?" It is almost a joke in its simplicity, but it lands like a stone. Of course the same God made both. The outside was never independent of the inside. You cannot tend one and ignore the other and call it devotion.

The remedy Jesus offers is surprising: "give alms, and behold, everything will be clean for you." He does not say pray more, or perform the washing after all. He says open your hand. The interior is healed by an outward act — but an outward act that flows from within, from noticing someone else's need and choosing to address it. Almsgiving in the tradition is not charity as we vaguely mean it; it is justice in motion, the reordering of what you have toward those who lack.

Inside and outside

What connects Paul and the dinner table scene is this: both are about the difference between managing appearances before God and before others, and actually being changed. Paul's opponents in Galatia want a visible mark that secures standing. The Pharisee in Luke wants clean hands before the meal. Neither is necessarily cynical — this is worth saying, because it is easy to be smug at the Pharisee's expense. Most of us want the same things: to be seen as observant, to feel that we have done what is required. The question both texts press is whether that desire is running the whole show, or whether something more vulnerable and more real is happening underneath.

To carry today

Think of one area of your life where you have been more careful about the appearance of goodness than about its actual presence — in a relationship, a habit, a quiet corner of your conscience. You do not need to fix it this moment, but let yourself look at it honestly. Ask for the grace to let God work on the inside, where the real housekeeping is needed.
