Thirty-third Week in Ordinary Time

Jesus weeps over a city that did not know the day of its rescue.

First readingRevelation 5:1-10
PsalmPsalm 149:1b-2, 3-4, 5-6b, 9b
GospelLuke 19:41-44

Two images, both grief-soaked, hold today's liturgy together. In the first, a sealed scroll contains the meaning of history, and no one is worthy to open it — until a Lamb appears, bearing the marks of slaughter. In the second, Jesus crests a hill overlooking Jerusalem and breaks into tears. These are not sentimental images. They ask a searching question: what does it cost to redeem something that cannot save itself?

The first reading — Revelation 5:1-10

The Book of Revelation is not, despite its reputation, primarily a coded roadmap of future catastrophes. It is a letter of pastoral encouragement written to communities facing persecution, using the dream-saturated imagery of apocalyptic literature — a tradition that uses visions, angels, numbers, and cosmic drama to say: God has not lost the thread. John writes from the island of Patmos, likely exiled there, and what he receives in his vision is meant to strengthen ordinary believers who are wondering whether their suffering means anything.

Chapter 5 is one of the book's hinge moments. John has been shown the heavenly throne room, blazing with light and surrounded by living creatures and elders — a scene borrowed partly from Isaiah's vision and Ezekiel's chariot. Now a scroll appears in the right hand of the enthroned One, sealed seven times over. Seven, in this book's symbolic grammar, signals completeness. The scroll is completely sealed, its contents completely inaccessible. An angel cries out: who is worthy to open it? And the answer, shockingly, is no one. Not in heaven, not on earth, not in the underworld.

John weeps. This is an easy verse to skip past, but it is worth pausing. He weeps because if the scroll cannot be opened, then history cannot be read. It means events have no meaning, that suffering is just noise, that the blood of martyrs counts for nothing. The sealed scroll is not just a document — it is the question of whether the world makes sense at all.

Then one of the elders interrupts: stop weeping. The lion of the tribe of Judah, the root of David, has conquered. This is the language of royal messianic expectation — the Messiah as a warrior-king, the fulfillment of everything Israel had hoped for. But when John looks toward where this lion should be, he sees a Lamb — and not a triumphant one. He sees a Lamb "that seemed to have been slain," standing in the middle of the throne. It still bears its wounds. It was killed, and yet it stands.

This is the great reversal at the center of Christian faith, and Revelation states it in the most dramatic theatrical terms possible. The one who can open history's meaning, who can break every seal, is not the one with the most power but the one who gave everything away. The Lamb takes the scroll and the whole court of heaven prostrates itself, singing: worthy are you, because you were slain, and with your blood you purchased people from every corner of the earth. The psalm for today, Psalm 149, catches this same note of joyful vindication — "the LORD loves his people, and he adorns the lowly with victory."

The Gospel — Luke 19:41-44

Luke places this scene at a precise moment: Jesus is descending the Mount of Olives, and Jerusalem comes into view. He has just been acclaimed by his disciples as the king who comes in the Lord's name — the Palm Sunday procession. But instead of triumphant entry, the scene lurches into lamentation. Jesus stops and weeps over the city.

The Greek word Luke uses here is not the quiet weeping he uses elsewhere. It suggests audible sobbing. This is public grief, the kind you cannot suppress. And what Jesus says is devastating in its specificity: if you only knew, on this day, what makes for peace. The phrase "this day" is doing a great deal of work. This is the moment of visitation, the moment when the one who could save them is literally standing on the hill above their city, in tears because he can see what they cannot — that they are about to miss it.

What follows is a prediction, phrased in the passive voice of prophetic literature, of the city's destruction. Jerusalem fell to Roman armies in 70 AD, within a generation of this moment. Luke's first readers likely knew this had happened. The horror Jesus describes — enemies building a siege wall, everything smashed to rubble, not one stone left on another — was not metaphor by the time Luke's community heard it. It was recent history.

What grief looks like when it still loves the thing it grieves

The connection between these two readings is not primarily about judgment, though judgment is real in both. It is about what the Lamb and the weeping Lord share: they grieve over what is lost because they love it completely. The Lamb in Revelation has been slain precisely because the world needed saving and would not save itself. Jesus weeps over Jerusalem not because he has given up on her but because love does not stop at the edge of refusal. Grief of this kind — grief that keeps loving the thing that walked away — is not weakness. It is the shape mercy takes when it is not returned.

The sealed scroll and the sealed city share a structure: something that holds the possibility of meaning, of rescue, of peace, but that cannot open itself. Only love from the outside can break the seals.

To carry today

Think today of some situation in your life that feels closed off — a relationship that has gone cold, a part of yourself you have given up on, a hope that seems past its moment. Consider what it would mean to believe that the scroll is not permanently sealed, that the Lamb is still standing in the middle of the throne. Ask for the grace to grieve over it honestly rather than simply moving on — because grief that still loves is not the same thing as despair.
