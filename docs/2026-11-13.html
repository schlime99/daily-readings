Friday · 13 November

Memorial of

Saint Frances Xavier Cabrini, Virgin

Whoever clings to life will lose it; whoever lets go will find it kept safe.

First reading2 John 4-9
PsalmPsalm 119:1-2, 10-11, 17-18
GospelLuke 17:26-37

Today the Church remembers Frances Xavier Cabrini, the Italian-born nun who crossed the Atlantic more than thirty times to serve immigrants and the poor, and who became the first American citizen to be canonized. It is a good day to notice that the readings do not bend around her feast — they run on their own track, and they are demanding in their own right. The letter of John holds a community steady against false teachers. The Gospel sounds the last trumpet of urgency. And yet, in the background, the life of Cabrini herself is a kind of answer to both: someone who stayed in the teaching of Christ and lost her life in service so completely that she saved it.

The first reading — 2 John 4-9

The Second Letter of John is the shortest book in the New Testament — thirteen verses, which is why the lectionary can read almost all of it in one sitting. It is written to "the Chosen Lady," which almost certainly means a local Christian community, addressed with the warm, slightly formal language of correspondence between church leaders. The author, who calls himself "the Elder," has heard that some members are walking in the truth, and he is glad — genuinely, effusively glad, in the way you are glad when something you feared has not happened yet.

The gladness is guarded, though, because the reason for the letter is a threat. "Many deceivers have gone out into the world," he writes, "those who do not acknowledge Jesus Christ as coming in the flesh." This is the early Church's encounter with a teaching we now call Docetism — from a Greek word meaning "to seem" — the idea that Jesus only appeared to be human, that the Word of God touched the earth without really landing on it. The Elder finds this terrifying, not as a dry theological error but as a practical catastrophe: if Jesus did not truly come in the flesh, then the cross was theater, and no one was saved by anything.

His remedy is not a lecture but a return to the beginning. He does not introduce new arguments; he reaches back to what the community already knows. Love one another. Walk according to his commandments. Stay in the teaching. The psalm for today, from the great Psalm 119, echoes this in its own register — "With all my heart I seek you; let me not stray from your commands." The psalmist is not asking for theological certainty so much as asking not to drift, not to lose the thread through carelessness.

The Elder's warning about being "so progressive as not to remain in the teaching" has an edge of dark humor to it. He is borrowing the vocabulary of sophistication — moving forward, advancing beyond — and turning it inside out. Real advancement, in his view, is faithfulness to the Word that became flesh, not movement away from it.

The Gospel — Luke 17:26-37

This passage falls late in Luke's travel narrative, as Jesus moves steadily toward Jerusalem, and the question of when the Kingdom of God will come is in the air. The disciples are eager for a dramatic arrival; Jesus keeps complicating the picture.

He reaches for two disasters that his audience would have known by heart: the flood and the destruction of Sodom. What is startling is what he does not say about those stories. He does not dwell on the wickedness of the people involved. He dwells on their ordinariness. They were eating and drinking, marrying, buying and selling, planting and building — none of these things are sins. The indictment is not that they were doing terrible things; it is that they were absorbed in the ordinary and therefore blind to what was about to break in. They were so busy tending their lives that they had no hands free when the moment arrived.

Then comes the haunting line: "Remember the wife of Lot." She looked back, the Genesis account says, and was turned to a pillar of salt. Jesus does not explain why she looked back — but most of us know why. She looked back at what she was leaving. At home, at goods, at everything she had accumulated and arranged. And the gesture of looking back killed her. So Jesus says: when the day comes, the person on the housetop must not go down to collect belongings. The one in the field must not return for what was left. There will be no time for retrieval.

The final exchange is vertiginous. The disciples ask, "Where, Lord?" — meaning, where will this happen, where will the taken and the left be separated? Jesus answers with an image that is deliberately jarring: "Where the body is, there also the vultures will gather." Some translations say eagles, but the word in context points toward scavengers. It is not a comforting image. It refuses to satisfy the question. It is as if Jesus is saying that asking where is the wrong question entirely.

What the readings share: the danger of clinging

These two passages are, on the surface, treating very different problems — false teaching in a community, and the shape of the end times. But underneath them runs the same current: the danger of holding on to what you have instead of remaining in what is true. The Elder's community is tempted to hold on to a version of Jesus that keeps him safely non-physical, non-suffering, non-demanding. The disciples are tempted — as all of us are — to hold on to their arrangements, their accumulated lives, their explanations for how and when and where.

Cabrini, whose feast today gives us permission to hold these readings up to a life, did neither. She crossed oceans, founded institutions, managed crises, and built hospitals — and apparently held none of it too tightly, because she kept going when it failed and kept going when it succeeded.

To carry today

Think of one thing you are holding so carefully that it is using up most of your attention. It may be entirely good and legitimate — a relationship, a plan, a way things have always been done. Ask honestly whether that grip is keeping you from seeing something that is breaking in right now, something you might miss because you are busy tending what you already have.
