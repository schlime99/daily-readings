Feast of

Saints Michael, Gabriel, and Raphael, Archangels

Heaven is not closed off above us; the angels of God are still ascending and descending.

First readingRevelation 12:7-12ab
PsalmPsalm 138:1-2ab, 2cde-3, 4-5
GospelJohn 1:47-51

Today the Church celebrates three archangels together — not as curiosities or as decorative figures from a more credulous age, but as messengers who appear at the hinge points of salvation history. Michael wages war. Gabriel announces. Raphael guides. Their names are practically job descriptions in Hebrew: Michael means "Who is like God?" — a question that doubles as a battle cry — Gabriel means something like "God is my strength," and Raphael means "God heals." The feast is a good occasion to take angels seriously again, and the readings help.

The first reading — Revelation 12:7-12ab

The lectionary offers a choice between Daniel and Revelation today. Daniel's vision of the Ancient One on a throne of fire, with the Son of Man approaching on the clouds, is magnificent — it is the passage Jesus will echo at his trial before the high priest, and it is where the title "Son of Man" gets its cosmic weight. But the Revelation passage is chosen here, and it is startling in its own right.

John's Apocalypse is not a prediction timetable. It is a work of visionary literature written for communities under pressure, full of imagery drawn from the Jewish prophetic tradition, meant to say: what looks like Roman power is not the deepest reality; what looks like defeat is not the final word. Chapter 12 is at the heart of the book. A great battle erupts in heaven — Michael and his angels against the dragon, who is identified explicitly as "the Devil and Satan, the accuser." And the dragon loses. He is thrown down.

The victory announcement that follows is worth sitting with: they conquered him "by the Blood of the Lamb and by the word of their testimony; love for life did not deter them from death." This is not a military victory in any ordinary sense. The martyrs won not by force but by faithfulness — by refusing to deny what they had seen and known, even at the cost of everything. The dragon's power, it turns out, rests entirely on accusation and deception. When the accuser is cast down, the heavens ring with something that sounds almost like relief: Now have salvation and power come.

The psalm picks up the theme perfectly. Psalm 138 is a song of thanksgiving sung, notably, "in the sight of the angels" — in the presence of the heavenly court. The psalmist praises God for hearing, for answering, for building up strength within. There is something quietly fitting about singing this today: the Church joins her voice to what is already going on in heaven.

The Gospel — John 1:47-51

Nathanael's encounter with Jesus at the end of John 1 is one of the Gospel's small, luminous scenes, and it ends in a place most readers don't expect. Philip has found Nathanael and told him about Jesus of Nazareth. Nathanael's response is the immortal line: "Can anything good come from Nazareth?" — which is either a reasonable question about an unimportant village or a piece of regional snobbery, and is probably both.

When he arrives, Jesus greets him with unusual warmth: "Here is a true Israelite in whom there is no duplicity." The word translated "duplicity" calls to mind Jacob, whose name means "the supplanter" and who spent the first part of his life getting what he wanted through cunning. A true child of Israel, Jesus seems to be saying, is the opposite of that: someone without guile, someone who can receive what is given rather than scheming for it. Nathanael is startled — how do you know me? — and Jesus tells him only that he saw him under the fig tree before Philip called him. That is apparently enough. Nathanael's confession comes quickly: "Rabbi, you are the Son of God; you are the King of Israel."

Jesus' response is gentle and a little teasing: you believe because I told you I saw you under the fig tree? Then brace yourself — because you will see heaven opened, and the angels of God ascending and descending on the Son of Man. This is Jacob again, and deliberately so. In Genesis 28, Jacob falls asleep at Bethel and dreams of a staircase between earth and heaven with angels going up and down upon it. Jesus is saying: that ladder is me. I am the place where heaven and earth meet. The angels ascending and descending on me — that is the ongoing reality, not just a dream from long ago.

Where the ladder meets the war

The two readings are not on separate tracks today; they converge at the same point, which is that the angels are active participants in the drama of salvation, and that drama is not abstract. In Revelation, Michael's army fights and the accuser falls. In John, the angels ascend and descend on Jesus — present, moving, carrying what needs to be carried between the visible and the invisible. What the feast asks us to receive is that creation is larger than what we can see, that the heavenly and the earthly are genuinely in contact, and that this contact runs through Christ himself. The war is real and it is already won. The ladder is real and it is already standing.

That is not mythology. It is the Church's conviction about the shape of the cosmos.

To carry today

Think today about what it means that Jesus is the place where heaven and earth touch — not a distant figure requiring elaborate effort to reach, but the very point of contact between the two. Consider where in your day you might actually be met, not by an angel necessarily, but by something that crosses the boundary between what you can manage and what you are given. Stay open to it.
