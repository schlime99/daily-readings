Twenty-first Week in Ordinary Time

Paul works with his hands; Jesus names the hollowness of those who only perform holiness.

First reading2 Thessalonians 3:6-10, 16-18
PsalmPsalm 128:1-2, 4-5
GospelMatthew 23:27-32

These two readings are running on separate tracks today, as they often do in Ordinary Time — one from Paul's letter, one from Matthew's Gospel, each with its own urgency and its own texture. That is worth saying plainly, because the temptation is always to force them into a single theme. The connection here is real but indirect, and it is worth arriving at honestly rather than by shortcut. Both Paul and Jesus are, in their different ways, asking the same underlying question: what does genuine faith actually look like from the outside?

The first reading — 2 Thessalonians 3:6-10, 16-18

Second Thessalonians is a short letter, and this passage comes near its end, where Paul pivots from theology to community conduct. The church in Thessalonica apparently had a problem: some members had stopped working. This was almost certainly tied to an expectation that the Lord was returning at any moment. If the end is near, why tend a garden or stitch leather? Just wait.

Paul's response is bracingly practical, and a little sharp. He points to himself and his companions as the standard: when they were in Thessalonica, they worked. They had what Paul calls "the right" — likely meaning the right to be supported by the community, as a traveling apostle — and they waived it deliberately, so that no one could accuse them of being a burden, and so that their lives would match their preaching. The phrase "toil and drudgery, night and day" is not dramatic exaggeration; Paul supported himself as a tentmaker, working long hours alongside his missionary work. He is not romanticizing manual labor. He is pointing to it as witness.

The famous line — "if anyone was unwilling to work, neither should that one eat" — can sound harsh read in isolation, and it has been put to harsh uses over the centuries. But the context softens it considerably. Paul is addressing people who are perfectly able to work and are choosing idleness on theological grounds. He is not speaking about the sick, the old, or the genuinely destitute. The logic is communal: the body of the church depends on people pulling their share, and faith that uses the promise of resurrection as an excuse to disengage from ordinary responsibility is not faith but evasion.

Psalm 128 sits beautifully beside this. It is a wisdom psalm that blesses the person who fears the Lord — and then immediately ties that blessing to eating "the fruit of your handiwork." The two things are not separated in the psalm's world. Reverence for God and honest daily work belong together; the blessing flows through the labor, not around it. It is a gentle and grounding image, and it gives Paul's practical instruction a deeper root.

The closing verses of the reading shift register entirely, moving into a benediction that is tender and personal: "The grace of our Lord Jesus Christ be with all of you." Paul even notes that the greeting is in his own hand — a signature, a sign of authenticity. After the firm instructions, he ends with warmth. That movement is worth noticing.

The Gospel — Matthew 23:27-32

This is late in Matthew's Gospel — Jesus is in Jerusalem in the final days before his arrest, and the extended passage we call the "woes" against the scribes and Pharisees is one of the most confrontational things he says in the whole book. Today's excerpt gives us two of those woes.

The first image is striking: whitewashed tombs. Jewish law required that tombs be whitewashed before major festivals, partly so that people — especially pilgrims arriving for Passover — would not accidentally touch a tomb and become ritually unclean. A whitewashed tomb was therefore gleaming, conspicuous, and easy to admire from a distance. Jesus takes this ordinary piece of Jerusalem scenery and makes it a mirror. The outside is beautiful. The inside is decay.

The second woe goes a step further and is, in its way, even more unsettling. The Pharisees build memorials to the prophets who were killed by their ancestors, and they say — almost certainly sincerely — that they would never have done what their forebears did. Jesus refuses to accept that self-exculpation. By building the tombs, he says, they are in fact completing the project: enshrining the prophets safely in stone and monument, where they can be honored without being obeyed. The prophets are much easier to admire once they are dead and their demands can be regarded as historical rather than present.

This is a disturbing passage to sit with honestly, because the Pharisees Jesus is addressing are not cynics. They are serious, observant, devout people who believe they are honoring God. The gap between intention and reality in that warning is wide enough to fall into.

The divide between performance and substance

What these readings share, approaching from different directions, is a concern for authenticity — for the match between what a life looks like from the outside and what is actually driving it. Paul addresses those whose idleness is covered by a pious reason. Jesus addresses those whose visible righteousness conceals a deeper refusal. In both cases, the failure is not obvious wickedness but the quieter problem of a life that has learned to project holiness without inhabiting it.

The good news, and there is always good news somewhere in the letters, is that Paul's closing benediction is not addressed to the people who got it right. "The Lord be with all of you" — including the ones he just scolded. The invitation back is already extended before the letter is done.

To carry today

Consider where in your own life you have learned to speak the right language — to say the things that sound faithful — without the inner life to back them up. It is not always hypocrisy in the dramatic sense; sometimes it is just habit, or exhaustion, or a slow drift. Ask today for the grace to close that gap, even one small degree.
