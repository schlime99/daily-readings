Nineteenth Week in Ordinary Time

God acts out the coming disaster in pantomime; a servant receives mercy and immediately refuses to pass it on.

First readingEzekiel 12:1-12
PsalmPsalm 78:56-57, 58-59, 61-62
GospelMatthew 18:21–19:1

Two readings today that share almost nothing on the surface — a prophet performing a strange silent drama in the streets of Babylon, and a king in a parable who forgives an impossible debt only to watch the forgiven man throttle his neighbor. Yet underneath both runs the same cold fact: receiving something you did not earn does not automatically change you. People can watch God's warning right in front of their eyes and still not see. People can be forgiven a fortune and still have the instinct to collect fifty dollars.

The first reading — Ezekiel 12:1-12

Ezekiel is one of the stranger books in the Bible, and that is saying something. He is a priest who was among the first wave of Judeans deported to Babylon in 597 BC, about a decade before Jerusalem actually fell. He writes from exile, to exiles, about a city most of them still hope will survive. His task — and it is an agonizing one — is to tell them: no. The city will fall. The king will flee. What you are hoping for is not coming.

The difficulty is that nobody believes him. They have prophets back in Jerusalem telling them the opposite, and the human instinct is to trust the word that costs you less. So God tells Ezekiel to stop talking and start acting. During the day, he is to pack up his belongings in full view of his neighbors — as a refugee would, bundling what little can be carried. Then in the evening, while people watch, he is to dig through the mud-brick wall of his house with his bare hands, crawl through the hole, cover his face, and walk into the darkness carrying his bundle.

This is what mime looks like in the ancient prophetic tradition, and it is as unsettling as it sounds. The act has a technical name — a "prophetic sign action" — which simply means that what the prophet does is itself the message, not just an illustration of it. Ezekiel is not saying "something like this will happen." He is enacting it in a way that, in the logic of the ancient world, begins to set it in motion.

The covering of the face is particularly striking. It is not so Ezekiel won't be recognized. God explains that the prince of Jerusalem — King Zedekiah — will cover his own face so he cannot see the land he is leaving. There is a grim historical accuracy here that the original hearers would not have known yet: the Book of Kings records that Zedekiah was captured while fleeing, and that the last thing he ever saw before Nebuchadnezzar put out his eyes was the execution of his sons. Whether or not Ezekiel knew this detail, the image of a king who covers his face at departure carries a weight of shame that goes beyond mere disguise.

The psalm is a direct answer to this passage — Psalm 78 holds a long recitation of Israel's infidelities, and the verses chosen today land on the part where God finally did what the prophets warned he would do: "he surrendered his strength into captivity, his glory in the hands of the foe." The refrain is almost wryly appropriate given what follows in the Gospel: Do not forget the works of the Lord. The people of Ezekiel's day forgot. The servant in Matthew's parable forgot.

The Gospel — Matthew 18:21-19:1

Peter's question is actually generous by the standards of his day. Some teachers held that forgiving the same offense three times was sufficient; Peter doubles it and adds one, which feels like a sincere attempt to be magnanimous. Jesus's answer — seventy-seven times, or in some manuscripts seventy times seven — is not meant to be calculated. It is meant to be uncountable. Stop keeping the ledger.

Then comes the parable, and it is one of the most uncomfortable Jesus tells. The first servant owes "a huge amount" — the Greek text specifies ten thousand talents, which ancient listeners would have recognized as a comically, impossibly enormous sum, the kind of number used to indicate the treasury of an empire. It is not a debt a servant could ever owe in any realistic sense; it is a debt designed to make the point that this man has received something that beggars imagination.

He begs. The king is moved with compassion — that word in Matthew nearly always signals something happening at a very deep level, not just a feeling but a movement of the whole person — and cancels the debt entirely. Not restructures it, not reduces it. Gone.

The servant then finds a colleague who owes him a hundred denarii, which is a real debt but a human-sized one, weeks of wages rather than the wealth of kingdoms. And he seizes him by the throat. The contrast is almost darkly comic in its precision: this man has just been released from a debt he could not pay if he worked ten lifetimes, and he cannot wait twenty minutes before demanding repayment of something far smaller.

What follows is not comfortable to sit with. The master's anger, the handing over to the torturers, and Jesus's direct statement that his heavenly Father will do the same — these are not the soft edges of the parable tradition. Jesus is saying that receiving mercy and refusing to extend it is not a small inconsistency. It is a catastrophic failure to understand what has happened to you.

Forgotten mercy, repeated hardness

This is where Ezekiel and Matthew quietly converge, though they arrive from different directions. In both texts, the problem is not ignorance of what God has done — it is failure to be changed by it. Israel had the record of the Exodus, the covenant, the law, the prophets. They had seen. The servant in the parable had experienced cancellation of an impossible debt with his own body, kneeling on the floor, hearing the words. Neither group let what they received land all the way.

The two readings are not really on separate tracks. They are the same track: how is it possible to witness grace and walk away unchanged?

To carry today

Think of the person whose debt to you — some real wrong, some failure, some accumulated disappointment — you have been mentally collecting. You have not necessarily done anything about it, but the ledger is there. Consider that you have been forgiven something far larger, not as a figure of speech, but as the actual arithmetic of your life. Ask whether the size of what you have received has fully registered yet.
