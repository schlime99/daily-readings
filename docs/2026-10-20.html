Twenty-Ninth Week in Ordinary Time

The master comes home and serves the servants who stayed awake for him.

First readingEphesians 2:12-22
PsalmPsalm 85:9-14
GospelLuke 12:35-38

Two things sit side by side today: a grand theological vision of a humanity knit back together by Christ's body, and a brief, almost homely parable of servants waiting up with their lamps lit. The first reading builds a cathedral; the Gospel sets a single candle in the window. Together they are asking the same question — what are you doing with the time between now and when he comes?

The first reading — Ephesians 2:12-22

Ephesians is a letter written to a predominantly Gentile community, and Paul — or a disciple writing in his spirit — is trying to get them to feel the weight of what has happened to them. He wants them to understand where they were standing before Christ found them. The list he offers is bleak: without Christ, alienated from Israel, strangers to the covenants of promise, without hope and without God in the world. That last phrase, "without God in the world," is not an accusation of atheism. It means something closer to unmoored — living in a world that has a God, knowing somewhere that it does, and having no way in, no door, no belonging. The Gentiles were not godless in the sense of not caring; they had plenty of gods. But the living God of Abraham, the God of promise and covenant, was not theirs. They were on the outside looking in.

And then the pivot, one of the great pivots in all of Paul's writing: "But now." Two words, and the whole world tilts. But now in Christ Jesus you who once were far off have become near by the Blood of Christ. What Paul is describing is not a policy change. It is not that the rules were loosened. Something structural collapsed. He uses the image of a dividing wall — scholars connect this to the actual stone barrier in the Jerusalem Temple that separated the Court of the Gentiles from the inner courts, with inscriptions warning that any Gentile who passed beyond it would be killed. Christ has torn that wall down, not by ignoring the law but by fulfilling and completing it in his own body.

What replaces the wall is a new structure entirely. Paul shifts to an architectural image — the whole community, Jewish and Gentile, is now being built into a single temple, with the apostles and prophets as foundation stones and Christ himself as the capstone. In ancient building, the capstone or cornerstone was not decoration; it set the angle and alignment for the whole structure. Everything else was measured against it. Paul is saying that the new humanity has its proportions set by Christ, and every person added to it is being shaped to fit.

Psalm 85 answers this reading almost perfectly. "Kindness and truth shall meet; justice and peace shall kiss." That image of opposites embracing — mercy and truth, justice and peace — is exactly what Paul is describing: two peoples who had no business sharing a table now sharing one body.

The Gospel — Luke 12:35-38

The parable is small and strange and quietly astonishing. Jesus tells his disciples to be like servants waiting for their master to return from a wedding banquet. Keep your lamps lit, have your belts on, be ready to open the door the moment he knocks. Nothing unusual so far — this is what good servants do. But then the story turns in a direction no one in Jesus' audience would have expected. If the master finds his servants alert when he arrives, he will seat them at table and wait on them himself. He will gird himself — put on the servant's belt — and serve the people who have been serving him.

This is not how masters behave. A first-century Palestinian household would not have recognized this as the normal shape of things. The reversal is the whole point. The master who returns is not coming to inspect and judge in the way a difficult employer would; he is coming to honor the ones who loved him enough to wait. The reward for faithful watching is not rest, exactly — it is a banquet, which in the Gospel of Luke is always a sign of the Kingdom, always a foretaste of the great feast to come.

Notice that the parable does not specify what the servants are supposed to do while they wait. They are not given tasks. They are to be ready — awake, dressed, lamp burning. The vigil itself is the virtue.

What a patient God does with a waiting people

These two readings are not on the same track thematically — Ephesians is about the reconciliation of humanity, Luke is about eschatological watchfulness — but they share something underneath: in both, God does the unexpected thing. In Paul, God does not require the outsiders to climb over the wall; he demolishes the wall. In Luke, God does not make the servants earn a reward through performance; he comes home and serves them dinner. In both cases, the movement is from God toward us, and it is larger and more generous than we had any right to expect.

The Ephesians reading tells us what we have been given. The Gospel tells us how to live in light of it: not anxiously, not frantically proving ourselves, but awake and steady, with our lamps burning, confident that the one who broke down every wall between us and God is also the one who will come knocking at the door.

To carry today

Think about where in your life you have been simply waiting — not in the good, attentive way, but in the passive, checked-out way, assuming nothing important is actually coming. Ask whether your lamp is still lit there, and what it would look like to tend it today. The vigil itself is the faithfulness; nothing dramatic is required, only presence.
