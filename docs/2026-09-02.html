Twenty-second Week in Ordinary Time

God causes the growth, and Jesus knows where he must go next — both readings point away from us and toward him.

First reading1 Corinthians 3:1-9
PsalmPsalm 33:12-13, 14-15, 20-21
GospelLuke 4:38-44

There is a quiet humility running through both readings today, though it arrives from very different directions. Paul is scolding a community that has made celebrities of its preachers. Jesus slips away from a crowd that wants to keep him. In each case, the lesson is the same: the point is not the minister, and it is not even the miracle. The point is the one who sends.

The first reading — 1 Corinthians 3:1-9

By the time Paul writes this letter, the church at Corinth has managed to turn the gospel into a spectator sport. Factions have formed around favorite teachers — Paul on one side, Apollos on the other — and the community is loud with the kind of rivalry that masquerades as loyalty. Paul's response is not diplomatic. He tells them flatly that this is the behavior of infants, and that he cannot feed them solid food because they are still living on milk.

It helps to know where this sits in the letter. In the chapters just before, Paul has been making the case that God's wisdom looks like foolishness to the world — that the cross is the center, not the eloquence of the preacher. Now he brings it down to earth. All the quarreling over which teacher is greater simply proves that the Corinthians have missed the point entirely. The preacher is not the source of anything.

Then comes one of the most beautiful images in Paul's letters: "I planted, Apollos watered, but God caused the growth." It is a farming image, and it is wonderfully deflating. A farmer who plants a seed does something real and laborious and necessary. The one who comes to water does the same. But neither of them makes the thing grow. That is entirely beyond their power. All they can do is set the conditions. The life itself comes from somewhere else.

Paul is not dismissing human effort — he goes on to say each worker will receive wages in proportion to their labor. But there is a clear order: the workers are real, their work matters, and they are still, finally, nothing compared to the one who makes things live. "We are God's co-workers," he writes, and that phrase is worth sitting with. Co-workers, not executives. Partners in a project whose outcome belongs to God.

The psalm echoes this beautifully. "Our soul waits for the LORD, who is our help and our shield." The people of God are not self-sufficient; they are those who have been chosen and who know it. From heaven God looks down and "fashions the heart of each." The psalm does not find this surveillance alarming — it finds it comforting. To be fully known by the one who causes all growth is not a threat; it is the ground of trust.

The Gospel — Luke 4:38-44

This passage comes from very early in Luke's account of Jesus's public ministry. He has just come from the synagogue in Capernaum, where he taught and drove out a demon, and the whole town is already buzzing. He walks into Simon Peter's house, finds his mother-in-law laid flat with a serious fever, and simply rebukes the fever — the same word Luke just used for the demon — and it leaves her. She gets up and serves them. Luke moves on almost without pausing.

Then the sun goes down. In Jewish reckoning this begins a new day, and more practically it ends the Sabbath, so what had been prohibited — carrying the sick, traveling to find a healer — is now permitted. Immediately, the whole town appears at the door. Luke's detail is tender: Jesus laid his hands on each one of them. Not a mass healing, not a gesture toward the crowd, but individual attention for every person who came.

The demons who come out are loud about who he is: "You are the Son of God." Jesus silences them. This is sometimes called the messianic secret, and it runs through the early chapters of Luke's Gospel — Jesus is not interested in being defined by dramatic supernatural proclamation. What the demons know is true, but it is not yet the right moment, and a demon is not the right messenger.

Then, before dawn, Jesus leaves. He goes to a deserted place, the way he often does in Luke when the demands of the crowd have been intense. When the people find him and try to keep him with them — which is a human and understandable impulse, to want to hold onto something good — he tells them he cannot stay. "For this purpose I have been sent." Not for Capernaum alone. Not for the people who have already heard. The mission is wider than any single town's desire to keep their healer local.

The movement away from self as the source

These two readings are on their own tracks — Paul's pastoral crisis in Corinth has nothing to do with a Galilean fishing village — but they arrive at something genuinely similar. Paul strips the teachers of their false importance so that God can be the one who matters. Jesus refuses to be contained by the crowd's enthusiasm so that the Kingdom can move where it must. In both cases, something that looks like loss — the preacher's prestige, the crowd's satisfaction — turns out to be the condition for something larger.

It is worth noticing that neither Paul nor Jesus is being cold. Paul's rebuke comes from someone who loves the Corinthians and wants them to grow up into freedom. Jesus heals every single person who comes to him before he leaves. The redirection is not indifference. It is faithfulness to something bigger than the moment.

To carry today

Think about where you are most tempted to make the minister the point — the preacher you follow, the parish you prefer, the approach to faith that feels like your brand. Ask whether the loyalty is to the instrument or to the one who actually causes the growth. That distinction might matter more than it seems.
