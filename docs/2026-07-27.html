Seventeenth Week in Ordinary Time

A rotted loincloth and a mustard seed: God works in the concrete and the small.

First readingJeremiah 13:1-11
PsalmDeuteronomy 32:18-21
GospelMatthew 13:31-35

Today's readings are, on the surface, doing two very different things. Jeremiah is staging a grim object lesson about a nation that has drifted away from God, while Matthew's Jesus is sketching the Kingdom in quick, almost playful images. These two are largely on separate tracks — the prophet's grief and the parables' quiet confidence belong to different moments in salvation history. But there is a thread worth noticing, not forced but real: God keeps working through the ordinary and the material, through cloth and seeds and flour, refusing to stay abstract.

The first reading — Jeremiah 13:1-11

We are early in the book of Jeremiah, in the section scholars call his "symbolic actions" — moments when the prophet is asked to do something strange in public, or in private, as a kind of living parable. He has already smashed a clay pot at the city gate. Now he is told to buy a linen loincloth, wear it for a time without washing it, then walk to the Parath and bury it in a rock cleft. He waits. He goes back. He digs it up. It is ruined, rotted through, completely useless.

The word translated "loincloth" here is important: this is not a belt or a sash but an undergarment, the piece of clothing worn closest to the body. When God says that Israel and Judah were meant to cling to him the way this garment clings to a man's body, the image is intimate to the point of being almost startling. This is not the language of alliance or covenant obligation in its legal sense — it is the language of closeness, of belonging, of being bound to another by habit and proximity and daily life. They were supposed to be his people, his renown, his praise, his beauty. Those are words of pride, even of delight.

And then they did not listen. The loincloth, buried in the earth and left there through neglect, rots. It becomes good for nothing. That, says God through Jeremiah, is what has happened to the people who were meant to wear his name.

The responsorial psalm is drawn from the Song of Moses in Deuteronomy — one of the oldest poems in the Hebrew Bible — and it answers Jeremiah with almost the same ache. "You were unmindful of the Rock that begot you, you forgot the God who gave you birth." The word "forgot" is doing heavy lifting there. This is not the forgetting of ignorance; it is the forgetting that comes from distraction, from slowly looking elsewhere until the original face has gone dim.

There is something almost darkly comic about God's response in the psalm — "I will provoke them with a 'no-people'; with a foolish nation I will anger them." God will use their own method against them: they have given their devotion to a "no-god," to idols that are hollow, and so he will stir their jealousy through a people they would not bother to notice. Even the divine grief has a kind of exasperated wit to it.

The Gospel — Matthew 13:31-35

Matthew 13 is the great parable chapter, and we have been moving through it for several days now. Jesus has already explained the sower and the wheat and the weeds. Now he offers two shorter parables, each barely a sentence long, and each turning on something almost laughably small becoming inexplicably large.

The mustard seed was proverbial in Jewish teaching as the smallest measurable thing — so small it was nearly invisible when held in the palm. Yet in the right conditions it grows into a plant large enough for birds to nest in. The yeast parable works the same way from the opposite direction: a woman folds a small amount of leaven into a large amount of flour — three measures is closer to fifty pounds than to a dinner quantity — and it works through the whole batch invisibly, from within.

Matthew tells us that Jesus spoke only in parables to the crowds, and he ties this to Psalm 78: "I will open my mouth in parables, I will announce what has lain hidden from the foundation of the world." There is something Jesus wants to say about the Kingdom that cannot be said directly, only shown sideways, through images that do their work slowly, the way yeast does.

Where smallness is the point

The connection worth sitting with today is this: both readings take God's activity seriously in the particular and the physical. Jeremiah is not given a vision or a dream; he is told to walk to the market, buy cloth, wear it, and get his hands dirty in a rock cleft beside the river. Jesus does not describe the Kingdom as a theological category; he points to a seed and a woman at work in her kitchen.

The loincloth and the mustard seed are not decorations hung on an idea. They are the idea. God works in the material world, through the concrete, the daily, the embarrassingly small. The tragedy in Jeremiah is that Israel looked past the tangible gifts — the land, the law, the daily provision — toward the spectacle of the idols. The hope in Matthew is that the Kingdom is already at work in what looks like nothing, doing what yeast does: changing everything from inside.

To carry today

Consider where in your own life you are waiting for the large and obvious sign while something small has already been placed in front of you — a returning habit of prayer, a small act of patience, an almost-invisible change in how you treat someone. Ask whether you have the patience to let the hidden thing do its quiet work.
