Twenty-second Week in Ordinary Time

Jesus unrolls the scroll in his hometown — and the welcome turns murderous before he leaves.

First reading1 Corinthians 2:1-5
PsalmPsalm 119:97-102
GospelLuke 4:16-30

There is something almost unbearable about the scene in Nazareth. The hometown crowd leans forward, charmed and curious, ready to be proud. And then, within the space of a few sentences, they are dragging him toward a cliff. Luke tells this story with startling speed, and the speed is part of the point: recognition and rejection are closer together than we think.

The first reading — 1 Corinthians 2:1-5

Paul is writing to Corinth, a city that prized eloquence the way a modern city might prize credentials. Corinth was a cosmopolitan port, and its intellectual culture admired the polished rhetor — the speaker who could argue a case with dazzling skill. When Paul says he came "not with sublimity of words or of wisdom," he is deliberately refusing to play that game. This is not false modesty from a man who was, by any measure, a formidable thinker and writer. It is a principled choice.

He narrows everything down to one thing: "Jesus Christ, and him crucified." The crucifixion was not a selling point in the ancient world. It was a shameful death for criminals and slaves, and claiming that the crucified one was Lord was, as Paul says elsewhere, foolishness to the Greeks and a scandal to Jewish ears. Paul knows this. He is not softening the message to make it easier. He is presenting the starkest possible version of it, and then stepping back and watching what the Spirit does.

The phrase "weakness and fear and much trembling" is easy to skim over, but worth pausing on. Paul is not performing humility for effect. He is admitting that he was genuinely afraid — afraid, presumably, that the message would not land, that the foolishness of the cross would simply look like foolishness and nothing more. His trust was not in his own confidence. It was in the Spirit moving through the weakness.

Psalm 119 sits a little apart from this, running on its own course. It is the great psalm of the Torah, the longest chapter in the entire Bible, a poem so in love with God's instruction that it returns to the theme from one hundred and seventy-six different angles. The verses today are about the wisdom that comes from attending to the law — a wisdom that surpasses what enemies plot, what teachers explain, what elders have accumulated. The connection to Paul is oblique but real: both are pointing to a source of knowledge that is not human strategy or human eloquence. The law, the Spirit, the cross — none of these are what the world would have designed as a winning argument. And yet.

The Gospel — Luke 4:16-30

Luke places this scene at the very opening of Jesus's public ministry — it is almost the first thing he does after returning from the desert. He goes home. He goes to synagogue, as he always has, and he is handed the scroll of Isaiah. What he reads is the passage we now call Isaiah 61: the anointed one sent to the poor, the captives, the blind, the oppressed. He rolls the scroll back up, sits down — sitting was the posture of a teacher — and says simply: "Today this Scripture passage is fulfilled in your hearing."

The crowd's first reaction is wonder. "Gracious words" — beautiful, astonishing words. They are impressed. But then comes the small, deflating thought: "Is this not the son of Joseph?" They know him. They have watched him grow up. And familiarity, Luke shows us, is a particular enemy of faith. Not contempt exactly, but a kind of comfortable disbelief: surely we would know if something extraordinary had happened here.

Jesus does not soften the confrontation. He names what they are thinking before they can put it into words, and then he does something remarkable: he tells them that Elijah was sent to a foreign widow in Sidon, and Elisha healed a foreign general named Naaman, when there were plenty of Israelites who needed help. This is not incidental history. Jesus is telling his neighbors that the mercy of God has never been the exclusive property of those who thought they had the best claim to it. Outsiders received what the insiders passed over. The crowd understands exactly what he is saying, which is why the fury is so immediate and so violent.

The moment familiarity closes the door

What connects Paul's letter and Luke's scene is the question of what gets in the way of receiving what God is actually offering. For the Corinthians, it was the desire for a more impressive package — better rhetoric, more polished wisdom, something they could admire on its own human terms. For Nazareth, it was the opposite problem: too much familiarity, a sense of already knowing what to expect from this particular person. Both are ways of deciding in advance what counts, and missing the thing itself as a result.

Paul stripped away everything except the essential claim. Jesus, at the synagogue, makes the essential claim plainly and refuses to walk it back when the room turns. In both cases, the invitation stands whether or not the room receives it well.

To carry today

Think today about where long familiarity has quietly flattened something that should still surprise you — a prayer you have said a thousand times, a passage of Scripture you think you already know, a relationship where you have stopped expecting to be changed. Ask for the grace of actually hearing something as if it were new, just once, and see what it costs you.
