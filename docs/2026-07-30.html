Seventeenth Week in Ordinary Time

The potter does not discard the clay — he works it again, into what he pleases.

First readingJeremiah 18:1-6
PsalmPsalm 146:1-6
GospelMatthew 13:47-53

There is a theme running through today's two readings that you could call patience, but that word is too thin. What the potter and the fisherman both embody is something closer to an unhurried authority — a willingness to work all the way to the end before declaring what anything is. That is a difficult posture to trust from the inside of the clay, or from inside the net. But it is the posture these readings ask us to consider.

The first reading — Jeremiah 18:1-6

Jeremiah is writing from one of the darkest periods in Israel's history — the decades before the Babylonian exile, when the kingdom is rotting from within and the prophets who say so are, for their trouble, threatened and thrown in cisterns. God has been sending Jeremiah word after word of warning, and the people have largely not listened. Into this grinding situation comes one of Scripture's most vivid domestic images: go down to the potter's house.

So Jeremiah goes. And what he sees is utterly ordinary — a craftsman at his wheel, hands wet with clay, shaping something. And then the clay goes wrong. It collapses or warps or simply refuses the shape the potter intended. And without dramatics, without discarding the lump and starting fresh with new material, the potter simply tries again. Same clay, different form, same patient hands.

The word God gives Jeremiah out of this scene is worth sitting with. It is not a threat, exactly, though it has been read as one. "Can I not do to you, house of Israel, as this potter has done?" The question is rhetorical, but it cuts both ways. Yes, God has the authority of the potter over the clay. That is real and should not be softened. But the image also contains mercy: the potter does not throw the clay away. He reworks it. The clay that went wrong is still in his hands. He is still at the wheel.

Psalm 146 answers this reading by naming where reliable help actually comes from. The psalm has a gentle edge of warning — do not trust princes, because even the best of them die and their plans die with them — but its real motion is toward praise. Blessed is the one whose hope is in the God of Jacob: the God who made everything, who keeps faith forever. That "keeps faith forever" lands differently after Jeremiah's potter. The hands at the wheel do not tire.

The Gospel — Matthew 13:47-53

This is the last in a string of parables Matthew has been running through chapter 13 — the sower, the wheat and weeds, the mustard seed, the yeast, the hidden treasure, the pearl of great price. The net parable is in some ways the simplest: a dragnet is thrown into the sea, it pulls in everything, and only at the shore is the sorting done.

What the parable insists on is the timing. The separation does not happen in the water. The net does not discriminate while it is being dragged; it catches every kind. The judgment — what is good, what is thrown away — happens at the end, on the shore, when the work is finished. Jesus is explicit that this is a picture of the end of the age, not a picture of human sorting in the present.

The brief exchange that follows is easy to rush past. Jesus asks, "Do you understand all these things?" and the disciples say yes — which, given how consistently confused they have been throughout this gospel, is a little bit funny and a little bit touching. Jesus does not contradict them. Instead he gives them one more image: the scribe who has been instructed in the Kingdom of heaven is like a householder who brings from the storeroom both the new and the old. Something is being passed to them, something they are now responsible for carrying forward.

The patience that runs through both readings

These two passages are on separate tracks in the sense that Jeremiah and Matthew have nothing directly to do with each other. But they are pressing on the same question from different sides: when does the final shape of things become clear?

Jeremiah's potter answers: not while the clay is still wet. You do not know what the vessel will be until the hands have finished their work. The net parable answers: not while everything is still in the water. The sorting is real, but it belongs to the end, not the middle. Both images place the reader — place us — firmly in the middle, and both ask for a trust that is not the same as passivity. The clay does not resist the hands. The fish do not try to sort themselves.

There is something genuinely consoling here for anyone who is in a season of waiting or confusion or incompleteness, and also something genuinely sobering for anyone who has decided the work is finished and the sorting already done. The potter is still at the wheel. The net is still being dragged. The end has not yet come to the shore.

To carry today

Think of somewhere in your life where you have decided a person — or a situation, or yourself — is finished goods, already sorted, beyond further working. Ask whether that judgment is yours to make, or whether the potter is simply not done yet. Hold a little looseness there today, and see what that opens up.
