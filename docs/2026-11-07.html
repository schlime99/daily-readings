Thirty-First Week in Ordinary Time

Paul has learned the secret of enough; Jesus asks whether we can be trusted with what is not ours.

First readingPhilippians 4:10-19
PsalmPsalm 112:1b-2, 5-6, 8a, 9
GospelLuke 16:9-15

Money is an uncomfortable subject, and both readings today lean into that discomfort without apology. What saves them from being merely a lecture on generosity is what each one does with the question underneath the question — not "how much should I give?" but "what does the way I handle money reveal about where my heart actually lives?"

The first reading — Philippians 4:10-19

Paul is writing from prison. The letter to the Philippians is late in his life, almost certainly composed during a Roman captivity, and by the time we reach chapter four we are at the warmest and most personal section of the whole letter. This is Paul at his most human. The Philippians had sent him a gift through a man named Epaphroditus — money, probably, and perhaps supplies — and Paul is writing to thank them. What strikes any careful reader immediately is how delicately he goes about it. He says outright that he is not writing out of need, that he has "learned the secret of being well fed and of going hungry." He doesn't want them to think he was sitting in his cell anxious about their generosity or annoyed at the delay. The word translated as "self-sufficient" here is a Greek term the Stoic philosophers loved — it meant a certain inner serenity, an independence from external circumstance. Paul borrows that honorable word and fills it with something the Stoics never meant: I have the strength for everything through him who empowers me. His contentment is not stoic detachment; it is a trust grounded entirely in someone outside himself.

Then, having established that he doesn't need the gift, he makes clear why it matters anyway. The profit, he says, "accrues to your account" — a deliberately commercial image, almost funny in its bookkeeping precision. He is essentially telling them that generosity is not just an act of charity toward the recipient; it is something that shapes the giver's own soul, an investment in something that will endure. He calls their gift "a fragrant aroma, an acceptable sacrifice, pleasing to God." That language comes straight from the Temple offering rites — the same phrase used in the Law to describe a sacrifice that rises to God. Their ordinary, slightly awkward financial gift has become, in Paul's reading of it, a liturgical act.

Psalm 112 answers this directly. The person who "lavishly gives to the poor" and "conducts his affairs with justice" is the one whose heart is steadfast, unafraid. The psalm makes the same connection Paul does: a certain freedom from anxiety is the fruit of a certain freedom with money.

The Gospel — Luke 16:9-15

Luke 16 is one of the stranger chapters in the New Testament. It opens with the parable of the dishonest steward — a manager who, facing dismissal, reduces his master's debtors' bills to win their favor — and Jesus holds that cunning up as a model of shrewd, forward-looking action. The disciples are then told: "Make friends for yourselves with dishonest wealth, so that when it fails, you will be welcomed into eternal dwellings." What Jesus seems to mean is that money, being a temporary and somewhat unclean instrument of a fallen world ("dishonest wealth" is literally mammon of unrighteousness), should be used in ways that forge relationships and do good — before it disappears, as it inevitably will.

Then Jesus drives the logic further: trustworthiness is a single character trait. Someone who is honest in small transactions and someone who handles great responsibilities faithfully are the same person. The reverse is equally true. The way a person relates to money — which Jesus calls "what belongs to another," since we are stewards of it, not owners — reveals the kind of person they actually are, and therefore whether they can be entrusted with what is "true wealth," meaning whatever has genuine and lasting value.

The Pharisees sneer. Luke tells us plainly that they loved money, so this teaching cuts close, and sneering is what people do when something hits its mark and they cannot respond to it honestly. Jesus looks at them and says something remarkable: "What is of human esteem is an abomination in the sight of God." The outward reputation for financial responsibility or piety or respectability counts for nothing if the heart underneath it is arranged around money rather than around God.

What trustworthiness reveals

The connection between Paul and Luke today is not about giving per se. It is about what a person's relationship to money discloses about the architecture of their inner life. Paul has arranged his life around Christ, and the result is a strange freedom — he can be poor, he can be rich, he can be in prison, and none of it moves him. The Pharisees have arranged their lives around wealth and esteem, and the result is a heart that cannot be known, only performed. Jesus is asking his disciples — and Luke is asking his readers — to notice which of those they are becoming.

The two passages are simply running on parallel tracks today, as often happens on weekday mornings in Ordinary Time. But they happen to arrive at the same place: money is a small thing, Jesus says. The question is whether small things make you smaller or free you to be larger.

To carry today

Think today about one area where you manage money quietly and without much reflection — where it flows out through habit, without examination. Ask honestly whether that habit is shaping you toward generosity and freedom, or toward a tighter, more anxious version of yourself. You don't have to fix anything today; just look at it clearly, the way you'd want to see it.
