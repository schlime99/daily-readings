Memorial of

Saint Clare, Virgin

God feeds the prophet with words that taste of honey, then sends him to look for the one lost sheep.

First readingEzekiel 2:8–3:4
PsalmPsalm 119:14, 24, 72, 103, 111, 131
GospelMatthew 18:1–5, 10, 12–14

There is a through-line running from Ezekiel's strange vision to Jesus' parable of the shepherd, and it is not immediately obvious — but it is real. God gives a word that is genuinely sweet, and then sends the recipient straight toward the difficult work of finding what is lost. The sweetness is not the end of the story; it is what makes the hard errand bearable.

The first reading — Ezekiel 2:8–3:4

Ezekiel is one of the stranger books in the Old Testament, and this passage comes right at its beginning. The prophet has just received an overwhelming vision of God's glory — wheels within wheels, living creatures, fire — and now God addresses him directly and gives him his commission. The instruction is startling: eat this scroll. A hand reaches out holding a written scroll, unrolled so Ezekiel can see it is covered on both sides. What is written on it? Lamentation, wailing, and woe. This is not light reading. The contents of the scroll are grief — the grief of a people who have broken covenant, and the grief of the God who will not simply ignore what is happening. And Ezekiel is told to eat it.

This image is worth sitting with. The word God gives his prophet is not a pleasant assignment: go tell a rebellious people difficult things. But when Ezekiel actually eats the scroll, it is sweet — as sweet as honey. This is not a contradiction so much as a paradox that the tradition has long found precious. The word of God, even the word of judgment, even the word of lamentation, has a goodness at its center. It is real. It is not flattery. It does not tell us what we wish were true. And yet it comes from the mouth of the One who made us, and there is something in the human soul that recognizes it as nourishment when it arrives.

The psalm today picks up this exact image without missing a beat. Psalm 119 — the great long meditation on God's law — sings in verse after verse that the decrees of God are sweeter than honey. "How sweet to my palate are your promises," the psalmist says, and then adds the wonderful image: "I gasp with open mouth, in my yearning for your commands." The psalmist is not describing a mild preference; he is describing hunger. Ezekiel eats the scroll because God tells him to, and the taste surprises him. The psalmist has been eating it long enough to know the taste and to want more.

The Gospel — Matthew 18:1–5, 10, 12–14

This passage sits near the beginning of what scholars call Matthew's "community discourse" — Jesus' extended teaching about how his followers are to treat one another. The disciples have just been arguing, in their thoroughly human way, about who among them is the greatest. Jesus responds not with a speech but with a gesture: he calls a child over and places the child in the middle of the group.

In Jesus' world, children had no social standing. They were not idealized as sources of wisdom or innocence — they were simply small and dependent and not yet counted for much. When Jesus says that whoever becomes humble like this child is the greatest in the kingdom, he is not saying "be innocent" or "be trusting" in some vague spiritual sense. He is saying: be someone who does not demand recognition, who has no stake in the rankings, who simply receives what is given. That is the posture of greatness in his kingdom — and the disciples, still jockeying for position, are being quietly shown that they have it backward.

Then the parable of the lost sheep follows, and it makes the same point from a different angle. The ninety-nine who did not stray are fine. The one who is lost is the one the shepherd goes after. And this, Jesus says plainly, is the will of his Father: not one of these little ones should be lost. The "little ones" here are the vulnerable, the unimportant, the ones nobody is counting — exactly the child he just set in the middle of the room. God's attention moves toward them, not away.

The word that sends you looking

Here is where the two readings find each other. Ezekiel eats a scroll of lamentation and goes to speak to a rebellious house — not a welcoming audience, not a pleasant errand. The shepherd leaves ninety-nine sheep to go into the hills after the one who wandered. Both images are about someone who receives a word from God, however difficult, and then moves toward the lost rather than the comfortable.

Clare of Assisi, whose memorial the Church keeps today, knew this posture well. She heard the word — partly through Francis, partly through her own encounter with the gospel — and spent her life choosing the small, the poor, and the forgotten. The scroll tasted sweet enough that she could bear what following it cost her. That is the shape of today as a whole: a sweetness that commissions, a word that sends.

To carry today

Think of someone in your life who feels genuinely overlooked — not dramatically lost, just quietly on the edges of things, easy to forget about. Consider what it would take to move toward them today, even in a small way. The shepherd's joy when he finds the stray is not incidental to the story; it is the whole point. Ask whether that kind of joy is something you have let yourself want.
