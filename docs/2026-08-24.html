Monday · 24 August

Feast of

Saint Bartholomew, Apostle

A man sitting under a fig tree becomes a foundation stone of the holy city.

First readingRevelation 21:9b-14
PsalmPsalm 145:10-11, 12-13, 17-18
GospelJohn 1:45-51

Today the Church celebrates Bartholomew, one of the Twelve, a man about whom we know almost nothing — and yet whose name is carved, according to the book of Revelation, into the very foundation of the eternal Jerusalem. That tension is the whole feast: obscurity on earth, permanence in heaven. The readings hold that together beautifully, and they do it by starting at the end and working backward.

The first reading — Revelation 21:9b-14

We are near the very close of the New Testament, in the final chapters of Revelation, where the seer John is being shown the new Jerusalem descending from heaven. An angel has offered to show him "the bride, the wife of the Lamb" — and what John sees is a city. This is not a confusion; it is the point. The Church, the gathered people of God throughout all time, is the bride, and her form is a city of astonishing symmetry and light.

The city gleams "with the splendor of God," and John reaches for the most radiant image he knows: jasper, clear as crystal. Then he turns to the architecture. Twelve gates, one for each tribe of Israel. And twelve foundation stones, one for each apostle. The geometry is deliberate. The number twelve in Jewish and early Christian thought signals completeness, the whole people of God — twelve tribes, twelve apostles, twelve as the shape of the community that belongs to God. The city is not built despite Israel's history and the apostles' witness; it is built from them. They are the stones everything else rests on.

Which means Bartholomew's name is literally in the foundation. The man we can barely find in the Gospels — listed in the apostolic rolls, sometimes identified with Nathanael, otherwise nearly silent — has his name inscribed in the city that gleams like crystal in eternity. The psalm the liturgy pairs with this catches exactly that note: "Your friends make known, O Lord, the glorious splendor of your Kingdom." The word friends is doing real work here. These are not administrators or executives of the Kingdom; they are friends, and their making-known is the whole point of an apostle's life.

The Gospel — John 1:45-51

The Church's tradition identifies Bartholomew with Nathanael, the man Philip goes to fetch in this passage from the opening chapter of John's Gospel. Whether or not that identification is certain, the scene fits the feast perfectly, and the liturgy has placed it here with good reason.

Philip arrives bursting with news: we have found the one Moses and the prophets wrote about. And Nathanael — characteristically blunt — says, "Can anything good come from Nazareth?" It is a small-town prejudice, a reasonable skepticism, the voice of someone who has probably heard excited announcements before and been disappointed. Philip does not argue. He says, "Come and see." That is still, after two thousand years, the only real answer to that kind of doubt.

What happens next is one of the strangest small moments in the Gospels. Jesus sees Nathanael coming and announces, before the man has said a word, that here is "a true child of Israel" in whom there is "no duplicity." Nathanael, understandably startled, asks how Jesus could possibly know him. And Jesus says: I saw you under the fig tree, before Philip called you. We do not know what Nathanael was doing under the fig tree — praying, reading, thinking, grieving, simply resting in the shade. We do not need to know. The point is that Jesus saw him there, in a private, unobserved moment, and Nathanael knows it. That is enough. "Rabbi, you are the Son of God; you are the King of Israel." The confession pours out of him.

Jesus gently pushes back on what seems like sudden, easy faith: do you believe only because I told you about the fig tree? You will see far greater things. And then, borrowing Jacob's dream of the ladder from Genesis, Jesus says the angels will be ascending and descending on him — the Son of Man is the ladder, the place where heaven and earth meet.

The foundation beneath the obscure

What the two readings share is a conviction about what God does with ordinary, unnamed, unrecorded lives. Nathanael sits under a fig tree, unseen by anyone he knows. He is nobody in particular. And Jesus has already seen him, already known him, already claimed him. The Revelation passage then shows us where that claim ends up: in a name on a foundation stone, in a city that gleams with the splendor of God forever.

The Twelve were not chosen because they were exceptional. The Gospels make this almost embarrassingly clear — they misunderstand, they argue about rank, they fall asleep, they run away. But they were called, and they came, and their names are in the foundation. Bartholomew, who left almost no trace in Scripture, is as much in that foundation as anyone. The splendor of the Kingdom is made known by friends, even quiet ones, even the ones history barely remembers.

To carry today

Think of the work you do that nobody sees or records — the patience, the faithfulness, the small acts of showing up. Consider that being unseen by the people around you is not the same as being unseen. Ask for the grace to go on doing the thing faithfully, without needing it to be noticed, because it turns out that is how foundation stones are made.
