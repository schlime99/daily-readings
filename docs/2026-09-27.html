Twenty-sixth Sunday in Ordinary Time

God's door stays open longer than we expect — and the way in is a change of mind.

First readingEzekiel 18:25-28
PsalmPsalm 25:4-5, 6-7, 8-9
Second readingPhilippians 2:1-11
GospelMatthew 21:28-32

There is a particular kind of pride that disguises itself as principle. It says, "I know where I stand," and it means, "I will not move." Today's readings examine that stance with unusual directness and then describe, without a shred of sentimentality, what it costs.

The first reading — Ezekiel 18:25-28

Ezekiel is writing during the Babylonian exile, which means he is writing to people who have already suffered catastrophe and are now in the business of explaining it. The explanation many of them landed on was that they were suffering for the sins of their ancestors — an idea that felt both theologically tidy and personally reassuring, since it placed the blame firmly in the past. Ezekiel's entire eighteenth chapter is a long, patient dismantling of that position. God, through the prophet, insists that each person stands before him on her own account. What you did last year matters. What you do today matters more.

The passage we hear this morning catches the argument at its sharpest. The people are grumbling that God's way is not fair — which, when you think about it, is a bold complaint to level at the creator of the universe. God's response is not defensive but pointed: Is it my way that is unfair, or rather, are not your ways unfair? The question is rhetorical, but it is also genuinely worth sitting with. Then Ezekiel lays out the logic. A righteous person who turns to wickedness will die for it. But a wicked person who turns away from wickedness and does what is right — that person will live. Not survive in some narrow sense, but live, in the fullest sense Ezekiel can name.

What is striking here is the direction of God's interest. The passage could have been written to comfort the complacent ("don't worry, your past virtue covers you"). Instead it is written to reach the despairing — the person who assumes the ledger is closed, that what they have done has settled the matter. It has not. The turn is still possible. Psalm 25 answers this directly, in the voice of someone who has actually made that turn: The sins of my youth and my frailties remember not; in your kindness remember me. The psalmist is not asking God to look the other way. He is asking God to look at him as he is now, changed.

The second reading — Philippians 2:1-11

Paul's letter to the Philippians is one of his warmest, written from prison to a community he genuinely loves, and this passage is among the most theologically dense things he ever committed to parchment. The first few verses are practical and communal — he is urging the Philippians to stop competing with each other, to set aside their selfishness and vainglory, and to regard others as more important than themselves. This is not easy advice, and Paul does not pretend it is. But then, to back up the ask, he reaches for the largest possible example.

Christ, Paul writes, was in the form of God — and did not treat that as something to hold onto. He emptied himself. He took the form of a slave. He became obedient to death, and not a clean or dignified death, but death on a cross. And then — because this is the shape of the gospel — God exalted him above every name that can be named. The pattern Paul is describing is not just a moral example to imitate. It is the deepest structure of reality as Christianity understands it: the one who empties himself is the one who is filled; the one who descends is the one who rises; the one who serves is the one who, in the end, every knee bends before.

This passage runs on its own track from Ezekiel and Matthew in some ways — Paul is not talking directly about repentance or vineyard workers. But it sits underneath everything else today like a foundation, because the change of mind and heart that Ezekiel demands and Jesus describes in the parable requires exactly this: letting go of your own position, your own estimate of yourself, your own grip on being right.

The Gospel — Matthew 21:28-32

This parable is told inside the Temple, in the final week before the crucifixion, in the middle of a running argument between Jesus and the chief priests and elders about authority and legitimacy. The parable is brief enough to land like a door closing. A father asks two sons to work in the vineyard. The first refuses, then changes his mind and goes. The second agrees cheerfully and does not go. Which one did the father's will? The chief priests answer correctly: the first. And then Jesus turns the parable directly against them.

Tax collectors and prostitutes — people the religious establishment had written off as congenitally disqualified — are entering the kingdom ahead of them. John the Baptist came preaching, and the people everyone had given up on heard him and turned. The religious leaders did not. And here is the knife edge: even when you saw that, you did not later change your minds and believe him. They watched the transformation of the wrong people and remained entirely unmoved. Their position was too settled to disturb.

What repentance looks like from the outside

What connects Ezekiel and Matthew is a specific claim about how moral and spiritual life actually works: it is not a balance sheet tallied at birth, and it is not a performance you either nail or don't. It is a living thing, capable of turning. The first son is not celebrated because he said the right words or came from the right family. He is celebrated because he went. The wicked person in Ezekiel is not condemned forever once she has turned. She lives.

What the religious leaders in the parable missed — and what Ezekiel's grumbling exiles missed — was that God's fairness and God's mercy are not in tension. The door staying open is not unfairness to those who walked in early. It is the same door. The question is only whether you are willing to walk through it.

To carry today

Think of a position you have held for a long time — about yourself, about someone else, about what you are capable of or what you deserve. Ask honestly whether that position is still serving you or whether you are simply protecting it because changing your mind feels like defeat. The first son's turn was not a humiliation; it was the whole point.
