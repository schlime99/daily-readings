Memorial of

Saint Gregory the Great, Pope and Doctor of the Church

A fisherman falls to his knees in a sinking boat and is told not to be afraid.

First reading1 Corinthians 3:18-23
PsalmPsalm 24:1-6
GospelLuke 5:1-11

Gregory the Great did not want to be pope. He was a monk, and he loved the cloister, and when the people of Rome acclaimed him bishop in 590 he reportedly tried to escape the city in a basket. He failed, of course — and then he became one of the most consequential figures the Church has ever produced. Today's readings were not chosen with Gregory specifically in mind; the weekday cycle runs on its own track, and the feast simply falls where it falls. But there is something quietly fitting about celebrating a man who knew his own inadequacy while reading Paul's words about the foolishness of wisdom and Luke's story of a fisherman who knew, terrifyingly and precisely, that he was not the right man for this.

The first reading — 1 Corinthians 3:18-23

We are in the third chapter of First Corinthians, early in the letter, and Paul is dealing with a messy situation in a young church that has started splitting into factions. The Corinthians were lining up behind their preferred teachers — Paul, or Apollos, or Cephas — and boasting about the connections. In a city where rhetorical skill and social patronage were the currencies of status, this made a certain kind of sense. You attached yourself to the most impressive figure you could find, and his reputation became yours.

Paul is having none of it. He quotes twice from the Hebrew wisdom tradition — once from Job, once from the Psalms — to make the same point two ways: God is not impressed by cleverness, and human strategies for securing status tend to collapse under their own weight. The word he uses for the wise man's "ruses" carries the sense of a trap set by someone who then blunders into it himself. There is almost a wry satisfaction in that image. The sophisticated move turns out to be the one that catches you.

But Paul does not stop at deflation. He ends with something genuinely astonishing. If you think you're grasping for the most impressive affiliation, he says, here is what actually belongs to you: Paul, Apollos, Cephas — yes — but also the world, life and death, the present and the future. Everything. "All belong to you, and you to Christ, and Christ to God." This is not a pyramid of status. It is a chain of belonging, and the bottom of the chain is not the bottom at all, because what you belong to turns out to be everything there is. The boasting is small; the actual inheritance is staggering.

Psalm 24 answers this beautifully. "The Lord's are the earth and its fullness; the world and those who dwell in it." The psalm began as a processional hymn, probably sung as the ark was carried into the temple — an act of returning to the one who owns everything. The question it asks — who may ascend to that holy place? — is answered not with credentials but with clean hands and a heart that does not chase what is empty. Paul would recognize the connection immediately.

The Gospel — Luke 5:1-11

Luke's version of the call of the first disciples is longer and stranger than Mark's brisker account. Jesus has already been preaching, already performed a healing in Simon's house, and Simon has witnessed something of what he is dealing with. Then comes the fishing.

The detail that matters is the timing. They have worked all night and caught nothing. The nets are washed and put away. The workday, by any reasonable measure, is over. When Jesus tells Simon to put out into deep water and let down the nets again, Simon's response is worth sitting with. He does not say yes with enthusiasm. He says, essentially: this makes no sense, but if you say so. "At your command I will lower the nets." That grudging, tired, improbable obedience is what opens the floodgates. The catch is so enormous the nets begin to tear and the boats begin to sink, and Luke names the names of the men who are there — James and John, Simon's partners — because this is the moment everything changes, and he wants you to know exactly who was present.

Simon Peter's reaction is not gratitude. It is terror. He falls to his knees in a half-swamped fishing boat and says, "Depart from me, Lord, for I am a sinful man." He understands, at a level beneath argument, that he is in the presence of something that sees him clearly, and what it sees is not encouraging. Jesus does not deny the assessment. He does not offer reassurance about Simon's hidden qualities or untapped potential. He says only: do not be afraid. And then he tells him what is going to happen. Not what Simon should aspire to, but what Simon will do: "from now on you will be catching men."

What the Lord does with people who know they are not enough

The thread running through both readings is not competence — it is the peculiar freedom that comes from knowing you are not the one holding this whole thing together. Paul tells the Corinthians to stop performing wisdom because the real gift has nothing to do with their credentials. Simon Peter falls to his knees because the catch has made it unmistakably clear that whatever is happening is not about his fishing skill. Gregory fled Rome because he knew what the job would cost him, and he knew he was not adequate to it.

None of this is false humility as a spiritual performance. It is an accurate reading of the situation. And in each case, the accurate reading is not the end of the story — it is precisely where the invitation begins.

To carry today

Think of something you are avoiding because you are fairly sure you will not be adequate to it — a hard conversation, a responsibility you have been deflecting, a place where someone needs more than you feel equipped to give. The question worth sitting with is not whether you are adequate, but whether you are willing to lower the nets anyway. That is usually where things get interesting.
