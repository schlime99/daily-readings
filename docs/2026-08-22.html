Memorial of the

Queenship of the Blessed Virgin Mary

The greatest among you must be your servant — and the Queen of heaven knew this first.

First readingEzekiel 43:1-7ab
PsalmPsalm 85:9ab, 10, 11-12, 13-14
GospelMatthew 23:1-12

Today's memorial falls the day after the Assumption, and that is not an accident. The Church celebrates Mary taken up into glory, and then, one day later, asks what that glory actually looks like. The answer Matthew's Gospel gives is not what anyone who has ever wanted power was hoping to hear. Glory, it turns out, looks like service all the way down.

The first reading — Ezekiel 43:1-7ab

To understand what is happening in this passage, it helps to know where it falls in the book of Ezekiel. Ezekiel is a priest writing during the Babylonian exile, and the great catastrophe of his life — and of his people's life — was the departure of God's glory from the Jerusalem temple. He saw it in a vision in chapter 10: the radiant divine presence, the kavod, slowly withdrawing from the sanctuary and then lifting away from the city entirely. It was, for a priest, the unthinkable thing. If God's glory has left, what remains?

Everything in the second half of Ezekiel is building toward the answer. The book ends with a long, strange, architectural vision of a new temple — precise dimensions, gates, courts, sacrificial arrangements — and here, near the end of that vision, comes the moment the whole book has been aching toward: the glory comes back. It comes from the east, the same direction it departed. It comes with the sound of many waters, and the earth shines with it. Ezekiel falls on his face, which is the only reasonable thing to do, and then the voice speaks from inside the temple: This is where my throne shall be. Here I will dwell among the children of Israel forever.

The people hearing this passage were exiles. They had lost Jerusalem, lost the temple, lost — or so it felt — the very presence of God. Ezekiel's vision says: that presence is not gone forever. It comes back. It finds a home. The word forever would have landed with enormous weight on ears that had heard nothing but loss.

The psalm catches this exactly. "The glory of the Lord will dwell in our land" — that is the refrain, and it is almost too good to say after the exile. Kindness and truth meeting, justice and peace kissing: these are not abstractions but the conditions of a world where God actually lives among his people. The psalm knows what glory produces when it arrives.

The Gospel — Matthew 23:1-12

This passage opens the longest sustained critique Jesus delivers in all of Matthew — chapter 23 is a hard chapter, a series of "woe to you" statements aimed at religious leaders who have made the practice of faith into a performance of status. But the opening verses, the ones we read today, are not yet the woes. They are the setup, and they contain something genuinely surprising.

Jesus does not dismiss the scribes and Pharisees as frauds whose teachings should be thrown out. He says, explicitly, to do and observe what they teach — they sit on the chair of Moses, which means they hold a real, legitimate authority in the interpretation of the Law. The problem is not their doctrine. The problem is the gap between what they say and what they do, and the particular shape that gap has taken: they have made religion into a ladder, and they are standing at the top of it. Widening the small leather boxes (phylacteries) that held scripture verses, lengthening the fringes on their garments, taking the best seats, expecting the respectful greeting in the marketplace — none of these things are wrong in themselves, but together they form a portrait of people who have started to use the things of God to secure their own position among people.

Then Jesus turns toward his own disciples, and the instruction is almost startlingly flat: do not be called Rabbi, father, master. Not because titles are evil, but because there is only one Teacher, one Father, one Lord, and any title that obscures that tends to obscure it in the direction of the person wearing the title. The one who leads, in Jesus's community, leads by going lower. The greatest one is the servant of everyone else.

Where Ezekiel and Matthew meet: what glory actually looks like

The connection between these two readings is not obvious at first, but it is real. Ezekiel sees the glory of God return and fill the temple, and the voice that speaks announces: this is where I will set the soles of my feet. God's feet. The image is unexpectedly physical — a God who walks, who stands somewhere, who occupies a place. The glory does not hover remotely; it comes down and stays.

Mary is the temple the Church has in mind today. The tradition has always read her that way — the place where God chose to dwell, the site of the Incarnation, the one whose yes made room. And if that is the image, then the glory that dwells in her is the same glory Ezekiel saw: not triumphant in the way power is triumphant, but present in the way love is present, all the way to the ground. The Queen of heaven is the woman who called herself a handmaid. The throne is a manger. The feast of her queenship is inseparable from that.

Matthew's Gospel, with its hard words about the trap of seeking honor, is not a strange companion for this feast. It is the feast's content. If we want to understand what it means for Mary to reign, we have to understand what Jesus says reigning means: it means serving, it means going to the bottom of the room, it means your greatness is entirely measured by what you give away.

To carry today

Think of a place in your life where you are holding a position — at work, at home, in a friendship — and ask honestly whether you are using it to help or to be seen. The question is not meant to sting; it is meant to clarify. Mary's crown, as the Church understands it, was won by a lifetime of handing herself over. There is something worth imitating in that today.
