Thursday · 1 October

Memorial of

Saint Thérèse of the Child Jesus, Virgin and Doctor of the Church

Job clings to a Vindicator he cannot see; Thérèse spent her whole life doing the same thing.

First readingJob 19:21-27
PsalmPsalm 27:7-8a, 8b-9abc, 13-14
GospelLuke 10:1-12

Today the Church celebrates a woman who died at twenty-four, having spent her final months in spiritual darkness so complete that she later said the very thought of heaven seemed like empty words. That woman is now a Doctor of the Church. The readings chosen for her memorial are not about sweetness and flowers — they are about someone in agony who will not let go of God, and about a mission that asks everything of those who accept it.

The first reading — Job 19:21-27

Job is in the middle of his book and in the middle of his catastrophe. He has lost his children, his property, his health. What makes the Book of Job strange and uncomfortable — in the best possible way — is that he has also lost the comfort of easy religious explanation. His friends keep insisting that his suffering must be punishment for some sin; he keeps insisting that this is wrong and that he knows it is wrong, even though he cannot explain what God is doing. The passage today comes just after he has accused those friends of persecuting him. "Pity me," he says — and there is no piety in it, no performance of patience. It is a raw plea from someone who feels abandoned on all sides.

Then something extraordinary happens. Out of that same desolation, Job suddenly declares his certainty: "I know that my Vindicator lives." The word translated "Vindicator" carries the weight of a kinsman-redeemer in ancient Israelite law — a close relative who had the legal right and obligation to come to your defense, to buy back what you had lost, to stand up for you in court. Job claims one. He claims that this Vindicator will one day stand on the earth, and that he, Job, with his own eyes, from his own flesh, will see God. Scholars have debated for centuries exactly what Job means by seeing God "from his flesh" — whether it means through his body or after his body — but the emotional force of the line is unmistakable: Job will not be absent from the vindication. He is not hoping for a theological concept to be vindicated somewhere in the abstract. He wants to be there. He wants to see it himself.

Psalm 27 answers this directly. "I believe that I shall see the good things of the Lord in the land of the living" — not in death, not in the abstract, but here, in embodied, living experience. The psalm also gives us the emotional texture of what waiting looks like: "Hide not your face from me." The psalmist is addressing someone who feels hidden from them. The command to "wait for the Lord with courage" is not advice to someone who is comfortable; it is instruction to someone who is barely holding on.

The Gospel — Luke 10:1-12

Luke's Gospel is the only one that mentions the sending of seventy-two disciples, distinct from the Twelve. Jesus sends them in pairs — which is already a mercy, since loneliness is one of the harder features of any mission — into towns he intends to visit. They are advance workers, preparing the ground. The instructions he gives them are deliberately stripped-down: no bag, no extra sandals, no money, and no stopping to chat along the road. That last detail sounds almost rude, but it reflects the urgency of the mission, not a permission to be unfriendly. They carry peace into each house and eat what is offered, depending entirely on hospitality.

What strikes me about this passage is how clearly Jesus names both outcomes. Towns that welcome them will receive healing and the announcement of the Kingdom. Towns that reject them will still receive the announcement of the Kingdom, just without the healing and with considerably less goodwill attached. The mission does not depend on its reception. The laborers go anyway. The harvest is happening whether the field hands feel adequate or welcomed.

Longing and sending

These two passages are on separate narrative tracks — one is ancient Hebrew poetry about suffering, the other is a missionary dispatch from Jesus — but they share something important today, which is the figure of Thérèse herself. What Job experiences in the first reading is exactly what Thérèse described in the last year of her life: a darkness that swallowed her consolations, a Vindicator she could not feel, a God whose face seemed hidden. She held on not because she felt anything reassuring, but because she had decided to. And what the seventy-two are sent to do in the Gospel is what Thérèse understood her own vocation to be — not to go anywhere physically, but to be at the heart of the Church's mission through prayer, through love, through a trust that does not depend on results. She famously wrote that she wanted to spend her heaven doing good on earth. She was not being sentimental; she was being Job. She was insisting that she herself would see the Vindicator, and that the harvest was worth laboring for even from a bed in a French infirmary.

The "little way" she is famous for teaching is not smallness for its own sake. It is the recognition that trust without consolation is still trust, and that the Kingdom is at hand whether we can feel it or not.

To carry today

Think of a place in your life where you have stopped expecting anything to change — a relationship, a prayer that seems to bounce off the ceiling, a hope you have quietly set aside. Ask yourself whether you have confused the absence of consolation with the absence of God. The invitation today is not to manufacture feeling, but to make the simple, stubborn choice that Job makes: to say "I know" before you can see it.
