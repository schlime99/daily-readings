Seventeenth Week in Ordinary Time

A nation confesses it has nothing left but the name of God to appeal to.

First readingJeremiah 14:17-22
PsalmPsalm 79:8, 9, 11, 13
GospelMatthew 13:36-43

There is a particular kind of prayer that comes only after everything else has failed — not a polished petition, but a cry from the rubble. Jeremiah's voice in the first reading and the disciples' quiet question in the Gospel have almost nothing to do with each other on the surface, and it is worth being honest about that. These two are running on separate tracks today. But each, in its own way, is asking for the same thing: to understand what God is doing in a world that looks, from where we stand, like a wreck.

The first reading — Jeremiah 14:17-22

This passage comes from a stretch of Jeremiah sometimes called the "drought liturgy" — a sequence of laments, divine responses, and communal prayers organized around a devastating drought and military catastrophe that fell on Judah in the final decades before the Babylonian exile. By chapter fourteen, the prophet has already heard God refuse to be moved by sacrifices and prayers offered insincerely. What we get here is almost unbearable: Jeremiah is told to weep without rest. He goes out to the fields and sees the dead. He goes into the city and sees the starving. Even the priests and prophets — the people who were supposed to have access to God — are wandering in a land they no longer recognize.

The poem turns, about halfway through, from description to confession. "We recognize, O Lord, our wickedness, the guilt of our fathers; that we have sinned against you." This is significant because throughout the book of Jeremiah, the people have been slow to admit anything. They have been blaming the drought on the wrong gods, turning to foreign alliances, telling themselves that the Temple's presence in Jerusalem guaranteed their safety regardless of how they lived. The confession here is real, and it is desperate.

Then comes what is, for my money, one of the strangest and most moving arguments in all of scripture. The people essentially say to God: among all the idols of the nations, not one of them can produce rain. The heavens themselves cannot send showers on their own. You alone have done all these things — which means you alone can undo them. They are not appealing to their own goodness, because they have just admitted they have none. They are appealing to God's name — to the fact that God is who God is, and that a God who abandons his people entirely would be a different God than the one who made the covenant. It is an argument built entirely from rubble, and it is oddly persuasive.

Psalm 79 is the perfect answer to this reading, because it is not an answer — it is a continuation. It carries the same confession, the same appeal to God's name rather than to human merit, and the same raw honesty about how low things have gotten. "For the glory of your name, O Lord, deliver us." When the congregation sings this refrain back, they are praying with Jeremiah, which is exactly what the liturgy intends.

The Gospel — Matthew 13:36-43

The disciples have just heard the parable of the wheat and the weeds — the story of a farmer whose good crop is infiltrated by a weed (almost certainly darnel, a plant that looks exactly like wheat in its early stages and whose roots are so intertwined with wheat that you cannot pull it up without taking the wheat too). The disciples are not satisfied with the story; they want the key. So Jesus goes inside, they follow him, and he gives them the decoded version, term by term.

The explanation is more alarming than the parable. The weeds are real, the enemy who sows them is real, and the final reckoning is real — a fiery furnace, wailing and grinding of teeth. Jesus does not soften this. He is not being cruel; he is being honest with people who are about to go out into a world that will resist everything they preach. But the last image is not fire. It is light: "the righteous will shine like the sun in the Kingdom of their Father." That line deserves to sit for a moment. Not that they will be rewarded, not that they will be safe — that they will shine. The image is startling and beautiful, a kind of transfiguration of ordinary faithful life.

Notice also what Jesus does not say. He does not tell the disciples to start identifying the weeds. That work belongs to the angels, at the end of the age. The implication is that our job is not sorting — it is growing.

What patience costs

If there is a thread between these two readings, it is the cost of waiting. Jeremiah's community is waiting for peace that doesn't come and healing that is replaced by terror. The disciples are asking, implicitly, why the weeds are still there. Both are sitting in conditions that look like abandonment.

The answer the liturgy offers — not loudly, but steadily — is that God's timing is not negligence. Jeremiah's people are held to confession and to the covenant. The disciples are held to trust in a final justice that is not theirs to administer. Both are asked to continue in conditions of uncertainty, which is exactly what faith looks like most days.

To carry today

Think about some situation in your life where you are waiting for God to act — a relationship that hasn't healed, a prayer that seems to go nowhere, some injustice that keeps standing. Ask yourself honestly: am I still willing to appeal to God's name, even when I have nothing else to offer? Notice whether your prayer today can be honest enough to sound like Jeremiah's.
