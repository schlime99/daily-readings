Twenty-fifth Sunday in Ordinary Time

God's generosity does not shrink to fit what we think we have earned.

First readingIsaiah 55:6-9
PsalmPsalm 145:2-3, 8-9, 17-18
Second readingPhilippians 1:20c-24, 27a
GospelMatthew 20:1-16a

The parable of the vineyard workers is one of those Gospel passages that, if you are honest with yourself, lands a little uncomfortably. You can hear the logic of the all-day workers perfectly well, and you can feel the sting of the landowner's question — "Are you envious because I am generous?" — aimed, at least partly, at you. Isaiah has been setting the stage for exactly this discomfort all morning: my thoughts are not your thoughts. That is the thread here, not a comforting one but an enlarging one.

The first reading — Isaiah 55:6-9

Isaiah 55 comes near the end of a long section of the book sometimes called the Book of Consolation, addressed to Israel in exile in Babylon. The people have been away from home for decades. The temple is rubble. And into that situation, Isaiah brings not a scolding but an extraordinary invitation: seek the Lord while he may be found. The urgency is real — while he may be found, while he is near — but it is the urgency of an open door, not a slamming one.

Then comes the turn. The scoundrel and the wicked are told to forsake their ways and return, and the reason given is not that they will be tolerated on a technicality but that God is generous in forgiving. That word generous matters. It is not grudging pardon; it is abundance. And then the famous lines about the distance between God's ways and ours arrive not as a rebuke but as an explanation of why such generosity is even possible. The heavens are above the earth — that is not a small gap — and what God can see from that height, what God can hold in mind, is simply different in kind from our ground-level accounting. We tally what is fair. God is doing something else entirely.

Psalm 145, which answers this reading directly, piles up the same qualities: gracious and merciful, slow to anger, of great kindness, good to all. The psalmist is not just reciting attributes; he is astonished by them. "His greatness is unsearchable" is not a dry theological claim. It means: I looked for the bottom of this and could not find it. The psalm's refrain, "The Lord is near to all who call upon him," is the practical consequence of everything Isaiah just said — a God whose ways are this far above ours is not therefore distant. The height of God's perspective produces nearness, not remoteness.

The second reading — Philippians 1:20c-24, 27a

Paul is writing from prison, probably Rome, and this short passage is one of the most personally revealing things he ever put down. He is genuinely uncertain whether he wants to live or die — not from despair but because both options seem good to him. To die is to be with Christ, which he says simply is far better. To go on living means more work, more love, more usefulness to the people he has come to care for deeply. He cannot choose. This passage runs largely on its own, separate from the Isaiah-and-Matthew pairing, but it is not accidental that it sits here. Paul is a man who has so thoroughly recalibrated his sense of value — life, death, gain, loss — that he no longer ranks things the way he once did. That is exactly what the parable is asking the first workers, and perhaps us, to do.

The Gospel — Matthew 20:1-16a

The parable is about a landowner who hires workers at dawn, at nine, at noon, at three, and finally at five o'clock — the last hour of the working day. At evening he pays everyone the same: the full day's wage. The workers hired at dawn are not cheated; they receive exactly what they were promised. But they expected more, and when they don't get it, they grumble.

The landowner's reply is worth reading slowly. He does not argue that the late workers somehow put in equivalent effort. He does not pretend the accounting is equal. He simply asserts his freedom: am I not free to do as I wish with my own money? And then the question that actually stings: are you envious because I am generous? In the Greek behind our translation, the phrase is literally "is your eye evil because I am good?" — the "evil eye" being a common idiom for envy and also for stinginess of spirit. The all-day workers are not being punished. They are being shown something about themselves: that their sense of what is right has quietly become a cage that excludes the good fortune of others.

Where God's logic and ours come apart

This is the real hinge. Isaiah says God's thoughts are not our thoughts, and we nod along pleasantly because it sounds like humility. The parable shows us what that actually means on the ground, and it is more demanding. Our thoughts naturally run to proportion, to fairness understood as equivalence. I worked more, therefore I should receive more. God's generosity, the parable insists, does not work this way — not because fairness is bad but because what God is handing out is not wages calculated against effort. It is something more like belonging, and belonging cannot be proportioned. The last workers are in the vineyard. They belong. The same life is theirs as the first workers'. God is not distributing a commodity; God is opening a home.

Isaiah told the scoundrel to come. The psalm said God is near to all who call. Paul found that everything he once counted as gain had rearranged itself entirely. The parable is asking whether we can let our own counting rearrange itself too.

To carry today

Think of someone whose good fortune has ever quietly annoyed you — a promotion you thought unearned, a second chance you felt undeserved, a welcome you thought too easy. Sit with the honest question: what does that reaction assume about how goodness is distributed? Ask for the grace to want abundance for others rather than merely accounting for what you believe you have earned.
