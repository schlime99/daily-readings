Memorial of

Saint Andrew Dung-Lac, Priest, and Companions, Martyrs

The harvest comes; the martyrs of Vietnam already knew which side of it they stood on.

First readingRevelation 14:14-19
PsalmPsalm 96:10-13
GospelLuke 21:5-11

Today the Church remembers Andrew Dung-Lac and the more than one hundred thousand Vietnamese Catholics who died for the faith between the seventeenth and nineteenth centuries — priests, catechists, farmers, families. The readings given to this day do not sentimentalize their deaths. They frame them in the largest possible terms: a coming harvest, an unshakeable kingdom, and a Lord who will not be deceived by noise or chaos.

The first reading — Revelation 14:14-19

Revelation is apocalyptic literature, which means it was written in a kind of code — not to be mysterious, but to speak safely under persecution. John's audience were people living through exactly the kind of terror that the Vietnamese martyrs would face centuries later: the demand to worship the empire's gods, the threat of death for refusal, the disorienting sense that the wrong side seemed to be winning. Revelation answers that disorientation with vision.

Chapter 14 sits in a hinge moment in the book. John has just seen the 144,000 — the full number of the faithful — standing unharmed with the Lamb. Now comes the harvest. The image John reaches for is the grain harvest and the grape vintage, two of the most fundamental rhythms of ancient agricultural life. Everyone in his audience would have known what it looked like when the sickle swung and the work of a whole year was gathered in. The figure on the cloud — "one who looked like a son of man," language borrowed from Daniel — carries a gold crown and a sharp sickle. When the angel from the temple cries out that the harvest is fully ripe, there is no hesitation. The sickle swings.

The grape vintage is the more sobering image. The grapes go into "the great wine press of God's fury," and the judgment it describes is fierce. It is worth sitting with that fierceness rather than softening it. For people who had watched innocents die, who had seen the powerful escape consequence, the promise of a final reckoning was not frightening — it was the only thing that made the present bearable. John is not writing a threat; he is writing a consolation. The world's cruelties do not have the last word.

Psalm 96 fits this moment exactly. It is a psalm of cosmic praise, calling on the heavens, the earth, the sea, the plains, even the trees of the forest to shout before the Lord who comes to judge. And crucially, the judgment is cause for joy: he shall rule the world with justice. The refrain the assembly sings — "The Lord comes to judge the earth" — is not a warning to be dreaded. It is the news everyone has been waiting for.

The Gospel — Luke 21:5-11

Luke 21 is what scholars sometimes call the "little apocalypse" — Jesus's own account of things coming apart before they come together. The setting is the Temple in Jerusalem, and the disciples are admiring it, as well they might. It was one of the great architectural wonders of the ancient world, its stones massive, its gold ornaments catching the light. Jesus looks at all of it and says flatly that not one stone will be left on another.

The disciples' immediate question is entirely human: When? They want a timetable. Jesus declines to give one, and this is itself a kind of teaching. He warns them against the ones who will come claiming to have the timetable — "I am he," they will say, "the time has come." Do not follow them, Jesus says. The urgency of the false prophet is one of the marks of the false prophet.

What Jesus does say is that the signs they will see — wars, insurrections, earthquakes, famines, plagues — are not the end. They are part of the story, but not its last chapter. This is cold comfort in one sense, and genuine comfort in another. The world's upheavals do not mean that God has lost control of the narrative. They mean the narrative is still unfolding.

Ripe grain and steady nerves

What connects Revelation and Luke today is the question of how to hold your nerve when everything looks like catastrophe. John's vision and Jesus's warning are aimed at the same temptation: the collapse of faith under pressure, whether the pressure is persecution or apocalyptic panic. The harvest will come. The judge is just. The ones who died rather than renounce their faith — Andrew Dung-Lac, the catechists, the fishermen, the mothers — are already in that harvest, already gathered in.

The alleluia verse, taken from Revelation itself, says what the martyrs lived: Remain faithful until death, and I will give you the crown of life. That is not an easy word. But it is an honest one. The Church does not promise its martyrs an easy road. It promises them that the road has an end, and that the end is the Lord.

Andrew Dung-Lac was arrested, released, arrested again, and finally beheaded in 1839. He had time, apparently, to know what was coming. The readings today suggest that he also had something to hold onto: a vision of a harvest already underway, a Lord already enthroned, and a world whose stones, however imposing, would not stand forever.

To carry today

When the news is loud with wars and upheavals, it is easy to mistake noise for final meaning — to feel that history is simply spinning out of control. Ask yourself today what you are placing your security in, and whether it is the kind of thing that lasts. The martyrs staked everything on what they could not see. What would it mean for you, in your much smaller and safer circumstances, to do the same?
