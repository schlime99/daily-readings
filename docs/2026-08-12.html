Nineteenth Week in Ordinary Time

The mark that saves and the community that holds — both are acts of the God who will not abandon his people.

First readingEzekiel 9:1-7; 10:18-22
PsalmPsalm 113:1-2, 3-4, 5-6
GospelMatthew 18:15-20

There is a strange and uncomfortable mercy running through today's readings. Ezekiel's vision is terrifying on its face — angelic executioners moving through Jerusalem, and a God who does not stop them. But there is a man with a writer's case who goes first, marking foreheads, and that mark changes everything. Matthew's Gospel, for its part, gives the early Church a careful, step-by-step process for handling conflict — not because the community is bureaucratic, but because reconciliation requires real effort, and real effort requires a structure. Both readings are, in their different ways, about a God who is precise in his love and will not settle for vague, undifferentiated mercy that costs nothing.

The first reading — Ezekiel 9:1-7; 10:18-22

Ezekiel is one of the strangest books in all of Scripture, and this chapter sits near the top of that strangeness. Ezekiel is a priest-prophet writing during the Babylonian exile — or, in this vision, just before it. Jerusalem has not yet fallen, but the book has been spending chapters on why it will: the temple has been filled with idols, the people have abandoned the covenant, and the leadership has led the way down. The vision of chapters 8-11 is one long sustained image of God's glory departing the city, and what we hear today is the hinge of that departure.

The seven figures who enter are not explained at length — Ezekiel sees them and reports them. Six carry destroying weapons. The seventh is dressed in linen, the priestly color, and carries something a priest would never carry: a writer's case, a scribe's tools. He is sent ahead of the executioners with a single instruction — find everyone who mourns the abominations being committed in the city, and mark their foreheads with the letter Thau. In ancient Hebrew script, Thau was written as an X or a cross, and the tradition has never been able to resist noticing this. Whether or not Ezekiel had the cross in mind, the shape of the letter is not the point — the act is. This is a God who distinguishes. He sees grief over sin. He sees the people who have not gone along, who have mourned privately while the official culture curdled around them, and he marks them.

The judgment that follows is severe, and the text does not soften it. But the severity itself is the setting for the mercy: without the mark, there is no distinction. Without the distinction, the mourners have no way to be saved. The glory of the LORD then departs the temple — slowly, reluctantly, resting on the cherubim at the eastern gate as if pausing before leaving — and this is almost more sorrowful than the slaughter. The building is just a building. What is leaving is the presence.

Psalm 113 answers this scene in a way that might seem jarring — it is pure praise, a song about God enthroned above the heavens who nonetheless stoops to look at the earth below. But that is exactly the point: the God who looks is the God who sees. The same God who marks foreheads is the one who is higher than the skies and lower than the dust of the poor. Seeing is not passive for this God.

The Gospel — Matthew 18:15-20

Matthew 18 is sometimes called the "community discourse," and these verses are near its heart. Jesus has just told the parable of the lost sheep, about the shepherd who leaves the ninety-nine to find the one — so the context is urgently about not losing anyone. What follows, then, is not a procedure for expelling difficult members; it is a procedure for getting them back.

The steps are deliberate. First, go alone — no audience, no humiliation, just the two of you and the truth. If that fails, bring a witness or two, in keeping with the ancient practice that a serious accusation requires more than one voice. If that fails, bring it before the whole community. And if even that fails — the phrase "treat him as you would a Gentile or a tax collector" is the one that can confuse people, because it sounds like a dismissal. But remember who Jesus himself ate with. Tax collectors sat at his table. Gentiles came to him and were not sent away. To treat someone as a Gentile or a tax collector is not to write them off; it is to return to the beginning, to see them as someone who still needs to be reached, someone outside who might yet come in.

The promise that follows — whatever you bind or loose, whatever two of you agree to ask — is often lifted out of context to suggest that the community's consensus makes something true. But the promise is embedded in this process of reconciliation. The authority to bind and loose is given to a community that is doing the hard work of staying together.

What Ezekiel and Matthew share: the weight of being seen

These two readings are not formally connected, but they rhyme. In Ezekiel, the God who departs the temple first sends someone to look — to find the grieving ones and mark them. In Matthew, the instruction before any judgment is to go to your brother, to look him in the eye, to say the difficult thing privately rather than making a scene. Both texts assume that real accountability requires real attention. Vague condemnation from a distance is not what either God or Jesus is after. Both want someone to go close.

To carry today

Is there a conversation you have been avoiding — something unresolved with another person that you keep meaning to address and keep putting off? It does not have to be dramatic. Sit today with the simple courage of going first, going privately, going with some hope of getting the person back rather than proving yourself right. That is what the Gospel is actually asking.
