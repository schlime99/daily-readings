Twenty-second Sunday in Ordinary Time

Jeremiah cannot stop, and Peter cannot yet begin — both are learning what God's way costs.

First readingJeremiah 20:7-9
PsalmPsalm 63:2-9
Second readingRomans 12:1-2
GospelMatthew 16:21-27

There is a word for what Jeremiah is doing in today's first reading, and it is not a word we expect to hear in church: he is complaining, directly and without apology, to God's face. And across the centuries, the Gospel shows us Peter doing something structurally similar — telling God, in the person of Jesus, that he has gotten things wrong. These two scenes are not opposites. They are two stages of the same hard education, and it is worth sitting with both.

The first reading — Jeremiah 20:7-9

Jeremiah is one of the most honest books in the Bible, and this passage may be its most honest moment. The prophet has been at his work for years, and it is going badly. He has proclaimed disaster, and his countrymen have responded with mockery and contempt. He is not a popular figure; he is an object of laughter, a man everyone rolls their eyes at. And here, in what the tradition calls one of his "confessions," he turns on God with startling directness: You duped me. The Hebrew behind that word carries something close to seduction or enticement — the sense that Jeremiah was drawn in, persuaded, perhaps even overpowered. He is saying, in essence: I did not ask for this. You put me here.

Then comes something even more remarkable. Jeremiah resolves to quit. He will not mention God's name, will not deliver another word. And it does not work. He tries to hold the word in, and it becomes — the image is extraordinary — like fire burning in his heart, imprisoned in his bones. He cannot carry it without delivering it, and he cannot put it down. He is caught.

This is a portrait of genuine, unwilling prophecy, and it matters because it is so far from what we might imagine a holy person's interior life looks like. Jeremiah is not serene. He is not grateful. He is angry and exhausted and feeling trapped. And yet the word burns. Psalm 63 steps into exactly this tension — the psalmist's soul is thirsting for God, pining for him the way parched earth aches for water. That thirst is Jeremiah's too, even inside his complaint. The fire in the bones and the parched earth longing for water are the same spiritual reality, described from different angles. You cannot burn with something you do not love.

The second reading — Romans 12:1-2

Paul's letter to the Romans has spent eleven chapters on the grand architecture of salvation — sin, grace, justification, the mystery of Israel, the faithfulness of God. Now, at chapter twelve, he turns to what all of that means for daily life, and the pivot word is therefore: because of everything I have just said, here is how to live. The two verses we receive today are his opening move in that practical turn. Offer your bodies as a living sacrifice — not an animal on an altar, but a whole self, given over. Do not be conformed to the shape of this age, but be transformed by the renewal of your mind. Paul is running on his own track here, somewhat separate from Jeremiah and the Gospel, but there is a quiet resonance: transformation is the subject all three readings share, and Paul is describing its interior mechanism. The mind must be renewed before the life can be changed. Discernment — knowing what God actually wills — is not a flash of intuition but the fruit of that slow renewal.

The Gospel — Matthew 16:21-27

This passage comes immediately after one of the highest moments in the Gospels. Just before this, Peter had declared that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of the living God, and Jesus had responded with extraordinary praise, calling Peter the rock on which the church would be built. The very next thing Jesus does is tell his disciples about the cross — that he must go to Jerusalem, suffer, be killed, and be raised. And Peter, still warm from his great moment, takes Jesus aside and rebukes him. "God forbid, Lord. No such thing shall ever happen to you."

The response is ferocious: Get behind me, Satan. This is not cruelty — it is precision. Peter has, without knowing it, stepped into the role of the adversary, offering Jesus an exit from the path the Father has set before him. Peter's vision of a triumphant Messiah is not wicked; it is simply human, and that is exactly the diagnosis Jesus gives: you are thinking not as God does, but as human beings do. Then comes the hard invitation — take up your cross, follow, lose your life to find it.

The cost of the word, and the cost of the way

What joins Jeremiah and Peter is that both of them bump, hard, against the shape of what God is actually doing. Jeremiah thought prophecy would look one way; it looked another. Peter thought the Messiah's path would look one way; it looked another. Both resisted. Jeremiah's resistance burned out from the inside; the word was simply stronger than his decision to stop. Peter's resistance met a direct and startling correction. In different ways, both men are being taught that God's way runs through places we would not choose, and that the choosing is not ours to do.

This is not a comfortable message, but it is an honest one. The fire in the bones, the cross taken up — these are not punishments. They are descriptions of what love looks like when it is given fully rather than managed carefully.

To carry today

Think today about the place in your life where you are most tempted to tell God he has gotten things wrong — the suffering that should not be happening, the call that seems too costly, the path that looks nothing like what you expected. Hold that place quietly, not to resolve it, but to notice whether there might be a fire in it that is not yours to put out.
