Thirty-first Week in Ordinary Time

Paul trades his whole résumé for one thing; heaven throws a party over one who was lost.

First readingPhilippians 3:3-8a
PsalmPsalm 105:2-3, 4-5, 6-7
GospelLuke 15:1-10

There is a strange kind of freedom in Paul's letter today, the freedom of a man who has looked at everything he built and decided it was ballast he needed to drop. And in the Gospel, Jesus is at a table that the respectable people find scandalous, telling stories about a shepherd who abandons the ninety-nine and a woman who tears her house apart — both of them in a kind of joyful frenzy when what was lost comes home. These two readings are on separate tracks in some ways, but they circle the same fire: what does it cost to find the one thing that matters?

The first reading — Philippians 3:3-8a

Paul is writing from prison, probably Rome, to the church at Philippi, a community he loves warmly and worries about less than most. But there is a problem in the background: teachers have arrived insisting that Gentile converts must be circumcised and observe the Mosaic law to be fully saved. Paul's response here is not to dismiss the law — he is too honest for that — but to outbid the teachers on their own terms before dismantling the whole contest.

He lists his credentials with something close to dark humor. Circumcised on the eighth day — by the book, not a late convert. Tribe of Benjamin — one of the two tribes that stayed loyal to Jerusalem. Hebrew of Hebrew parentage — not a Greek-speaking diaspora Jew who has softened at the edges, but someone formed in the full tradition. A Pharisee, which meant serious, disciplined, committed to the law's finest grain. And then the one that stops you: in zeal I persecuted the Church. He names it right there in the list, without flinching, as though to say: I know exactly what I was.

The point is that he is not speaking from ignorance. He is not a man who failed at the law and then found something easier. By every measure the tradition offered, he was winning. And then he says he counts it all as loss — the Greek word behind "loss" carries the sense of refuse, of something thrown overboard. Not that it was worthless in itself, but that compared to what he has found, it cannot even be weighed on the same scale.

What he found, he calls simply "the supreme good of knowing Christ Jesus my Lord." That phrase, knowing, is not intellectual. It is the knowing of intimacy, of having been met and changed by someone. Psalm 105 picks up exactly this note — "rejoice, O hearts that seek the LORD" — and the psalmist is addressing the descendants of Abraham, the very lineage Paul just rattled off. The psalm assumes the seeking is what God delights in. Paul is saying the seeking has found its end.

The Gospel — Luke 15:1-10

Luke 15 is one of the great chapters of the New Testament, and this is its opening. The two parables here — the lost sheep and the lost coin — lead into the parable of the prodigal son, which follows immediately. All three are told in response to the same complaint: "This man welcomes sinners and eats with them." Jesus is not teaching in a neutral setting; he is defending himself, or rather, he is explaining himself to people who think an explanation is owed.

The shepherd who leaves the ninety-nine is, if you stop and think about it, a slightly alarming figure by any practical standard. Ninety-nine sheep in the desert while he goes searching for one? The math is terrible. But that is precisely the point — this is not an economics lesson, it is a portrait of a love that does not calculate. And when he finds the sheep, he does not scold it or drag it. He sets it on his shoulders. There is something almost tender and undignified about that image, a grown man carrying a sheep home on his neck because the animal is too exhausted or too foolish to walk.

The woman with the coin is the same parable in a domestic key. Ten coins — likely a small sum, possibly her savings, possibly coins from her wedding headdress that carried sentimental weight beyond their value — and she loses one. She lights a lamp, sweeps the whole house, searches carefully. The word Luke uses suggests patient, methodical attention, not frantic ransacking. And when she finds it, she calls the neighbors. The rejoicing is out of proportion to the find, and that disproportion is the whole message.

Both parables end with the same punch: there is more joy in heaven over one sinner who repents than over ninety-nine who have no need. That last phrase is quietly ironic — Jesus is looking at the Pharisees when he says it. Who exactly has no need of repentance?

What Paul's ledger and the shepherd's math have in common

Paul throws his credentials overboard. The shepherd abandons the ninety-nine. The woman tears the house apart. In each case, something that looks like reasonable, proportionate behavior gets thrown out the window in favor of what is actually most important. Paul's résumé was real. The ninety-nine sheep were real. The nine coins were real. None of it was worth clinging to when the one thing — the one person — that mattered was at stake.

This is what the Gospel keeps doing to ordinary accounting. It doesn't say the other things were nothing. It says there is something that makes everything else look like ballast.

To carry today

Think today about something you are holding onto because you earned it — a reputation, a standing, a carefully maintained image of yourself as capable or righteous or put-together. Ask honestly whether it is helping you move toward God or whether it has quietly become the thing you protect instead. The invitation is not to throw your life away, but to notice what you are actually carrying, and whether it is carrying you back.
