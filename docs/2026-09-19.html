Twenty-fourth Week in Ordinary Time

The seed that falls on good soil bears fruit not by intensity, but by perseverance.

First reading1 Corinthians 15:35-37, 42-49
PsalmPsalm 56:10-14
GospelLuke 8:4-15

There is something quietly remarkable about the way today's two readings, without any apparent coordination, end up telling the same story from opposite ends. Paul is working out what happens after the harvest — what kind of body rises from what has died. Luke's Jesus is standing at the beginning, at the moment of sowing, wondering what kind of ground the seed will find. Together they trace the arc of a life given to the word of God, from first hearing to final flowering.

The first reading — 1 Corinthians 15:35-37, 42-49

This passage comes near the end of Paul's great argument about resurrection in 1 Corinthians 15 — the longest single chapter he ever wrote, and one of the most sustained pieces of theological reasoning in the New Testament. Someone in Corinth has raised an objection that probably felt very clever at the time: How are the dead raised? With what sort of body? The question sounds like skepticism dressed up as curiosity, and Paul answers it with something between impatience and genuine delight. "You fool," he says, and then immediately offers an image that is worth sitting with slowly.

A seed is placed in the ground. It dies — or at least, it ceases to be what it was. What comes up is not larger or more elaborate version of the seed; it is something categorically different, a stalk, a plant, a tree. The seed was necessary. It was not incidental. But the seed is not the point; the wheat is the point. Paul's argument is that asking what kind of body will rise is like asking why a kernel of wheat doesn't look like the loaf of bread you bake from it. The question misunderstands the relationship between what goes in and what comes out.

He then lays out his contrasts in pairs: corruptible and incorruptible, dishonorable and glorious, weak and powerful, natural and spiritual. These aren't improvements of degree — they are transformations of kind. And behind all of this stands a theological claim about what Christ's resurrection means for the rest of us. Adam, the first man, was formed from the earth and gave us our earthly nature. Christ, the last Adam, is from heaven, and gives us a heavenly one. We have already worn the image of the earthly — there is nothing speculative about that. Paul's confidence is that we shall also bear the image of the heavenly one.

The psalm picks this up from the inside. The psalmist has been rescued from death and stumbling, and his response is not philosophy but gratitude in motion: "I will walk in the presence of God, in the light of the living." That is resurrection language before the doctrine exists — the conviction that God's deliverance is not only from something but into something, into a life that walks forward in light.

The Gospel — Luke 8:4-15

The parable of the sower is one of the few parables Jesus explains himself, which is one reason scholars have turned it over so many times — it is rare to have the text and the key in the same room. Luke places it here as Jesus is moving through towns and villages, crowds growing, a mixed company of the curious and the committed pressing in around him. The parable itself is a piece of realism: any farmer in first-century Galilee knew that not all seed found good ground, and that the ratio of loss to yield was real and sometimes painful. A hundredfold return from the seed that did land well was extraordinary, almost extravagant — but it was a return on a fraction of what was sown.

What is striking in Luke's version is that Jesus identifies the Word of God as the seed. It is not a teaching method, not an illustration for the spiritually literate — it is the very life of God being placed into the world, into human hearts, and the question is entirely about what receives it. The path, the rock, the thorns: Jesus is not describing different categories of people so much as different conditions of a heart. Most of us have known all of them in ourselves at different seasons. The hearing that doesn't hold. The joy that doesn't root. The life so crowded with anxiety and comfort and distraction that nothing matures. And then — quietly, without fanfare — the good soil, which Luke describes with a phrase worth returning to: a generous and good heart, bearing fruit through perseverance.

That last word is important. The good soil doesn't produce by intensity or fervor. It produces by perseverance — by continuing, by not giving up when the crop is slow, by remaining in the ground long enough for something to grow.

What the seed becomes

Here is where Paul and Luke, arriving from opposite directions, meet. Paul's seed goes into the ground and dies, and what rises is unrecognizable but real — transformed rather than merely continued. Jesus's seed falls into the soil of a human life and, when it finds good ground, produces fruit through the long patience of perseverance. Both images ask us to trust a process we cannot see and cannot control. Both ask us to believe that what is placed in the dark does not stay dark.

The connection is not forced; it is the logic of the thing itself. Growth is hidden. Resurrection is hidden. What we tend — the quality of the soil we offer, the faithfulness of the keeping — is what we have. The outcome is not ours to manufacture.

To carry today

Consider where in your life the word of God has been landing lately — and whether it has found room to stay. Not whether you have been hearing it, but whether you have been holding it: carrying it through an ordinary Tuesday, returning to it when something hard happens, letting it slowly change what you expect and what you fear. Perseverance is not a dramatic virtue, and that is exactly what makes it worth asking about today.
