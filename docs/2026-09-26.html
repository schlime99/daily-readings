Twenty-Fifth Week in Ordinary Time

Youth passes, death is coming, and the disciples are too afraid to ask what any of it means.

First readingEcclesiastes 11:9–12:8
PsalmPsalm 90:3-4, 5-6, 12-13, 14, 17
GospelLuke 9:43b-45

Two things sit side by side today, and at first they seem unrelated. Qoheleth, the great brooding voice of Ecclesiastes, watches a life arc from the bright energy of youth to the slow dimming of old age and then to death, and his conclusion is breathtaking in its honesty: vanity of vanities, all things are vanity. Then the disciples, walking with the Son of God himself, are told something world-altering, and they simply cannot bear to hear it — so they go silent and ask nothing. One text looks at the whole span of a human life. The other looks at a single moment of fear. Together they say something worth sitting with on a Saturday morning.

The first reading — Ecclesiastes 11:9–12:8

Ecclesiastes is a strange book to find in the Bible. Its author calls himself Qoheleth, which means something like "the gatherer" or "the assembler" — the one who gathers wisdom and sets it before the community. He is not a pessimist exactly, but he is a rigorous one, and he refuses to pretty things up. His great word is hebel, the Hebrew word translated here as "vanity," but the literal meaning is closer to breath, or vapor, or mist — something real but insubstantial, here and then gone.

This passage is the book's ending, and it reads like a long, gorgeous elegy. Qoheleth begins by telling the young man to enjoy his youth, to follow his heart, to let his eyes lead him — and he means it. He is not being sarcastic. He genuinely believes that gladness and pleasure are gifts. But then he adds the thing we tend to leave out of that advice: understand that God will call you to account for all of it. Enjoy, but know that the life you are enjoying is yours only briefly and belongs ultimately to the one who gave it.

Then comes the allegory of aging, one of the most sustained and beautiful pieces of writing in the Old Testament. The "guardians of the house" who tremble are the arms and hands; the "strong men" who are bent are the legs; the "grinders who are idle because they are few" are the teeth going missing; "they who look through the windows grow blind" — the eyes cloud over. The doors shut, meaning the ears stop hearing well. The "daughters of song" are suppressed — voice and music fade. One fears heights. The almond tree blooms white, like an old man's hair. The locust grows sluggish. The caper berry, which was understood as a stimulant for appetite and desire, loses its effect. And then, with unmistakable finality: "man goes to his lasting home, and mourners go about the streets." The body, which housed all that joy and seeing and hearing and desire, returns to dust. The life breath returns to God who gave it.

The psalm answers this directly. Psalm 90 is attributed to Moses, which is fitting — it is one of the oldest prayers in the Psalter, and it knows exactly where it stands before God. A thousand years in God's sight are like yesterday. Human beings spring up like grass at dawn and wilt by evening. And then, right in the middle of all that transience, comes the line that keeps the whole thing from despair: "Teach us to number our days aright, that we may gain wisdom of heart." Not "help us forget" and not "make us numb." Number them. Let their finiteness teach us something. And then: "Fill us at daybreak with your kindness" — the day is short, so let it begin well, with mercy already present.

The Gospel — Luke 9:43b-45

The setting here matters. The crowd is amazed at everything Jesus is doing. The air is charged with wonder. And into that charged moment, Jesus turns to his disciples — not the crowd, his disciples — and says something sobering: the Son of Man is going to be handed over. He is saying, quietly but directly, that he is going to be betrayed and killed.

And they do not understand. Luke says it plainly and without softening it: the meaning was hidden from them, and they were afraid to ask. This is a remarkable thing. Here are people walking with Jesus every day, hearing everything he says, witnessing the healings and the teaching — and when he speaks directly to them about the most important thing, they go silent out of fear. They are not stupid; they are afraid of what the answer might mean. The fear of knowing is sometimes stronger than ignorance, and that is one of the most honest observations the Gospels make about the human heart.

What youth, age, and incomprehension share

These two texts are not obviously connected by theme, but they both press toward the same honest confrontation with reality. Qoheleth insists on looking at the whole arc of life without flinching. The disciples cannot quite bring themselves to look at what is coming for Jesus, or for them. One text models unflinching attention; the other shows what it costs to avoid it. "Teach us to number our days aright" is both the psalm's prayer and the disciples' unspoken need — they cannot number what they refuse to see.

To carry today

Think about something you already half-know but have been afraid to ask about directly — a question in your prayer life, a truth about your health, a conversation you have been putting off. The disciples' silence is understandable, but Jesus had already told them not to be afraid. Ask the question today, even if you are not sure you are ready for the answer.
