Twentieth Week in Ordinary Time

The prince of Tyre calls himself a god, and the camel is still stuck at the needle's eye.

First readingEzekiel 28:1-10
PsalmDeuteronomy 32:26-27, 27-28, 30, 35-36
GospelMatthew 19:23-30

There is a kind of madness that comes on slowly, the way a person stops noticing how many times a day they say "I." The prince of Tyre did not wake up one morning and decide to commit blasphemy; he just kept succeeding, kept accumulating, kept being right — and somewhere in the long run of victories he stopped being able to tell the difference between himself and God. Today's readings put that madness on trial, and then Jesus walks his bewildered disciples right up to the edge of it.

The first reading — Ezekiel 28:1-10

Ezekiel is one of the stranger books in the Old Testament, written by a priest-prophet who was deported to Babylon in the first wave of exile, before Jerusalem even fell. He writes in visions, in enacted signs, in oracles against foreign nations — and the oracle against Tyre is among the most sustained and scalding things he ever delivers. Tyre was a Phoenician city-state built partly on an island in the Mediterranean, legendarily wealthy from trade, nearly impregnable to siege. Its prince had every reason to feel untouchable.

The accusation God brings against him is precise: it is not that he is wicked in the ordinary moral sense, though that may be true as well. It is that he has confused competence with divinity. "By your wisdom and your intelligence you have made riches for yourself" — and Ezekiel does not dispute the intelligence. The prince of Tyre really is clever. He really did build something extraordinary. The problem is that he read those achievements as proof of a different order of being, as though excellence were the same thing as transcendence. "A god am I," he says, and apparently he more or less means it.

The divine response has a darkly ironic quality. God does not argue with him philosophically; God simply promises to send foreigners with swords, and asks whether, in that moment, he will still say "I am a god." The rhetorical question is almost grim in its patience: you are a man, not a god, handed over to those who will slay you. The grandiosity collapses not through debate but through mortality. Death is the argument that cannot be answered by accumulation.

The psalm today is drawn from the Song of Moses in Deuteronomy — an unusual choice, since it is more often the Psalter that furnishes the responsorial. Its refrain, "It is I who deal death and give life," runs like a current beneath everything. The enemies of Israel boast, "Our own hand won the victory; the LORD had nothing to do with it" — and the psalm treats that claim the way Ezekiel treats the prince of Tyre: not with fury exactly, but with the calm certainty that the claim is absurd on its face. The one who actually holds life and death is not the one who thinks he does.

The Gospel — Matthew 19:23-30

This passage follows directly on the story of the rich young man, who came to Jesus genuinely seeking eternal life and then walked away sorrowful because he had many possessions. Jesus watches him go and says what he says about the camel and the needle, and the disciples are, as they so often are, completely astonished. Their astonishment is actually theologically interesting: they had absorbed the common assumption of their world, which was that wealth was a sign of God's favor. If the rich cannot be saved, then who can?

Jesus does not soften the image. The camel and the needle's eye is one of those phrases that has attracted centuries of attempts to make it more manageable — theories about a small gate in Jerusalem called "the needle," or a scribal variant reading "rope" instead of "camel." But the plain reading is the one that has always pressed hardest: Jesus means something that is impossible. And then he says exactly that. "For men this is impossible" — he does not walk it back. What he adds is not a loophole but a different kind of door altogether: "but for God all things are possible."

Peter, being Peter, hears the difficulty and immediately does the math on his own situation. "We have given up everything and followed you. What will there be for us?" It is a very human thing to ask, part anxiety and part bargaining, and Jesus does not rebuke him for it. He answers with generous specificity: thrones, a hundredfold return, eternal life. And then that final line, which cuts across the whole calculation: many who are first will be last, and the last will be first.

The pride that cannot receive

What connects these two readings is not wealth as such, but the particular self-sufficiency that wealth can produce — the sense that one has secured one's own position, that the outcome is already in hand, that there is nothing left to be given because one has already taken care of it. The prince of Tyre has put gold and silver in his treasuries and concluded he is a god. The rich young man has kept all the commandments and still, somehow, cannot open his hands. The disciples are alarmed because they assumed prosperity and salvation ran on the same track.

Jesus points toward a different economy entirely, one in which the impossible is not a problem to be solved by cleverness but a gift that arrives from outside the self. The hundredfold promise is not a better investment strategy. It is grace operating in a register that self-sufficiency cannot even perceive, because self-sufficiency is not looking in that direction.

To carry today

Think about where you feel most competent and most in control — the area of your life where things are, largely, working. Ask yourself whether that competence has quietly narrowed what you expect God to do there. The grace worth asking for today is the kind of openhandedness that stays curious about what might still be given, rather than assuming the account is already settled.
