Twenty-Third Week in Ordinary Time

You cannot drink from two cups at once — the question is which one you keep lifting.

First reading1 Corinthians 10:14-22
PsalmPsalm 116:12-13, 17-18
GospelLuke 6:43-49

Paul is asking the Corinthians to think hard about what they do with their bodies, and Jesus is asking his disciples to think hard about what they do with their lives. These are not the same question dressed up twice — they are two angles on one reality: that what you repeatedly do, you become, and what you become shapes everything around you.

The first reading — 1 Corinthians 10:14-22

Paul is writing to a church in one of the most religiously busy cities in the ancient world. Corinth was full of temples, and those temples were full of social life. When a wealthy patron sacrificed an animal to a god, the meat that wasn't burned went somewhere — often to a dinner party, often at the temple itself. For a Christian convert who still had business ties, family ties, social obligations in that world, the question of whether to attend one of these meals was genuinely pressing and genuinely complicated. Paul has been working through it across several chapters, and here he arrives at his sharpest point.

He doesn't come at it abstractly. He comes at it through the Eucharist. Look at what you do when you gather at the Lord's table, he says — you share the cup, you break the bread, and in doing so you are participating in something, you are being knit into something, you are not merely performing a ritual but entering a communion. "Because the loaf of bread is one, we, though many, are one body." The Eucharist is not a symbol of unity; it is the unity, enacted and made real. That's why what you do at other tables matters. Participation runs in both directions.

Paul's point about idols is careful: he is not saying the statues are real gods. He says flatly that an idol "is nothing." But he does say that something is happening when people worship at those altars — something he names, surprisingly, as demonic. He means that false worship, whatever its object, pulls a person into a current that runs away from God rather than toward him. You can't be simultaneously drawn in two opposite directions and pretend the tension doesn't exist. "Are we stronger than him?" — that last line is almost wry, a small cold splash of water.

Psalm 116 answers Paul's argument from the other side. The psalmist asks, "How shall I make a return to the Lord for all the good he has done for me?" and then answers the question himself: "The cup of salvation I will take up." The cup Paul has been describing — the eucharistic cup — is exactly this. It is the response. The whole movement of worship is a return of love for love, and it requires the whole self.

The Gospel — Luke 6:43-49

These two short parables come at the end of what Luke calls the Sermon on the Plain — Jesus's long teaching to his disciples about what his kingdom looks like in practice. By the time we arrive here, Jesus has told them to love enemies, to give without counting, to stop judging, to examine the plank in their own eye. Now he makes the conclusion explicit: eventually, the tree shows its fruit. Not because you try very hard at fruit-bearing, but because of what is already inside you. "From the fullness of the heart the mouth speaks."

The house parable is vivid in a way that people in flood-prone regions would have recognized immediately. In Palestine, dry streambeds — called wadis — could fill with terrifying speed after rain in the hills. A house that looked fine in summer was a gamble. The man who digs down to bedrock is not doing something exotic; he is doing the sensible, costly, slow thing. The man who builds on the surface is not foolish-looking in the short run. He saves time, he saves effort, and his house stands just as well — until it doesn't.

Jesus is talking about hearing and doing. Not about learning more, not about feeling more moved, but about whether what you hear from him actually changes the structure of how you live. The house doesn't stand because the builder had warm feelings about the rock.

What Paul and Jesus are both pressing on

These two readings are not carefully paired to make a single theological point — this is an Ordinary Time weekday, and they come from different tracks. But they turn out to be genuinely in conversation. Both are concerned with the gap between what you profess and what you actually practice, between the table you say is central and the life you actually build. Paul asks whether you can share the Lord's cup and then keep lifting other cups as though it doesn't matter. Jesus asks whether you can call him Lord and then build your house on sand as though his words were optional. The challenge in both cases is not to believe more fervently but to let belief reach all the way down into behavior.

To carry today

Think about the thing you know you should stop doing, or start doing, that you have been treating as optional — something you have heard clearly but not yet acted on. The structure of your life is being built every day, stone by stone. What would it look like to dig one layer deeper today, toward something more solid than good intentions?
