Twenty-sixth Week in Ordinary Time

Job sees at last, and the disciples see what kings and prophets longed for — and both fall quiet.

First readingJob 42:1-3, 5-6, 12-17
PsalmPsalm 119:66, 71, 75, 91, 125, 130
GospelLuke 10:17-24

There is a particular kind of knowing that comes not from argument but from encounter. Job has been arguing for thirty-nine chapters — with his friends, with his own anguish, with God — and now, at the end, the arguments simply stop. The disciples have been out on the road, and they come back astonished at what they have seen. Both readings today circle around the same quiet mystery: what happens when you stop talking long enough to actually see.

The first reading — Job 42:1-3, 5-6, 12-17

The Book of Job is one of the strangest and most honest books in the entire Bible. For most of its length, Job sits in ash and rubble, having lost his children, his property, and his health, while his friends explain — confidently, theologically, incorrectly — that he must have done something to deserve it. Job refuses to accept their tidy reasoning and demands an audience with God. He gets one. God speaks from the whirlwind in chapters 38 through 41, and what God says is not an explanation but a torrent of questions: Where were you when I laid the foundations of the earth? Not cruelty — revelation. God is not dodging Job's suffering; God is showing Job the sheer scale of a reality Job had been trying to fit inside his own categories.

And Job, remarkably, does not feel crushed by this. He feels seen. "I had heard of you by word of mouth, but now my eye has seen you." This is the hinge of the whole book. Everything before was secondhand — theology received, ideas about God, frameworks inherited. What the whirlwind gave him was contact. He says he disowns what he said and repents in dust and ashes, but notice that God has already told the friends that Job spoke rightly while they did not. Job's repentance here is not the admission that he sinned and deserved his suffering. It is the natural posture of someone who has just been in the presence of something so vast that all their previous certainties feel small. You bow not because you were wrong to ask, but because you now see how much larger the answer is than any question you could have formed.

The lectionary then skips ahead to the restoration — the sheep and camels and oxen, the seven sons and three daughters with their beautiful and unusual names, and the detail, striking for its time, that Job gave his daughters an inheritance alongside their brothers. Job lives another hundred and forty years and dies old and full of years. This ending has troubled readers who want suffering to remain suffering, who worry that a happy ending papers over the real anguish of the earlier chapters. But the restoration is not a refund; it is not as if the first children were returned. It is something new, given on the other side of the dark. The Psalm chosen for today fits exactly: "It is good for me that I have been afflicted, that I may learn your statutes." That is not a claim that suffering is good in itself, but that something was received through it that could not have arrived another way. The psalm's refrain — Lord, let your face shine on me — is exactly what Job received when everything else was taken.

The Gospel — Luke 10:17-24

The seventy-two disciples are back from their mission, and they are delighted. Demons fled when they spoke in Jesus's name, and they want Jesus to know about it. His response is layered. He confirms what happened — he saw it, he tells them, he watched Satan fall like lightning from the sky, which is an astonishing image, the power of evil toppling suddenly and completely. He tells them they have real authority. And then, gently, he redirects them: do not rejoice because the spirits are subject to you, but rejoice because your names are written in heaven. He is not scolding them; he is protecting them from a particular kind of spiritual mistake. Power is a dazzling thing to discover, and the disciples could easily spend the rest of their lives pursuing the experience of that power. But that is not the ground of their joy, and it would be a brittle thing to build on. Their joy rests on something God has done, not something they have demonstrated.

Then comes one of the most intimate moments in Luke's Gospel. Jesus rejoices in the Holy Spirit — the text actually says that, that Jesus rejoiced — and prays aloud in front of them, thanking the Father for hiding these things from the wise and learned and revealing them to the childlike. He is not disparaging intelligence; he is naming the disposition that makes reception possible. The learned can be so busy explaining that they never arrive at seeing. Then he turns to his disciples privately and says something that ought to stop everyone reading it cold: many prophets and kings longed to see what you are seeing right now, and never did.

What Job and the disciples both discovered

These two readings are on separate tracks in the lectionary — Job is working through a long sequential reading that has been running all week, and Luke's Gospel is simply where the weekday cycle lands today. But they are, unexpectedly, about the same thing. Both Job and the disciples pass from secondhand knowledge to direct encounter. Job heard about God and then saw God. The disciples had heard and hoped, had walked with Jesus and watched, and now something broke through into their actual experience. In both cases the response is not pride but wonder.

The childlike quality Jesus names is not naivety; it is openness — the willingness to receive what is actually being given rather than insisting on the version you had already prepared. Job had to be broken open before he could be that available. The disciples, still fresh and astonished, have it almost by accident, and Jesus is telling them to stay there.

To carry today

Think of something you believe about God that you have believed for a long time, something you could explain clearly and quickly. Ask yourself honestly whether it has ever made the transit from something you know to something you have experienced — whether it is still inherited, or whether it has become your own. You do not have to manufacture an experience; just sit with the difference, and let the question do its work.
