Twenty-first Sunday in Ordinary Time

A key is placed on Peter's shoulder, and the gates of death are told they will not win.

First readingIsaiah 22:19-23
PsalmPsalm 138:1-2, 2-3, 6, 8
Second readingRomans 11:33-36
GospelMatthew 16:13-20

There is a moment in the Gospel today when Jesus stops walking and turns to the people closest to him and asks the question that is, in a sense, the only question: who do you say that I am? The whole Church was born in that pause. It is worth sitting in the weight of it for a moment before moving on to what happened next.

The first reading — Isaiah 22:19-23

To hear this passage clearly, it helps to know where it falls in Isaiah. Chapter 22 is a chapter of national crisis — Jerusalem is under threat, the city is panicking, and the prophet is furious at the leadership for its response. Shebna, who holds the title "master of the palace," is the chief steward of the king's household. This was not a minor role. The master of the palace managed access to the king, administered the royal estates, and functioned as something like a prime minister. He was the man who held the key.

In the ancient Near East, a large key was sometimes literally carried on the shoulder — it was a symbol of office, worn publicly, announcing who had the authority to open and shut the king's house. So when God says through Isaiah that he will remove Shebna and place that key on the shoulder of Eliakim instead, the image would have landed with full political force on everyone listening. This is a transfer of power, and God is the one making it.

What makes the passage remarkable is its specificity. God is not speaking in grand abstractions about authority and justice. He is talking about two named people, in a named city, in a particular crisis. And the promise attached to Eliakim is worth pausing on: "I will fix him like a peg in a sure spot." This is practical, almost domestic language — the kind of peg you hang a coat on, something sturdy and useful that holds things together. It is not the language of glory; it is the language of reliable service. That peg, though, becomes "a place of honor for his family."

Psalm 138 follows naturally from this. Its refrain — "Lord, your love is eternal; do not forsake the work of your hands" — is the prayer of someone who has seen God act in human affairs and trusts that he will continue to do so. The psalm is not afraid to pair exaltation with lowliness: "The LORD is exalted, yet the lowly he sees." That paradox sits directly underneath both the Isaiah reading and what Jesus will say to Peter.

The second reading — Romans 11:33-36

Paul's exclamation here is not decorative. He has spent eleven dense chapters of Romans working through the problem of Israel's history, God's faithfulness, and the mystery of how Gentiles have been grafted into the covenant. Having arrived at something like an answer — which is really an acknowledgment that the answer is beyond him — he breaks into this doxology. The depth he is exclaiming over is not a vague spiritual depth; it is the depth of a specific problem he has wrestled with and not fully solved. The passage stands somewhat on its own track from the other two readings today, but it is not out of place. If anything, it is a note of honesty: do not expect the logic of God's choices to be fully legible to us. The key goes where it goes.

The Gospel — Matthew 16:13-20

Caesarea Philippi is worth knowing about. It was a city built at the foot of a great rock cliff, inside which was a cave that pagans called the gate of the underworld — a place where the god Pan was worshipped and where, according to legend, the waters of the Jordan had their source. It was, in the geography of the region, a place deliberately associated with the powers of the earth and the realm of the dead. Jesus chooses this backdrop, or Matthew preserves this backdrop, to ask the defining question of the whole Gospel.

Peter's answer — "You are the Christ, the Son of the living God" — is met with something unexpected: not just approval, but a return gift. Jesus renames him. Simon becomes Peter, which means rock, and in the same breath Jesus announces that on this rock he will build his church. Then the keys appear. "I will give you the keys to the kingdom of heaven." The echo of Isaiah is unmistakable and deliberate. What Eliakim was to the house of David — a steward, the holder of the key, a peg in a sure spot — Peter is to the household of God.

Keys and rocks: what authority looks like in these texts

The thread that connects Isaiah and Matthew is not an institutional argument about leadership; it is a picture of how God works. He entrusts real authority to fallible human beings, named and specific, for the sake of communities that need someone to hold the door. Shebna is removed not because authority is bad but because he held it badly. Eliakim will hold it well. Peter, for his part, does not hold it well in every chapter that follows — he will deny Jesus three times before the rooster stops crowing. And yet the keys stay on his shoulder. That is not naivety on Jesus's part. It is something closer to the logic Paul is gasping at in Romans: God's ways are not ours, and what looks like a shaky foundation can turn out to be a rock.

To carry today

Think today about the authority you carry in your own small sphere — in a household, a workplace, a friendship, a role you did not entirely choose. Ask whether you are holding the door open or keeping it shut, and for whom. The same question God put to Shebna is one worth sitting with quietly: what is this authority actually for?
