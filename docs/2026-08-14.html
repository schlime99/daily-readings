Friday · 14 August

Memorial of

Saint Maximilian Kolbe, Priest and Martyr

God clothed a castaway in silk and swore an oath — and kept it anyway.

First readingEzekiel 16:1-15, 60, 63
PsalmIsaiah 12:2-3, 4bcd, 5-6
GospelMatthew 19:3-12

Today the Church remembers a man who walked into a starvation bunker in Auschwitz so that another man could go home to his family. Maximilian Kolbe's choice was not an impulse — it was the end of a long life of believing that love is not a sentiment but a decision that costs something. The readings today don't speak about him directly, but they speak the language he lived.

The first reading — Ezekiel 16:1-15, 60, 63

Ezekiel is one of the great strange prophets, and this chapter may be the strangest passage in his book. He is writing to the exiles in Babylon, people who have watched Jerusalem fall and are now trying to make sense of the catastrophe. His method is shocking: he tells the story of Israel as the story of an abandoned infant girl, left in a field with her umbilical cord uncut, lying in her own blood, nobody bothering to wash or swaddle her.

The image is meant to be uncomfortable. Israel has no ancient dignity to stand on, no prestigious origin. The Amorites and Hittites were Canaan's peoples — not Israel's proud ancestors, but the very nations Israel was meant to displace. Ezekiel is stripping away any story Israel might tell about deserving God's attention. They were, in the beginning, nothing and nobody.

And then the Lord passes by. That detail is quiet and almost offhand — I passed by and saw you — but it carries everything. Nobody else stopped. God does. He speaks a single word — Live — and she does. Later he passes by again, and she has grown, and he spreads the corner of his cloak over her. That gesture, spreading a cloak, is a formal act of betrothal in the ancient Near East. It is what Boaz will do for Ruth. God is not rescuing a city. He is taking a bride.

What follows is extravagant: embroidered robes, fine leather sandals, gold bracelets, a diadem, the food of a queen. The beauty is entirely given — the text is explicit that her splendor was his splendor bestowed on her. And then comes the turn that makes the whole thing so painful: she took that beauty and traded it away, one passer-by at a time.

The lectionary skips from verse 15 to verse 60, and the jump is merciful. Verses 16 through 59 are a catalog of infidelity that goes on at length and without softening. But we land in verse 60 on a sentence that resets the whole account: Yet I will remember the covenant I made with you when you were a girl. Not — you have now earned your way back. Not — the record has been balanced. But: I remember. I swore. The covenant holds from my side even when it has been shattered from yours.

The psalm today, drawn from Isaiah 12, is Israel's song after the anger has turned: You have turned from your anger — God indeed is my savior, I am confident and unafraid. It belongs right here, answering Ezekiel's anguished story with the end of the story, the joy that stands on the other side of shame.

The Gospel — Matthew 19:3-12

The Pharisees are testing Jesus, and their question about divorce is a trap with a history behind it. Two respected schools of thought disagreed sharply: one held that divorce was permitted only for serious cause, the other that a man could divorce his wife for something as minor as burning his dinner. The Pharisees want to know which camp Jesus is in — and perhaps to catch him in an unpopular position.

Jesus does something neither camp expected: he goes behind Moses entirely. He reaches back to Genesis, to the moment before any law was given, and says that the original intention was union — not contract, not arrangement, but one flesh. Moses permitted divorce, Jesus says plainly, because of hardness of hearts, which is to say he was legislating for people who could not yet be what they were made to be. But the beginning tells us what we were made for, and that stands.

The disciples' reaction is revealing and a little funny: then it's better not to marry at all. They understand that what Jesus is describing is a high and demanding thing, and they instinctively reach for the exit. Jesus doesn't scold them. He says, honestly, that this way of life is not for everyone — some will be called to marriage as he has described it, some will be called to celibacy for the Kingdom, and each is given what they can accept. It is not a loophole. It is a recognition that different callings require different graces.

What covenant and fidelity share

Both readings today are, at their heart, about what it means to hold to a promise when everything makes abandonment easier. In Ezekiel, God holds the covenant when the other party has long since walked away. In Matthew, Jesus restores marriage to its original shape as covenant rather than contract — something you hold not because you have calculated the cost but because you swore an oath.

Maximilian Kolbe swore an oath at his ordination — to give his life to God and, through God, to others. On July 30, 1941, when a man named Franciszek Gajowniczek was selected for the starvation bunker, Kolbe stepped forward and said, I am a Catholic priest. Let me take his place. This is what covenant looks like when it is not a feeling but a fact — something more durable than fear.

To carry today

Think of a promise you have made — to a person, to God, to your own better self — that has grown harder to keep than it once seemed. Ask today whether what is wearing you down is the promise itself, or simply the cost of holding to it. Kolbe found that even the hardest fidelity was not carried alone.
