Seventeenth Sunday in Ordinary Time

Solomon asks for one thing; the merchant sells everything — both know what is worth wanting.

First reading1 Kings 3:5, 7-12
PsalmPsalm 119:57, 72, 76-77, 127-128, 129-130
Second readingRomans 8:28-30
GospelMatthew 13:44-52

There is a particular kind of clarity that comes over a person when they finally know what they want. Not what they want to want, not what they think they should want — but the thing itself, seen plain, and recognized as worth any cost. Today's readings circle this experience from different angles, and together they raise a question worth sitting with all week: do I actually know what I am looking for?

The first reading — 1 Kings 3:5, 7-12

This passage arrives early in the reign of Solomon, David's son, and the scene is a dream. God appears and offers Solomon anything — which is the kind of sentence that stops you cold when you read it slowly. Anything. The offer is real; this is not a riddle or a test of nerve. It is a blank check extended to a new king who has just inherited the most powerful throne in the region and who, by his own admission, is young, uncertain, and overwhelmed.

What follows is one of the most quietly remarkable moments in the Hebrew scriptures. Solomon does not ask for long life, or wealth, or victory over his enemies — the three things a sensible advisor would have told him to want. Instead he asks for an understanding heart. The Hebrew behind that phrase suggests something closer to a listening heart, a heart that can hear what a situation truly requires, not just what it appears to require on the surface. He wants to be able to tell right from wrong in the complex, tangled, human business of governing real people with real grievances.

God's pleasure at this response is explicit and almost touching. Because Solomon did not ask for the things he could reasonably have wanted — and which God would presumably have granted — he receives something rarer. A wisdom so deep that, the text says, no one before him had it and no one after him will equal it.

Psalm 119 answers this directly. It is the great long psalm of love for God's law, and the verses chosen today strike exactly the same note: the psalmist prizes God's commands above silver and gold. Not as a pious sentiment, but as a tested judgment — the law gives light, gives direction, gives life. The psalm and the first reading together are making a claim: that wisdom, properly understood, is not a human achievement but a gift that flows from loving what God loves.

The second reading — Romans 8:28-30

Paul's letter to the Romans is moving, in these middle chapters, through a dense and beautiful argument about what God has been doing in human history — and in individual human lives — from the very beginning. These three verses are among the most consoling in the entire New Testament, and they are also, if you look at them straight, among the most dizzying. Paul strings together a chain of verbs — foreknew, predestined, called, justified, glorified — and each one carries the weight of a whole theology. He is not promising that good things will happen to people who love God; he is saying that the whole sweep of a life, including its hard and confusing stretches, is held inside a purpose larger than any single moment can reveal.

This reading is on its own track today, in the sense that it does not tie neatly into the images of the kingdom from Solomon and Matthew. But it sets a frame around them. If God's call and purpose are as comprehensive as Paul says, then the longing Solomon feels for wisdom, and the longing the merchant feels for the pearl, are not random. They are part of something being worked out from a direction we cannot fully see.

The Gospel — Matthew 13:44-52

This is the third and final Sunday in Matthew's great parable chapter, and Jesus offers three parables here in quick succession. The treasure hidden in a field, the pearl of great price, and the net — and then a small coda about the scribe who is like a householder drawing from a storeroom both old things and new.

The treasure and the pearl are often read as a pair, and they are, but they have a subtle difference that is worth noticing. The man who finds the treasure stumbles on it — he was not searching for it specifically; it was just there, in a field he happened to be working. The merchant, on the other hand, is a professional seeker. He has been looking for fine pearls his whole career. One man finds by accident; the other finds by long, intentional search. But the response is identical: both sell everything they have, and do so with what the text quietly calls joy. This is not anguish or sacrifice in the tragic sense. It is the relief of a person who has finally found the thing that makes all other things make sense, and who parts with them not grudgingly but gladly.

The net parable that follows is darker — it acknowledges that not everything hauled in is worth keeping, and that a sorting will come. Jesus is not letting his disciples off the hook entirely. But notice that he ends not with the sorting but with the scribe and the storeroom: the one who has learned the kingdom draws on everything, old wisdom and new revelation alike, and finds use for all of it.

What wisdom costs — and what it frees

Solomon gives up the easier asks. The merchant gives up his inventory. What both gain in return is not a thing they can hoard but a capacity — to see rightly, to judge rightly, to know what matters. That is the connection the readings are making: real wisdom is recognizable by what it makes a person willing to release. The man who clutches his gold and his silver and his long life at the expense of understanding has not made a wise trade. He has made the trade of someone who does not yet know what the field contains.

To carry today

Think about what you are actually asking for when you pray — what you hope for, what you would choose if the choice were laid bare. Are those the things that would make you more capable of love, more able to see what is right, more free? That is not a rebuke; it is an honest question worth bringing to the quiet of the day.
