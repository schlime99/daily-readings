Twenty-sixth Week in Ordinary Time

Job loses everything in a single afternoon and still will not curse the God who gave it.

First readingJob 1:6-22
PsalmPsalm 17:1, 2-3, 6-7
GospelLuke 9:46-50

The Book of Job opens this week in the liturgy, and it is worth pausing before we go any further to recognize what kind of book we are entering. Job is not a historical account; it is a sustained, fierce poem about innocent suffering — one of the most daring texts in all of Scripture, a book willing to argue with God out loud and in public. The lectionary gives us just the first chapter today, the prose prologue that sets the catastrophe in motion, and already the writing is extraordinary.

The first reading — Job 1:6-22

The scene that opens this passage is one of the strangest in the whole Bible: a kind of divine assembly, where "the angels of God" — literally, in the Hebrew, the "sons of God," meaning members of the heavenly court — come before the Lord, and among them walks Satan. This "Satan" is not quite the figure of later Christian imagination; the Hebrew word means "the adversary" or "the accuser," and here he functions more like a prosecuting attorney than the prince of evil. He is a character in a courtroom drama, and the courtroom is the heavens themselves.

God begins almost bragging about Job: there is no one like him on the earth, blameless, upright, genuinely God-fearing. The adversary's counter is cynical and pointed — Job is devout because devotion has paid off handsomely. Strip away the prosperity, and watch what happens to the piety. It is a question that cuts deep because it cuts at all of us: would I love God if it cost me something? Would I pray if nothing ever seemed to come of it?

God accepts the challenge — which is itself a mystery the text does not explain — and Job's world collapses in a single terrible afternoon. The messengers arrive one after another, each with worse news than the last, each barely finishing a sentence before the next one appears at the door. It is constructed to be almost unbearable: the cattle are gone, the sheep are gone, the camels are gone, and then — while this last messenger is still speaking — the children are dead. The storytelling technique is deliberate: the rhythm of it, the relentless accumulation, does not let the reader, or Job, catch a breath.

And then Job does something astonishing. He tears his robe and cuts off his hair — the ancient gestures of mourning, grief given its full physical expression — and then he falls to the ground and blesses God. "The Lord gave and the Lord has taken away; blessed be the name of the Lord." This is not a man suppressing his grief; the torn robe and the cut hair show that the grief is real and raw. This is a man who grieves fully and does not let the grief become a verdict against God. Psalm 17, which answers this reading, fits the moment almost perfectly — the psalm of a man who calls on God in extremity, who knows he is being tested, and who refuses to conclude from the testing that God has turned away. "I call upon you, for you will answer me, O God." That "for" is doing a great deal of work: it is an act of will, a choice to hold on.

The Gospel — Luke 9:46-50

The disciples in this brief passage are caught in a moment that is almost endearing in its smallness. They have been traveling with Jesus, watching him heal, watching him transfigure on a mountain, hearing him speak of his coming death — and among themselves they are arguing about which one of them is the greatest. Luke says Jesus "realized the intention of their hearts," which is a delicate way of saying he knew perfectly well what they were squabbling about before anyone said a word.

His response is characteristic: he places a child beside him. In the ancient world a child had no social standing whatsoever — no status, no influence, no claim to greatness. To receive a child, to attend to a child, is to practice greatness in the only form Jesus seems to recognize: the form of service rendered to someone who cannot benefit you. The one who is least among you is the greatest. This is not a paradox meant to be admired from a distance; it is a description of the actual shape of love.

Then John raises a second question — they have been preventing someone from casting out demons in Jesus' name because he is not in their group. This follows naturally from the argument about greatness: if we are the great ones, then the work belongs to us. Jesus dismisses the logic entirely. Whoever is not against you is for you. The kingdom is larger than the company.

Where the readings meet: the grip of self

These two passages are not artificially paired, but they do share a common pressure point. Job's test is whether self-interest is the secret engine of his faith — whether he loves God only because God has been good to him. The disciples' failure is the same mechanism running in a smaller register: they are measuring themselves against each other, protecting their position, managing their status in the group. In both cases the question underneath is: what are you actually holding onto, and why? Job's answer, given at devastating cost, is that he is holding onto something that cannot be taken. The disciples have not learned that yet. Neither, if we are honest, have most of us on most days.

To carry today

Think today about whether there is someone you have been treating as a rival, or someone you have tried to exclude from something good because they are not part of your circle. Consider what it would look like to simply get out of the way and let them do the good they are trying to do. Ask yourself whether your faith would survive a season when nothing seemed to be going right — and if the answer makes you uneasy, let that unease be the beginning of a more honest prayer.
