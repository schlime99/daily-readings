Wednesday · 4 November

Memorial of

Saint Charles Borromeo, Bishop

Jesus asks for everything — and Paul says that giving it is somehow cause for joy.

First readingPhilippians 2:12-18
PsalmPsalm 27:1, 4, 13-14
GospelLuke 14:25-33

Today the Church remembers Charles Borromeo, the sixteenth-century Archbishop of Milan who sold his household furniture to feed the starving and walked barefoot through his city during the plague. He is an unlikely mirror for the Gospel — a man of immense privilege who actually did calculate the cost of following Christ and paid it. That the readings assigned for his memorial press so hard on renunciation and sacrifice is probably not a coincidence.

The first reading — Philippians 2:12-18

By this point in his letter to the Philippians, Paul has already given them what may be the most compressed and beautiful account of the Incarnation in all of scripture — the famous Christ-hymn in which the Son empties himself and becomes a servant. Now he applies that movement to their own lives. He writes from prison, almost certainly in Rome, and he writes with the calm warmth of a man who has stopped pretending that circumstances control him.

"Work out your salvation with fear and trembling" is a phrase that gets misread. It is not an instruction to be anxious. The Greek behind "fear and trembling" in Paul's usage is closer to what we might call reverence — the seriousness and attentiveness a surgeon brings to an open chest, or that a musician brings to a difficult passage she has practiced for years. It is the opposite of carelessness, not the opposite of peace. And Paul immediately explains why peace is possible: "God is the one who, for his good purpose, works in you both to desire and to work." The labor is real, and so is the help. These are not in contradiction.

Paul then asks them to "shine like lights in the world," and the image is worth a moment. He is borrowing from Daniel's vision of the righteous who shine like stars at the end of time — but he plants it in the present, in the middle of ordinary, crooked, grumbling life. Not later, when everything is easier. Now.

The passage closes with something startling. Paul says that even if he is "poured out as a libation" on top of their faith — that is, even if his death is added to their offering as a kind of additional gift — he rejoices. He wants them to rejoice with him. Psalm 27, which the congregation sings in response, holds the same posture: "I believe that I shall see the bounty of the LORD in the land of the living. Wait for the LORD with courage." The psalm is for people who are genuinely afraid and have decided to trust anyway. It is not the absence of trouble; it is light in the middle of it.

The Gospel — Luke 14:25-33

Great crowds are traveling with Jesus toward Jerusalem, and he turns around and says something that sounds designed to send them home. This is Luke's pattern: Jesus is not an easy teacher who flatters his audience into staying. He looks at the crowd and tells them what it actually costs.

The word "hating" in "hating his father and mother" has stopped readers in their tracks since the first century, and it should. It is a Semitic idiom of comparison — to love one thing so much more than another that the second, by comparison, seems like hatred. Jesus is not asking his followers to feel contempt for their families. He is asking them to be honest about which loyalty governs when the two come into conflict. The question is one of ultimate allegiance, and he presses it hard enough that there is no comfortable middle.

Then he gives two illustrations, and they are both practical to the point of being almost businesslike. A builder who begins a tower without counting the cost becomes a laughingstock. A king who marches to war without calculating his odds had better negotiate before the battle, not after. The implied point is that half-commitment is worse than no commitment. Someone who starts and quits has less standing, in a way, than someone who never started. Jesus is not trying to lower the number of disciples by frightening people off. He is trying to ensure that the ones who come have actually thought about it.

What Paul and Jesus are asking for

These two readings are on the same track, though they arrive from different directions. Paul describes a life that has already been surrendered — a community that works, and shines, and rejoices, even when their teacher is in chains. Jesus lays out the terms of surrender before anyone signs. Together they frame the same reality: discipleship is not an add-on. It reorganizes everything, the same way that building a tower reorganizes a budget or war reorganizes a kingdom.

What makes this bearable — what keeps it from being crushing — is Paul's testimony that the reorganization produces joy. Not relief, not mere duty, but joy, the kind he wants to share and wants them to share back. Charles Borromeo's biography reads like a man who found out that this was true. He gave away his wealth and his comfort, and the accounts of his life suggest he was not miserable. He was lit up. The saints have always been a little embarrassing about this.

To carry today

Think today about one area of your life where you are keeping a reservation — something you are willing to give to God only if he doesn't actually ask for it. You do not need to solve it or surrender it this morning. Simply name it honestly and sit with it. That kind of honesty is often where the real conversation begins.
