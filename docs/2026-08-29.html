Memorial of the

Passion of Saint John the Baptist

A man who would not be silent is killed to spare a king the embarrassment of backing down.

First reading1 Corinthians 1:26-31
PsalmPsalm 33:12-13, 18-19, 20-21
GospelMark 6:17-29

Today the Church pauses to remember not the birth of John the Baptist — that feast came in June — but his death: the ugly, politically convenient execution of a man whose only offense was telling the truth to power. It is a hard memorial, and it is supposed to be. The Church keeps it in red, the color of blood, because John's death is not a tragedy to be softened but a witness to be honored.

The first reading — 1 Corinthians 1:26-31

Paul is writing to Corinth, a city dazzled by status, rhetoric, and the social hierarchies that governed the ancient Mediterranean world. The Corinthian church was apparently sorting itself along the same lines — some members convinced that their wisdom, their connections, or their spiritual gifts put them above the rest. Paul's response is almost deliberately deflating: look around at yourselves, he says. Not many wise, not many powerful, not many well-born. This community, which fancies itself sophisticated, is mostly made up of people the world does not bother to count.

But that is precisely the point. God chose the foolish, the weak, the lowly, the despised — Paul stacks the adjectives and then adds one more cutting phrase: "those who count for nothing." He is not being cruel. He is making a theological argument. The gospel cannot be a human achievement dressed up in divine robes, cannot be the product of the right education or the right bloodline, because then the glory would go to the human. So God works through the ones no one expects, and the credit cannot be taken by the instruments.

The passage ends with a quotation from Jeremiah: whoever boasts should boast in the Lord. Paul means it concretely. Our standing before God is not something we constructed — it is a gift. Christ has become our wisdom, our righteousness, our sanctification, our redemption. Every one of those words is doing work: they cover every category of human need, every way we fall short. We have been given the whole thing. Boasting about it would be like boasting that you have an inheritance.

Psalm 33 answers this with quiet confidence. The eyes of the Lord are on those who fear him, who hope for his kindness. The psalm is not about achieving the Lord's attention but about resting in the fact that you already have it.

The Gospel — Mark 6:17-29

Mark's account of John's death is gripping and a little grim, and it is worth sitting with the details. Herod Antipas — not the Herod of the infancy narratives but his son, ruler of Galilee — had married Herodias, who had been his brother Philip's wife. John said publicly that this violated the law, and Herodias never forgave him for it. She wanted him dead. Herod, strangely, did not — he feared John, Mark says, recognizing him as righteous and holy. He kept John in prison but also kept summoning him to talk. The text says Herod was perplexed when John spoke, and yet he liked listening to him. There is something almost poignant in this: a man drawn toward a truth he cannot quite bring himself to receive.

The birthday banquet is where everything collapses. Herod, surrounded by his officers and the leading men of Galilee, makes a reckless public oath to a young girl who has just danced for the room. When she asks her mother what to request, Herodias takes her moment. The girl delivers the answer: the head of John the Baptist, and please make it prompt. Mark notes that Herod was "deeply distressed." He did not want this. But he had made his oath in front of witnesses, and the social cost of appearing weak before his court was, in his judgment, higher than the moral cost of ordering a murder. So a righteous man dies because a king cannot stand to lose face.

This is the mechanism the gospel has always been up against: not cackling evil but mundane calculation, the preference for public dignity over private conscience.

What the Corinthian letter lights up in the execution of John

These two readings are not from the same liturgical cycle — on memorials the first reading is often chosen for a connection to the saint, and here it is pointed. Paul's argument in Corinthians is that God works through those the world counts for nothing. John is the proof. He was not powerful, not well-connected, not a man who could protect himself. He was a voice in the wilderness, and then a voice in a prison cell, and then no voice at all — just a head on a platter handed from a girl to her mother.

The world counted him for nothing, and then reduced him to nothing. But the Church calls him the greatest of the prophets. He prepared the way for the Word, and the Word remembers him. The boast is the Lord's, and the Lord's accounting is different from Herod's.

To carry today

Think today about a moment when you stayed quiet because speaking would have cost you something — a friendship, approval, the comfort of the room. You do not need to judge yourself harshly for it; most of us know that moment. Ask instead what it would look like to care a little less about how you appear, a little more about what is true. That is not one decision made once; it is a disposition slowly built.
