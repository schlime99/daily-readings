Twenty-fifth Week in Ordinary Time

Herod beheaded John, yet still cannot explain who Jesus is.

GospelLuke 9:7-9

Qoheleth looks at the world — its spinning sun, its cycling winds, its rivers that pour endlessly into a sea that never fills — and calls it all vanity. Herod looks at Jesus and cannot figure out who he is, though the question is eating at him. These two readings are on separate tracks; the Church's lectionary cycles have brought them to the same table on the same morning without designing a neat connection. But sitting with each on its own terms, you find that they are both, in their very different ways, about what it means to be confronted with something you cannot contain.

The first reading — Ecclesiastes 1:2-11

Ecclesiastes is one of the stranger books in the Bible, and it is worth knowing why. Its author calls himself Qoheleth, which means something like "the one who addresses the assembly" — a teacher, a preacher, a convener of minds. He writes as a man who has tried everything: wisdom, work, pleasure, achievement. And again and again he arrives at the same word: hebel, the Hebrew word translated here as "vanity." That word literally means breath, or vapor — the kind of thing that appears in the cold air for a moment and is gone. When Qoheleth says "vanity of vanities," he is not using a word that means wicked or immoral. He means: insubstantial, like trying to hold mist in your fist.

What strikes him most is the relentlessness of natural cycles without any corresponding progress. The sun makes its rounds. The wind blows south, then north, turning and turning, resuming its rounds. The rivers pour into the sea and the sea never fills, so the rivers pour in again. There is a kind of grim comedy in this image — all that effort, all that constant motion, and the sea just sits there, unmoved, never full. The eye looks and looks and is not satisfied. The ear listens and keeps listening. Even speech, Qoheleth says, is labored — there is nothing new to say.

And then the sentence that hits hardest: "There is no remembrance of the men of old; nor of those to come will there be any remembrance among those who come after them." He is not denying that people existed or built things or had children. He is saying that the world forgets. Memory is short. The generations pass and the world stays, indifferent.

Psalm 90 answers this directly, and it is one of the most stunning moves in the liturgy — placing, right after Qoheleth's meditation on human forgetting, a prayer that begins with a simple act of faith: In every age, O Lord, you have been our refuge. The psalm also sits with the passing of time, also watches the grass spring up at dawn and wilt by evening. But it does not end in emptiness. It asks for something: "Teach us to number our days aright, that we may gain wisdom of heart." This is the turn. The point is not to deny that time passes or that memory fails, but to live inside that truth wisely — with our eyes open, holding our days carefully rather than burning through them.

The Gospel — Luke 9:7-9

This passage is brief to the point of being almost a footnote — three verses, a snapshot of Herod Antipas (the tetrarch, which means ruler of a quarter of the territory) hearing the rumors about Jesus and being baffled. Some people are saying John the Baptist has been raised from the dead. Others say Elijah has appeared. Others say one of the old prophets has come back. Luke gives us Herod's response in two pieces: a statement and a question. "John I beheaded." Full stop. He knows what he did. "Who then is this about whom I hear such things?" He cannot make the categories fit.

What is interesting is how Luke ends it: "And he kept trying to see him." Herod is perplexed, and his perplexity does not resolve into faith. He wants to see Jesus, but Luke will show us later (in chapter 23, at the trial) that when Herod finally gets his wish, he treats Jesus as entertainment — hoping for a miracle, getting silence, sending him back to Pilate in a splendid robe. The desire to see Jesus is not, by itself, the same as being willing to receive him. Herod wanted a resolution to his perplexity on his own terms.

What the world cannot hold

What these two readings share is, at bottom, a picture of human understanding running up against its limits. Qoheleth finds that the world's cycles outrun any meaning he can manufacture from them. Herod finds that Jesus outstrips every category available to him. In both cases, the honest response would be to stop, admit the limit, and ask a different kind of question — not "how do I explain this?" but "what is being asked of me?" Qoheleth, wisely, moves from observation to prayer: teach us to number our days. Herod keeps circling, wanting to see without wanting to change.

The Alleluia verse — borrowed from John's Gospel — quietly inserts itself between these two readings like a lamp: I am the way and the truth and the life. Not a category. A person. Not an explanation of the cycles, but someone who stands inside them with us.

To carry today

Think of the question you have been turning over for a long time — about your life, your direction, a person you cannot figure out — and ask whether you are genuinely open to an answer that costs you something, or whether you are looking for confirmation of what you already think. The willingness to be surprised is not weakness. It is what makes room for something real to arrive.
