Thirty-second Sunday in Ordinary Time

The wise ones brought extra oil — not because they were better, but because they were ready.

First readingWisdom 6:12-16
PsalmPsalm 63:2, 3-4, 5-6, 7-8
Second reading1 Thessalonians 4:13-18

There is something quietly urgent about today's readings — not the urgency of panic, but of a person who has thought ahead, who has taken seriously the fact that the night will be long and the light has to last. All three texts circle the same question: what does it mean to be ready, not in a frantic way, but in a deep one?

The first reading — Wisdom 6:12-16

The Book of Wisdom is one of the later books of the Old Testament, written in Greek, almost certainly in Alexandria, sometime in the century before Christ. Its audience was Jewish people living in a pagan world, surrounded by other philosophies and other ways of making sense of life. The book is an argument that the wisdom at the heart of Israel's tradition is not parochial or narrow — it is the very structure of reality.

What is striking about this passage is what it does with seeking. We expect a text about wisdom to tell us to work hard, to study, to earn understanding through effort. Instead, Wisdom turns the whole thing around: she is seeking us. She makes her own rounds. She goes out early to anticipate desire before the desire has even fully formed. She sits at the gate so that whoever arrives at dawn will find her already there, already waiting. The posture of Wisdom here is almost maternal — attentive, anticipating, present before she is asked to be.

This does not mean there is nothing required of us. The passage opens with those who love her and seek her, and it promises they will find her. The desire has to be real. But the great mercy of the passage is that our desire is met by a desire that precedes it. We lean toward wisdom; wisdom is already leaning toward us.

Psalm 63 fits here with remarkable precision. It is a psalm of thirst — the soul longing for God the way parched earth longs for rain. And yet the psalmist is not despairing; he has already been in the sanctuary, has already tasted something, and now remembers it in the night watches and finds strength in the memory. The thirst and the finding exist together, which is exactly the shape of the first reading: seek and you will find, because she is already seeking you.

The second reading — 1 Thessalonians 4:13-18

This letter is probably the earliest piece of Christian writing we have — Paul writing to a small, young church that is confused and grieving. Some of their members have died since Paul left, and the community is worried. Did those people miss the Lord's return? Have they been left out?

Paul writes to reassure them, and his reassurance is not vague. He does not say "trust that it will work out." He says that the dead in Christ will rise first — that those who have already died will not be at any disadvantage. The imagery is vivid and strange by our standards: a word of command, an archangel's voice, a trumpet, clouds. This is the language of the ancient world for a momentous arrival, the kind of ceremonial meeting a city would mount for a visiting emperor. Paul borrows that imagery and applies it to something far larger.

This reading is running on its own track — it is not about wisdom or lamps or oil. But it shares something with the other texts: it is about the future arriving, about being ready when it does, and about the fact that love is not defeated by time or even by death. "Thus we shall always be with the Lord" is the whole point — not the mechanics of the event, but the destination.

The readiness that cannot be borrowed

The parable of the ten virgins is one of the strangest and, if you let it, most uncomfortable stories Jesus tells. Ten young women are waiting for a wedding — a long celebration that could begin at any hour of the night once the bridegroom arrived. Five bring extra oil for their lamps; five do not. The bridegroom is delayed. They all fall asleep, which the story does not criticize — the problem is not that they slept, but that five of them had nothing in reserve when midnight came.

What makes the story uncomfortable is the moment when the five foolish ones ask to borrow oil and are refused. It seems harsh. But the parable is pointing at something that cannot actually be shared or transferred. The oil is not stinginess; it is whatever it is in a person that has been built up over time — the habit of prayer, the practiced trust, the long cultivation of attentiveness to God. You cannot hand that to someone else at midnight. It has to have been grown.

The locked door and the words "I do not know you" are sobering. They are not about a God who keeps score, but about the simple reality that a relationship has to have been formed. You cannot walk into the wedding feast of a bridegroom you have never spent time with.

What Wisdom and the parable together suggest is that readiness is not an event but a practice — something built in ordinary time, in dawn watchings and kept vigils, in the habits of attention that add up to a life oriented toward the right thing.

To carry today

Think about one practice of prayer or attention that you have let slide — not out of laziness exactly, but out of busyness, or the assumption that you can always pick it up later. Ask yourself honestly: what would it cost me to begin again this week, in a small way, before the day gets loud? Start there, not with a resolution, but with the actual small beginning.
