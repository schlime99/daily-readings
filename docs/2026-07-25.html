Saturday · 25 July

Feast of

Saint James, Apostle

A mother asks for thrones; Jesus offers a cup — and James drinks it.

First reading2 Corinthians 4:7-15
PsalmPsalm 126:1-6
GospelMatthew 20:20-28

James, the son of Zebedee, was martyred by Herod Agrippa around the year 44, making him the first of the Twelve to die for the faith. He left behind no letter, no Gospel, no recorded sermon. What we have instead is a story about his mother asking for the wrong thing on his behalf, and the quiet fact that when the cup came around, he drank it. The Church keeps his feast in red, the color of blood, and the readings she gives him are not comfortable ones.

The first reading — 2 Corinthians 4:7-15

Paul is writing to a community that has started to doubt him. His opponents have apparently argued that a genuine apostle wouldn't look so battered — wouldn't be so often imprisoned, hungry, shipwrecked, or afraid. Real authority, they suggest, would be more impressive. Paul's response is one of the most quietly stunning passages in all his letters: yes, exactly, that is precisely the point. The treasure — meaning the gospel, the knowledge of God revealed in Christ — is being carried in earthen vessels, which is the Greek way of saying clay pots. Ordinary, breakable, worth almost nothing in themselves.

The image would have landed hard for Paul's original readers, because clay jars were everywhere in the ancient world and they were understood to be temporary containers. You did not treasure a clay jar; you treasured what was inside it. Paul is saying that the apostle himself is the clay jar. His weakness, his suffering, his very un-glamorous appearance is not evidence against his mission but evidence of where the power actually comes from.

What follows is a string of paradoxes so carefully balanced they feel almost like a creed: afflicted but not constrained, perplexed but not despairing, struck down but not destroyed. Each pair holds the weight without resolving it — Paul is not saying the suffering doesn't hurt, he is saying it doesn't have the last word. And the reason is specific: he is "always carrying about in the body the dying of Jesus." This is not metaphor for Paul; it is a literal description of what apostolic life has cost him. The death is real. But so is the logic that follows — if the dying of Jesus is present in the body, then the life of Jesus may also be manifested there.

Psalm 126 sits alongside this with its image of those who go out weeping, carrying seed, and come back carrying sheaves. The psalm was originally about the return from exile in Babylon — the shock of captives discovering, almost disbelieving, that they were going home. Paul is living inside exactly that movement: the exile of suffering, the homecoming of resurrection.

The Gospel — Matthew 20:20-28

There is something genuinely touching about the mother of James and John marching up to Jesus and asking for the best seats in the Kingdom. Matthew gives us the mother as the speaker; Mark has the brothers ask directly themselves. Either way, the request is the same and the embarrassment is the same — she wants her boys on the right and left of Jesus when he comes into his glory.

Jesus doesn't mock her for it. He turns and speaks to the sons: "Can you drink the chalice that I am going to drink?" And they say, without hesitation, that they can. They almost certainly have no idea what they are agreeing to. They are thinking about glory; he is thinking about Gethsemane. But here is what the Church holds onto on this feast day: they said yes, and eventually they meant it. James would be beheaded. The cup would come, and he would drink it — not because he was fearless, but because he had said yes to something larger than he understood.

Jesus then gathers all twelve and explains what authority is going to look like among them. The Gentile rulers lord it over their subjects; greatness among the disciples is to work the other way entirely — the greatest is the servant, the first is the slave. And then the sentence that grounds all of it: the Son of Man did not come to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many. He is not issuing a rule about church governance in the abstract. He is describing himself, and he is saying: be like this.

Ambition turned inside out

What holds these readings together is the question of what power is actually for. The mother wants her sons elevated; Paul is writing from a posture of radical diminishment; James ends up executed. The trajectory of the Gospel is not upward mobility but downward service — and not because suffering is good in itself, but because something gets through in the broken places that cannot get through any other way. Paul's clay jar carries the treasure precisely because the jar doesn't get in the way. James's death bore witness to something no living comfortable apostle could have said quite so clearly.

This is not a comfortable feast. But it is an honest one, and there is something freeing about the honesty. The Church is not asking us to be more impressive. She is asking us to be more available — to what is being poured in, and to where it needs to go.

To carry today

Think of something you are carrying right now that feels like pure loss — a situation that has cost you something and shows no sign of producing anything. Ask whether it might, despite appearances, be one of those places where something is getting through that couldn't get through any other way. You don't have to feel that yet. Just stay open to the possibility that the jar being broken is not the end of the story.
