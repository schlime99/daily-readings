Twenty-first Week in Ordinary Time

Clean the inside of the cup, and the outside will follow.

First reading2 Thessalonians 2:1-3a, 14-17
PsalmPsalm 96:10-13
GospelMatthew 23:23-26

There is a particular kind of religious failure that looks, from a distance, like devotion. It tithes. It washes its dishes. It tracks every rule carefully, almost lovingly. And yet something essential has been left out — something that cannot be faked with any amount of precision. Today's readings press on that hollow place, and they also offer a way through it.

The first reading — 2 Thessalonians 2:1-3a, 14-17

Second Thessalonians is a short letter, probably written to the same community Paul had addressed in his first letter — a community that was anxious about the end times. Someone, apparently, had been circulating the idea that "the day of the Lord" had already come, or was coming any moment. The source of this rumor was murky: Paul says it might be "a spirit," a spoken message, or even a forged letter — perhaps one passing itself off as his. The community was rattled.

What strikes me about Paul's response is that he does not try to fix their theology by giving them a more accurate timetable. He does not sit them down and explain exactly what the last days will look like. Instead, he tells them to stand firm and hold to what they have already received — the actual Gospel, the teaching they got from people they trusted, the tradition that was handed on to them. The word "traditions" here is worth pausing over. In Greek it carries the sense of something delivered, passed along, entrusted. What Paul is pointing to is not a system of rules but a living transmission: the faith as it was given to him, given to the Thessalonians, and meant to be handed on further.

Then the letter turns into a prayer, and it is a beautiful one. "May our Lord Jesus Christ himself and God our Father, who has loved us and given us everlasting encouragement and good hope through his grace, encourage your hearts." Paul's antidote to apocalyptic anxiety is not better information — it is encouragement, grounded in a love that precedes and outlasts any particular moment in history. Psalm 96 picks this up naturally, with its vision of a God who comes not to terrify but to govern with justice and constancy. The psalm's mood is jubilant: the sea, the plains, the trees of the forest are all invited to join in. The Lord's coming is good news for creation, not a catastrophe to survive.

The Gospel — Matthew 23:23-26

Matthew 23 is one of the most uncomfortable chapters in the Gospels. Jesus is delivering a series of "woes" against the scribes and Pharisees, and his language is cutting. It is worth knowing that the Pharisees were not simply villains — they were the serious religious people of their day, devoted to the law in ways most of us are not devoted to anything. Jesus is not attacking casual believers; he is challenging the most committed ones. That is part of what makes this chapter so unsettling.

The first image here is almost darkly funny. The law required that a tenth of any agricultural produce be given to God — and the Pharisees extended this to the smallest kitchen herbs: mint, dill, cumin. That is extraordinary precision, almost comically meticulous. Jesus does not say it was wrong. He says, quite explicitly, "these you should have done." But the obsessive attention to the spice rack had somehow crowded out "judgment and mercy and fidelity" — the bones of what the law was always about. Then comes the memorable image of straining a gnat from your drinking water while managing to swallow a camel. The gnat and the camel were both technically unclean under Jewish law; you were supposed to avoid both. But you have to work hard to swallow a camel without noticing.

The second image is the cup and dish. Ritual purity for eating vessels was a real concern, and washing them carefully was a genuine practice of holiness. Jesus says: you are cleaning the outside. But inside the cup is plunder and self-indulgence. The fix is not to stop cleaning the cup — it is to start with the inside, because a clean inside produces a clean outside as a matter of course. Interior transformation is not just one item on a list of religious duties; it is the thing that makes the rest of the list real.

When precision becomes evasion

These two readings are on largely separate tracks — Paul writing to a worried community about the end times, Jesus pressing the scribes about religious practice — but there is something underneath that connects them. Both are warnings against a kind of busyness that substitutes for the real thing. The Thessalonians were so caught up in calculations about when the Lord would arrive that they needed Paul to call them back to the faith they already held. The Pharisees were so absorbed in fine-grained observance that the inner life went unexamined. In both cases, a legitimate concern — when will the Lord come? how do I honor God precisely? — had metastasized into something that actually displaced the center. Paul's answer is to return to what was entrusted. Jesus's answer is to start with the inside.

To carry today

Think about the religious practices or habits of mind that you tend to your most carefully. Are they keeping you connected to what actually matters — generosity, honesty, love of the people in front of you — or have they quietly become a substitute for examining something harder? Ask for the grace today to let your outer habits flow from something real rather than paper over something hollow.
