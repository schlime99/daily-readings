Tuesday · 15 September

Memorial of

Our Lady of Sorrows

A mother stands at the foot of the cross while her son dies, and does not run.

First reading1 Corinthians 12:12-14, 27-31a
PsalmPsalm 100:1b-2, 3, 4, 5
GospelJohn 19:25-27

Today the Church pauses at the foot of the cross — not to look at Jesus from a distance, but to stand close enough to see his mother's face. This memorial asks something quiet and almost unbearable of us: to stay, to witness, not to fix anything but to be present. There is a great deal of theology in simply standing still.

The first reading — 1 Corinthians 12:12-14, 27-31a

This passage falls in the middle of Paul's great argument about gifts in the Corinthian community, and it might seem oddly chosen for a feast of Mary's grief. But the Church is not wrong to put it here. Paul's image of the body is among the most practical and the most daring in all his letters. The Corinthians were a community in love with certain spectacular gifts — speaking in tongues, for instance — and inclined to rank one another accordingly. Paul will have none of it. He reaches for the body as his argument: a hand does not outrank a foot. An ear is not inferior to an eye. The body is one thing, made of many parts, none of them optional.

What's remarkable about the list Paul actually gives is how unglamorous most of it sounds. Apostles and prophets are there, yes, and then teachers, and then — almost in the same breath — administration. Assistance. Ordinary practical service sits in the same list as mighty deeds, and Paul gives no indication that this arrangement bothers him. Every member is genuinely necessary. This is not consolation for the ungifted; it is a description of reality. The Body of Christ would fail without every part doing what it does.

Psalm 100 lifts directly from this: "We are his people, the sheep of his flock." Not I am his person, my own individual sheep. The flock is one flock, tended by one shepherd, belonging entirely to someone who is not any of them. The psalm is sheer gratitude for this — that we did not make ourselves, that we belong somewhere, that the belonging is permanent.

The Gospel — John 19:25-27

These three verses are among the most concentrated in the entire New Testament. John does not linger or editorialize; he simply reports who was standing there and what was said. Four people are at the foot of the cross: Mary, her sister, Mary Magdalene, and the Beloved Disciple. John's Gospel does not name this disciple anywhere, and that anonymity has always seemed deliberate — as if the reader is meant to step into his place.

Jesus looks down from the cross and does two things. He gives his mother a son, and he gives his disciple a mother. "Woman, behold, your son." "Behold, your mother." This is not primarily domestic arrangement, though it is that too — Mary is now a widow, and someone needs to care for her. But the gesture goes further. The word woman is the same word Jesus used at Cana, his first sign, when Mary pressed him toward something he said had not yet arrived. Here, at the hour that has arrived, he addresses her the same way. She is not being distanced by the title; she is being placed at the center of something.

The tradition has long read this moment as the birth of the Church in miniature. Mary becomes mother to the Beloved Disciple, who stands for all disciples. What she bore in her body, she now holds in her grief, and is given back in a new form. The sword Simeon predicted — the Gospel of Luke has him speak it over the infant Jesus while Mary watches — has passed through her, and here she still stands. She does not collapse. She does not leave. She is, at the end as at the beginning, entirely present.

Sorrow as a form of love

What the first reading and the Gospel share is an account of what love costs in a body — in particular bodies, in actual communities, in a family standing in a real place watching something real happen. Paul's body metaphor is not abstract; it is about the inconvenient, irreplaceable dependence we have on one another. Mary at the cross makes that dependence visible in the most extreme possible way: she cannot do anything for him, and she stays anyway. That is not weakness. It is a kind of fidelity that the Body of Christ is built to recognize and honor.

These two readings are, honestly, on somewhat separate tracks — Paul's letter is a weekday reading running its own course through 1 Corinthians and would have been paired with different Gospels in any other week. But today the Church sets them side by side, and something does emerge: the body is one, and the body suffers, and remaining with the suffering rather than fleeing it is not incidental to Christian life but central to it.

To carry today

Think of a place in your life — a relationship, a situation, a grief — where the only thing asked of you right now is to stay and not look away. It may not feel like much, and it may cost you more than it looks like from the outside. Ask whether you have the courage to remain present, the way a mother stood at the foot of a cross and did not move.
