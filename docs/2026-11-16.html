Thirty-third Week in Ordinary Time

A church that works hard but has gone cold, and a beggar who cries out until Jesus stops.

First readingRevelation 1:1-4; 2:1-5
PsalmPsalm 1:1-2, 3, 4, 6
GospelLuke 18:35-43

Two very different scenes today, and they do not share a common thread so much as they share a common question: what does it look like to actually want God? One answer comes from a letter written to a church that has everything right except the one thing that matters most. The other comes from a ditch outside Jericho, where a man with nothing gets it exactly right.

The first reading — Revelation 1:1-4; 2:1-5

The Book of Revelation is one of those texts that people tend to either avoid entirely or read too eagerly, scanning it for a secret map of history. It is worth pausing at the beginning, because the beginning tells you what it is: a letter. John, writing from the island of Patmos where he has been exiled, addresses seven actual churches in the Roman province of Asia — communities of real people, struggling to stay Christian under real pressure. The visions he records are not puzzles to decode but a form of prophetic literature his readers would have recognized, full of symbolic numbers and images drawn from the Hebrew prophets. When he calls himself a servant and says the angel "made it known," he is placing himself in the line of those prophets — not above them, but among them.

The opening blessing is quietly beautiful: "Blessed is the one who reads aloud." Revelation was written to be read in the assembly, out loud, to people who would listen. It was liturgical from the start, meant for exactly the kind of gathering we are participating in now.

The letter to Ephesus, which follows almost immediately in today's reading, is striking for the particular nature of its criticism. Ephesus is a church that has done everything right — hard work, patient endurance, vigilance against false teachers, refusal to compromise. The Lord has nothing but praise for their conduct. And then, quietly devastating: "yet I hold this against you: you have lost the love you had at first." It is not that they have abandoned God in some dramatic way. They have simply kept going — correctly, faithfully, consistently — while something essential cooled. The works are still there. The fire that once drove the works is not.

Psalm 1 echoes this in its own way, describing the person who does not just follow the law mechanically but delights in it, meditating day and night. The psalm's image of the tree planted near running water is not about straining effort; it is about a life rooted in something living, drawing from it naturally. The refrain drawn from Revelation — "those who are victorious I will feed from the tree of life" — ties the two together: Ephesus, warned about losing its first love, is being called back toward exactly this kind of rooted delight.

The Gospel — Luke 18:35-43

Jesus is approaching Jericho, which means he is on the last leg of his journey to Jerusalem. The crucifixion is close. Luke has placed this healing near the end of a long travel narrative that began back in chapter 9, and by now a certain gravity hangs over everything.

The blind man sitting by the road hears a crowd. He asks what is happening. Someone tells him: Jesus of Nazareth is passing by. What he does next is worth noticing. He does not wait to see if Jesus will come to him. He does not compose himself or find a polite way to get attention. He shouts. When the people around him try to silence him — and that detail is not incidental; there is real social pressure being applied here — he shouts louder. "Son of David, have pity on me." The title is theologically loaded: Son of David is a messianic claim, a declaration that this passing teacher is the fulfillment of the promise made to Israel. The blind man sees something, in other words, that many of the sighted people around him do not.

Jesus stops. That moment — Jesus stopping — is one of the most humanly immediate moments in the Gospels. The whole procession halts. He asks: "What do you want me to do for you?" It seems like an odd question to ask a blind man, but it is a real one. Jesus does not assume; he asks. And the man answers with total plainness: "Lord, please let me see." He receives his sight and follows, and the crowd that had been silencing him joins the praise.

What first love looks like

The connection between these two readings is not about sight as a metaphor for faith, though that reading is tempting. It is simpler than that. The Ephesian church has the right beliefs, the right practices, and the right record of endurance. What it has lost is desire — the urgent, unself-conscious wanting that drove those first works. The blind man outside Jericho has nothing but desire. He cannot see, he has no credentials, the crowd is against him, and none of that slows him down for a moment. He wants one thing and he is not embarrassed to keep crying for it.

The warning to Ephesus is not an accusation of hypocrisy. It is something subtler — a recognition that faithful practice, sustained long enough without that original fire, can become its own kind of going-through-the-motions. The remedy the Lord proposes is not to work harder but to remember: "realize how far you have fallen, repent, and do the works you did at first." He is not asking for new works but for the old works with the old heart underneath them.

To carry today

Think of one practice in your life — prayer, mass, a habit of generosity — that you began with some real warmth and now do largely by inertia. That is not a reason to stop. But it may be an invitation to ask honestly whether the wanting is still there, and if not, what it would take to recover it. Ask for the grace of wanting again, the way the man outside Jericho wanted — plainly, persistently, without dignity.
