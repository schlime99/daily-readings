Twenty-third Week in Ordinary Time

The measure you pour out is the measure that comes back — so pour generously.

First reading1 Corinthians 8:1b-7, 11-13
PsalmPsalm 139:1b-3, 13-14ab, 23-24
GospelLuke 6:27-38

Today's readings are, at first glance, about two different problems: Paul is sorting out a church dispute over food, and Jesus is teaching an almost terrifying ethic of love. But underneath both is the same uncomfortable question — what do you do when being right isn't enough, when your freedom or your principles would cost someone else something precious? The answer, in both cases, is love that costs you something real.

The first reading — 1 Corinthians 8:1b-7, 11-13

Paul is writing to the church in Corinth somewhere around the mid-50s AD, and one of their live controversies is whether a Christian can eat meat that has been sacrificed to a pagan idol. The market stalls were full of such meat — after temple rituals, the leftovers were sold commercially, and refusing it meant limiting your options considerably. Some Corinthians, the more theologically confident ones, had worked out the obvious answer: idols are nothing, there is only one God, so the meat is just meat. Eat freely.

They were not wrong, exactly. Paul agrees with their theology. "There is no God but one," he says, and he means it. The so-called gods are empty — the word behind "idols" carries that sense of vanity, of a thing that has no reality backing it up. The confident Christians had done their thinking correctly.

And yet Paul tells them their knowledge is making them dangerous. There are new believers in that community who come from backgrounds thick with idol worship, and for those people, eating this meat is not a neutral act — it stirs up old allegiances, old feelings, old confusion about where their loyalty now lies. Their conscience is, as Paul puts it, weak — not stupid, but not yet settled. And when they see the theologically confident member eating freely at the idol's table, they may follow along, not from freedom but from pressure, and it tears at something in them.

Paul's move here is striking. He doesn't say the confident Christians are wrong. He says their knowledge has inflated them — the Greek behind that word is the same one used for a bellows or a bladder puffed up with air — and that knowledge without love builds nothing. Love, by contrast, builds up. It attends to what the other person actually needs. So Paul sets down his own freedom as a kind of pledge: if food causes my brother to sin, I will never eat meat again. Not because the meat is bad, but because his brother matters more than his menu.

Psalm 139 answers this beautifully. It is one of the great psalms of being fully, uncomfortably known — "you have probed me and you know me," even "my journeys and my rest you scrutinize." The psalmist does not run from that knowledge; by the end, he asks for it: probe me, O God, and know my heart. That is the disposition Paul is reaching for — the person who knows they are known, who has nothing to prove, who can afford to lay down their rights because they are not holding themselves up by those rights in the first place.

The Gospel — Luke 6:27-38

This is Luke's version of the Sermon on the Mount material — often called the Sermon on the Plain, since Luke places Jesus on level ground with the crowd. What Jesus is doing here is not gentle advice. He is describing something structurally different from ordinary human community, which runs on the logic of reciprocity: you love those who love you, you help those who help you, you lend to those who will pay you back. Jesus does not deny that sinners do this — he says it plainly, almost wryly. Of course they do. Even that is not nothing. But it is not what he is describing.

What he describes instead is love that is not transactional at all. Love your enemies — the people who are actually arrayed against you. Do good to those who hate you. Lend without expecting repayment. These are not romantic instructions; they are almost economically absurd. And the reason Jesus gives is not that it will pay off eventually, but that God himself is "kind to the ungrateful and the wicked." The love he is calling his disciples to is not a human strategy; it is a participation in the character of God. You are behaving as a child of the Most High when you act the way the Most High acts — lavishly, without keeping score.

The image at the end is one of the most vivid in Luke: a good measure, packed down, shaken together, and overflowing, poured into your lap. This is the image of a grain merchant giving an honest, generous measure — not skimping, not level at the brim, but heaped up. The point is not that you give generously to get more back; the point is that the measure you pour out is what your life becomes.

Where the knowledge question and the love ethic meet

Paul and Jesus are both working against the same human temptation — the temptation to make being right the end of the matter. The confident Corinthian is right about idols and stops there. Jesus's listener loves the people worth loving and stops there. In both cases, the stopping point is too early. What both readings push past is the comfort of correctness into the harder territory of love that notices the other person, love that costs something, love that does not protect itself by pointing to what it deserves.

To carry today

Think of one person in your life toward whom your default position is careful, measured, self-protective — someone you treat fairly but not generously. Ask yourself what it would look like to give them a full measure today: packed down, shaken together, overflowing. Not a grand gesture, but something you would not normally offer because you're not sure they've earned it.
