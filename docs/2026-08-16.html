Twentieth Sunday in Ordinary Time

A foreign woman refuses to be turned away, and Jesus calls it great faith.

First readingIsaiah 56:1, 6-7
PsalmPsalm 67:2-3, 5, 6, 8
Second readingRomans 11:13-15, 29-32
GospelMatthew 15:21-28

A Canaanite woman walks into the Gospel today and will not leave until she gets what she came for. She is not supposed to be here — not by the customs of her world, not by the narrow reading of Jesus's own mission — and yet by the end of the scene she has wrested something extraordinary from him and he names it, openly, as great faith. That is the center of this Sunday, and the Isaiah and Paul readings have been preparing the room for it all morning.

The first reading — Isaiah 56:1, 6-7

Isaiah 56 opens what scholars call the final movement of the book, written for a community returning from exile in Babylon. They are trying to rebuild — the temple, the city, a sense of who belongs. The natural instinct in that situation is to draw the circle tight: the pure, the proven, the pedigreed. And right into that instinct the prophet drops a word from God that expands the circle instead of tightening it.

The passage singles out "the foreigners who join themselves to the LORD" — people with no Israelite ancestry, no claim by blood. And the promise to them is not toleration at the margins but full belonging at the holy mountain, joy in the house of prayer, offerings accepted on the altar. The name for the temple here is particularly striking: not "a house of prayer for Israel" but for all peoples. That phrase is worth sitting with. It is not an afterthought about who else might eventually be included; it is a description of what the place actually is.

Psalm 67 answers this directly, so much so that they almost feel like a call and response. "O God, let all the nations praise you" — not just Israel, not just the observant, but all the ends of the earth. The psalm imagines God guiding the nations with equity, blessing flowing outward until the whole world knows the way. It is an expansive, almost dizzyingly universal vision, and it sits right between the Isaiah and the Gospel as a kind of hinge.

The second reading — Romans 11:13-15, 29-32

Paul is doing something different here than Isaiah — he is working through a deeply personal puzzle about his own people. He is writing to Gentile Christians in Rome, and he is trying to explain what has happened with Israel's response to the Gospel, and why. His logic in this passage is intricate and somewhat knotty, and it runs on its own track from the narrative of the Gospel today.

The line worth resting on is near the end: "the gifts and the call of God are irrevocable." Paul is insisting that God does not revoke what he has offered. The long history of covenant and promise to Israel is not cancelled by the present moment of apparent rejection. And the design he sketches — Gentiles receive mercy, Israel will receive mercy, everyone has been delivered to disobedience so that mercy might reach everyone — is less a worked-out theory than a doxology of bewilderment. Paul is astonished that God's mercy is this wide, and he cannot quite contain it in his argument. That astonishment is the truest thing in the passage.

The Gospel — Matthew 15:21-28

Jesus is outside Israel — in the district of Tyre and Sidon, Gentile territory — when a Canaanite woman approaches him. Matthew's choice of the word "Canaanite" is deliberate and a little jarring: it is an archaic term, not in common use in the first century, and it carries centuries of weight. The Canaanites were the ancient other, the people Israel had been defined against since Joshua. Matthew is reaching for that old otherness on purpose.

The exchange that follows is one of the most uncomfortable in all four Gospels. The woman cries out; Jesus says nothing. The disciples ask him to dismiss her. He tells her his mission is to Israel alone. Then, when she persists, he uses a word that would have stung anyone — dogs. Readers have argued for centuries about what is happening in this moment. Is Jesus testing her? Is he voicing the disciples' attitude in order to expose and then overturn it? Is he himself being changed by the encounter? Matthew does not explain, and that silence is part of the point. What he shows us is this: the woman does not crumble. She does not accept the framing. She takes the very word he used and turns it: even the dogs eat the scraps from the table. And the reply is not a concession wrung from reluctance but a declaration — great is your faith. Her daughter is healed from that hour.

Where a foreign woman outpaces everyone else

What connects Isaiah, Psalm 67, Paul's bewildered doxology, and this woman is a single stubborn truth: God's mercy keeps exceeding whatever container his own people have built for it. The house of prayer is for all peoples. The nations will praise. The gifts are irrevocable. And a Canaanite woman who has no standing in any religious boundary argument refuses to stop calling out. Her faith is great not because it is theologically correct but because it is wholly uncontained — she cannot be told that grace has run out. Every other character in today's Gospel, including Jesus's closest companions, wants her gone. She stays. And mercy reaches her.

To carry today

Think of something you have quietly decided is beyond grace — a situation, a relationship, a part of yourself that seems too far outside the circle to be healed. Notice the assumption behind that decision, and ask whether it is yours to make. The Canaanite woman was told no three times before the answer changed. She kept asking anyway, and that persistence was itself the faith.
