Sixteenth Week in Ordinary Time

God gathers the scattered; the question is what kind of soil we give him to work with.

First readingJeremiah 3:14-17
PsalmPsalm 31:10, 11-12, 13
GospelMatthew 13:18-23

Two passages, one from the prophet and one from the Lord himself, and they are not quite on the same track — Jeremiah is speaking of exile and return, and Jesus is explaining a parable about hearing. But they rhyme in a way that is worth noticing: both are about what happens when the word of God actually lands somewhere, and both are quietly urgent about it.

The first reading — Jeremiah 3:14-17

By the time we reach chapter three of Jeremiah, the prophet has already laid out the charge against Israel at some length. The people have been unfaithful — not in some vague spiritual sense, but in the specific, political, religious chaos of the late seventh century before Christ, when the nation was hemorrhaging its allegiance to God and patching it back together with idols and foreign alliances. The tone of the opening chapters is by turns indignant and heartbroken, and what makes Jeremiah remarkable is that the heartbreak belongs not just to the prophet but to God himself. The LORD speaks in this book the way a husband speaks of a wife who has left — not coldly, not with bureaucratic judgment, but with a grief that keeps reasserting itself even in the accusation.

So when verse 14 opens with "Return, rebellious children," the word "rebellious" in the original carries something like the idea of turning away, of an about-face in the wrong direction. And yet the sentence doesn't end in punishment — it ends in gathering. "I will take you, one from a city, two from a clan, and bring you to Zion." That image is worth sitting with: not a mass return, not a parade, but a patient individual rescue — one person here, two families there — as though God is going house to house. The numbers suggest a remnant, a small and scattered group, not a triumphant national restoration. But the tenderness is in the detail.

Then comes something startling. The LORD says that in those coming days, people will no longer speak of the ark of the covenant, no longer miss it or look for it or try to rebuild it. For Israel, the ark was the most sacred object in existence — the gold-covered chest housing the tablets of the law, the physical sign of God's presence with the people. Its loss to the Babylonians was catastrophic. And yet here God says: don't worry about the ark. Jerusalem itself will be called the LORD's throne. The presence of God will not be contained in an object; it will fill the city, and through the city, draw all nations. It is a promise so large it is almost hard to absorb — an end to the exile of the heart, not just the exile from the land.

The psalm, drawn from Jeremiah 31 rather than from Psalms, sings this same hope back in a warmer register: the scattered are gathered, mourning turns to joy, the young and the old dance together. It is the same promise sung rather than declared.

The Gospel — Matthew 13:18-23

This is not the parable of the sower itself — that came the day before, and the disciples asked Jesus what it meant. This is Jesus answering. It is a rare thing in the Gospels: an explicit, point-by-point interpretation given by Jesus himself. Usually he leaves the parables open, lets them work in the listener. Here he closes the loop, probably because the disciples have asked directly and because he trusts them with more than the crowd receives.

The fourfold soil — the hard path, the rocky ground, the thorny patch, the good earth — maps onto four kinds of hearing. What is striking is that three of the four actually receive the word. The seed falls. The problem in most cases is not that the word is absent but that something prevents it from taking root: incomprehension in the first case, shallow enthusiasm without depth in the second, the slow suffocation of anxiety and wealth in the third. Only the last hearer both receives and understands — and the verb "understands" here is doing real work. It doesn't mean intellectual mastery; it means something closer to taking in, absorbing, letting the word become part of how you see and act.

The yields — a hundred, sixty, thirtyfold — are agricultural extravagances. A Palestinian farmer would have been delighted with a tenfold return. Jesus is describing not merely adequate fruit but abundance.

Hearing as homecoming

What connects Jeremiah and Matthew today is not a shared image but a shared question: what does it take for the word of God to actually arrive? Jeremiah's God is gathering a scattered people one by one — it is an act of pure initiative, God doing the seeking. Jesus describes the word going out like seed, falling on every kind of ground — that too is God's initiative, prodigal and unprejudiced. But in both cases, something is required on the receiving end. The scattered have to be willing to return; the soil has to be what it is.

Jeremiah promises that God will appoint shepherds "after my own heart" to tend the gathered people wisely. The psalm echoes it: the Lord himself is the shepherd who guards the flock. And in Matthew, the word that the good-soil hearer receives and understands is precisely the word of the Kingdom — the news that the shepherd is here, that the gathering has begun, that even now the seed is going out.

The invitation is not to anxiety about which soil we are. It is to notice whether we are actually hearing.

To carry today

Think about where your attention goes when you sit with something difficult — a scripture reading, a conversation you didn't want to have, a question about your own life. Do you absorb it, or does something else quickly crowd it out? Ask today for one moment of genuine attention, of letting something you hear actually land and stay.
