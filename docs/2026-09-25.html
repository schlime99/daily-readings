Twenty-Fifth Week in Ordinary Time

God has put the timeless into our hearts, and still we cannot see past the next bend.

First readingEcclesiastes 3:1-11
PsalmPsalm 144:1b, 2abc, 3-4
GospelLuke 9:18-22

Two questions sit at the center of today's liturgy, though they come from very different directions. Qohelet — the ancient teacher behind the book of Ecclesiastes — looks at the whole turning of human life and asks what any of it amounts to. Jesus, praying alone in the hills, turns to his disciples and asks something far more direct: Who do you say that I am? These two questions are not in conversation with each other by design, but they rhyme in a way worth noticing.

The first reading — Ecclesiastes 3:1-11

Ecclesiastes is one of the stranger books in the Old Testament, and it helps to know that before diving in. Its author calls himself Qohelet, which means something like "the Gatherer" or "the Preacher," and the whole book is his long, restless attempt to make sense of human experience by observing it as honestly as he can. He is not cynical exactly, but he refuses easy comfort. He has seen too much. He stares at the rhythms of life — birth and death, planting and uprooting, weeping and laughing — and refuses to look away.

The famous list in today's passage is often read as consolation: everything has its season, so relax. But Qohelet is making a subtler and harder point. He lines up these fourteen pairs of opposites — and in Hebrew, seven is the number of completeness, so fourteen pairs means the whole of existence — and the effect is vertiginous. Every gain pairs with a loss. Every embrace pairs with a parting. The rhythm is real, but you cannot stand outside it and choose your moment. You are inside it, carried along.

Then comes the turn that makes the passage genuinely theological: God has made each thing suited to its time, and has placed eternity — the Hebrew word points to something like a longing for the whole, a sense that there is more — in the human heart. We are creatures who can feel the shape of forever, and yet we cannot see what God is actually doing, not from beginning to end. That gap between the longing and the knowing is not a flaw. It is the condition Qohelet is describing with such precision.

Psalm 144 answers this directly. The psalmist calls God his rock, his fortress, his stronghold — all the stable things Qohelet says are hard to hold onto — and then asks the same question from a different angle: Lord, what is man, that you notice him? A breath. A passing shadow. The psalm does not dispute the smallness; it marvels that the rock-like God attends to such small, fleeting creatures anyway.

The Gospel — Luke 9:18-22

Luke tells us Jesus is praying alone — and then, a beat later, that the disciples are with him. That small apparent contradiction is probably Luke's way of saying something true: Jesus draws apart into prayer in a way that the disciples cannot quite share; they are present physically but he is in a different country. Out of that prayer, he turns and asks who the crowds think he is. John the Baptist. Elijah. One of the old prophets. All honorable guesses. All wrong.

But who do you say that I am? Peter's answer comes without hesitation: The Christ of God. The anointed one. The one Israel has been waiting for. And Jesus, strikingly, tells them to say nothing about it — because what they think they mean by "the Christ" is not yet what he is going to be. He immediately reframes the title in the hardest possible terms: suffering, rejection, death, and then rising on the third day. Peter named him correctly and did not yet understand what he had said.

This is one of those moments in the Gospels that is almost darkly funny if you read it honestly. The disciples have gotten the right answer and Jesus responds by telling them not to use it yet, and then piling onto the answer a weight it was never designed to carry. The Christ is the one who will be killed. The Messiah is the one who will be rejected by the religious establishment. They had the word; they did not have the shape of the thing.

The distance between knowing and understanding

What joins these two readings is that gap — the one Qohelet names as the space between our longing for eternity and our actual inability to see what God is doing. Peter names Jesus correctly and still cannot see. Qohelet sees every season of human life and still cannot trace the pattern God is weaving through all of them. The point is not that knowledge is useless or that faith is blind. The point is that God's work is larger than any frame we bring to it.

The Alleluia verse, tucked between the readings, offers its own quiet answer to both questions: The Son of Man came to serve and to give his life as a ransom for many. This is what the suffering means. This is the shape of the thing Peter named without knowing. The seasons turn, and this is the one who stands inside all of them.

To carry today

Consider where in your life you are certain you know what is happening — what this difficulty means, where this road is heading — and sit for a moment with the genuine possibility that you are seeing only the pair, not the whole list. Ask for the willingness to hold your confident interpretation loosely, trusting that the one who placed eternity in your heart is still working, even through what you cannot yet see.
