God walks Ezekiel through a valley of dry bones and promises to breathe them back to life.

Ezekiel in a field of bones, and Jesus pressed by a scholar with a test question — these two readings are on separate tracks today, as weekday pairings often are. There is no hidden thread forcing them together, and the honest thing is to take each on its own terms. Both have more than enough to offer.

The first reading — Ezekiel 37:1-14

Ezekiel is among the strangest and most vivid writers in all of scripture. He was a priest deported to Babylon in 597 BC, part of the first wave of exiles before Jerusalem finally fell. He prophesied to a people who had lost everything — temple, city, land, and with them every visible sign that God was still with them. By chapter 37, that catastrophe is complete. The bones in the valley are not metaphor working at a careful distance; they are the emotional truth of what Israel felt. The people have said it themselves, and Ezekiel quotes them directly: "Our bones are dried up, our hope is lost, and we are cut off." That is not poetry. That is a community that has concluded it is over.

God sets Ezekiel down in the center of the plain and makes him walk among the bones in every direction, which is worth sitting with for a moment. The prophet is not given a distant view from a hilltop. He walks through the whole of it — the extent, the dryness, the sheer number. God does not minimize the situation before promising to reverse it. The desolation is real, and the vision begins by insisting that Ezekiel see it clearly.

Then comes the question that should probably stop us every time we hear it: "Son of man, can these bones come to life?" Ezekiel's answer is one of the most honest things in the Bible: "Lord GOD, you alone know that." He does not say yes out of piety, and he does not say no out of despair. He declines to answer for God, which is its own kind of faith.

What follows is almost theatrical in its staging. God tells Ezekiel to prophesy — to speak the divine word over the bones — and even as he speaks, there is a rattling. Bone finds bone. Sinew comes. Flesh covers it. Skin seals it. And then — nothing. The bodies lie there, complete but inert, because there is no spirit in them. It is an eerie pause in the vision, and it seems intentional: restoration of form is not the same as restoration of life. God tells Ezekiel to prophesy a second time, this time to the spirit, calling breath from the four winds. And the breath comes, and they stand — "a vast army."

The word for spirit here is the same word as breath and wind, and the vision plays all three meanings at once: the wind blowing from the four corners of the earth, the breath entering the lungs, the spirit of God animating from within. It is the language of Genesis 2, when God breathed into the nostrils of the first human and the creature became alive. Ezekiel's vision is a new creation, a second Genesis, and it is addressed specifically to people who believe the story is finished.

The psalm answers this directly. Psalm 107 is a song of gathered exiles — people brought back "from the east and the west, from the north and the south" — who went hungry and thirsty in the desert and cried out, and were led by a direct way to an inhabited city. The redeemed are called to say so. The psalmist is insistent: let them give thanks, not merely feel gratitude, but speak it aloud, because the testimony of those who have been rescued matters to those who are still afraid.

The Gospel — Matthew 22:34-40

The scholar's question — which commandment is the greatest? — is framed as a test, and in Matthew's Gospel it comes after a string of traps that Jesus has already navigated with an authority that visibly unsettles his questioners. The Pharisees have heard him silence the Sadducees; now they want their turn.

Whatever the man's motive, the question itself is serious. Rabbinic tradition counted 613 commandments in the Torah, and teachers genuinely debated which carried the most weight. Jesus answers with the Shema — the prayer every observant Jew prays morning and evening, Deuteronomy 6:5 — and then immediately adds Leviticus 19:18, the commandment to love the neighbor, calling the second "like" the first. That word "like" is doing a great deal of work. He is not ranking them one-two with a gap between. He is saying they are of the same kind, that you cannot really honor one while disregarding the other. Then he says something that would have landed with some force: the whole law and the prophets depend on these two. Not just summarized by them — dependent on them, hanging from them the way a door hangs from its hinges.

What spirit and love share

These two readings may not share a planned theme, but they do share something worth noticing: in both, the gift has to come from outside. The bones cannot reconstitute themselves; they need the breath that comes from the four winds. And the love described in the Gospel — love with all your heart, soul, and mind — is not a commandment to manufacture feeling, but to orient yourself entirely toward the source of everything, and let the neighbor be included in that orientation. The great tradition has always held that we love the neighbor not by our own resources alone, but because God's own love has entered and is working through us. Ezekiel's bones do not generate their own breath. But they do stand up.

To carry today

Think of something in your own life that feels genuinely finished — a relationship, a hope, a version of yourself you have quietly given up on. Sit with the question God asked Ezekiel: can these bones live? You are not required to answer. But you are allowed to remain in the question rather than closing it yourself. And notice where today you are being asked to love — not with warmth you don't feel, but with a simple act of attention toward someone in front of you.
