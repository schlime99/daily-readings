Saturday · 21 November

Memorial of the

Presentation of the Blessed Virgin Mary

God is not the God of the dead, but of the living — and to him, all are alive.

First readingRevelation 11:4-12
PsalmPsalm 144:1-2, 9-10
GospelLuke 20:27-40

Today the Church keeps the memorial of the Presentation of the Blessed Virgin Mary, an ancient feast rooted less in scripture than in devotion — the tradition that Mary was brought as a young child to the Temple in Jerusalem, offered there to God by her parents, and consecrated to his service before she could fully understand what that meant. It is a small, quiet feast. And the readings assigned to it are not about Mary at all, which turns out to be fitting in its own way: they are about what she was given to, and what she trusted.

The first reading — Revelation 11:4-12

The book of Revelation is a letter written under persecution — most likely during the reign of the emperor Domitian, toward the end of the first century — and it speaks in the coded, image-laden language that Jewish apocalyptic writing had developed precisely because plain speech was dangerous. When you understand that, the strangeness of the text becomes less alien. These are not predictions meant to be decoded like a puzzle; they are images meant to sustain people who are suffering.

The two witnesses in this passage are almost certainly figures for the whole prophetic witness of the Church, though some early readers saw Elijah and Moses in them, or Elijah and Enoch — the two figures from the Hebrew scriptures who were taken up without dying. They are described as olive trees and lampstands, an image borrowed directly from the prophet Zechariah, where the same symbols stand for the anointed ones who serve before God. They have power and they testify, and then — this is the part that matters — they are killed. Their bodies lie in the street of the city called symbolically "Sodom" and "Egypt," which is also the city where their Lord was crucified. Jerusalem itself, in Revelation's bitter grief, has become what once enslaved Israel. The enemies celebrate. And then, after three and a half days, the breath of God enters them again.

Three and a half days. The echo is deliberate. The witnesses pass through something shaped like the death and resurrection of Christ, and they rise. Psalm 144 — which the Church sets as the response — is a warrior's song, a hymn of David praising the God who trains hands for battle and delivers his servant from the evil sword. It may seem like an odd pairing with the vision of slain prophets, but that is exactly the point: the God who is praised here is the one who brings his people through, not the one who keeps them from difficulty.

The Gospel — Luke 20:27-40

The Sadducees were the priestly aristocracy of first-century Judaism, and they differed from the Pharisees on a crucial point: they accepted only the written Torah — the five books of Moses — as authoritative, and since resurrection is not clearly taught there, they rejected it entirely. This question they put to Jesus is not an honest inquiry. It is a trap, and a clever one. By the law of levirate marriage — a provision in Deuteronomy meant to protect widows and preserve family lines — a woman could pass through seven husbands. The Sadducees think this makes resurrection look absurd: heaven would be a legal nightmare.

Jesus declines to play on their terms. He does not argue about the marriage law. He simply says that the life of the resurrection is not an extension of this life with the same structures intact — it is a different kind of existence altogether, one where death has no more dominion and therefore the whole architecture built around death and its management falls away. Then he reaches into their own territory and beats them on their own ground: Moses, at the burning bush, calls God "the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob" — present tense, not past. He is not God of the dead. If God is in a living relationship with Abraham, then Abraham is alive to him, somehow, even now. The resurrection is not a strange new doctrine grafted onto an old faith. It was already there in the fire and the voice and the holy ground.

The scribes — who would have been Pharisees, and who had their own quarrels with the Sadducees — cannot help themselves: "Teacher, you have answered well." After that, no one dares ask him anything more.

The ground Mary stood on

What connects all of this, on a day honoring a child brought to the Temple, is simply the conviction that God is a God of the living — that to be given to him is not to be swallowed by an abstraction but to be held by someone who keeps faith with his own. The two witnesses die and rise because that is what happens to those who belong to the God of the bush. Mary is presented at the Temple, tradition says, as a small girl, before she can articulate a theology of resurrection. She is simply given. And the God she is given to turns out to be the one who does not abandon what is his.

That is the quiet logic of the feast. The offering comes before the understanding. The trust is extended into mystery. And the God on the other end of that trust is — as Jesus insists, with a patience that leaves the room speechless — not the God of the dead, but of the living.

To carry today

Think of something you have entrusted to God — a person, a situation, a hope you have more or less stopped checking on. Sit with the reminder that God is in a living relationship with what you have handed over. Ask for the grace to trust that what is given to him is not simply gone, but held.
