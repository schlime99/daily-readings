Thirty-fourth Week in Ordinary Time

The victors stand on the sea of glass and sing, while Jesus tells us how to become victors.

First readingRevelation 15:1-4
PsalmPsalm 98:1, 2-3ab, 7-8, 9
GospelLuke 21:12-19

We are in the final days of the liturgical year, and the Church knows exactly what she is doing by placing these readings here. The book of Revelation shows us the end of the story — the victors already singing — and then Luke's Gospel shows us what the road to that shore actually looks like. These are not two separate pieces. They are the destination and the journey, set side by side so we can see both at once.

The first reading — Revelation 15:1-4

Revelation is a notoriously difficult book, and much of its difficulty comes from people trying to read it as a coded prediction of current events rather than as what it actually is: a letter of encouragement written in the visionary language of Jewish apocalyptic literature, addressed to Christians living under Roman persecution in the late first century. Once you understand that, passages like this one open up considerably.

John sees a "sea of glass mingled with fire." Earlier in Revelation (chapter 4), the sea of glass appeared before God's throne — crystalline, still, impossibly pure. Now fire runs through it, and people are standing on it. The fire almost certainly evokes the trial they have come through; in biblical imagery, fire purifies as much as it destroys. These are people who have been tested, and they are standing. That is the point. Not floating, not collapsed — standing, holding harps.

The song they sing is called "the song of Moses and the song of the Lamb," which is a remarkable pairing. The song of Moses in Exodus 15 is the great hymn sung after Israel crossed the sea and Pharaoh's army drowned — a song of deliverance at the edge of the water. Here the victors sing a new version of that song at the edge of a different sea, having crossed through a different kind of danger. The connection is deliberate: what God did at the Red Sea, he has done again, on a cosmic scale, for all the nations.

The words of their song are not triumphalist. They praise God's justice, his holiness, his righteous acts. They do not celebrate their own survival so much as they marvel that God turned out to be exactly who he said he was. This is what victory looks like in Revelation — not gloating, but worship.

Psalm 98 echoes the first reading so closely that the refrain is actually taken straight from Revelation 15: "Great and wonderful are all your works, Lord, mighty God." The psalm itself is one of the great enthronement hymns, calling the sea, the rivers, and the mountains to join the song. The liturgy is making the point quietly but insistently: this song has been going on for a long time, and we are invited to add our voices to it.

The Gospel — Luke 21:12-19

This passage comes from Luke's version of what scholars call the "eschatological discourse" — Jesus's teaching about what is coming, delivered in the Temple just days before his death. He is not speaking abstractly. His listeners are going to be arrested. Some of them will be killed. Family members will betray them. This is not metaphor; for the early Christians, it was a straightforward description of what was happening to them.

What is striking is the particular comfort Jesus offers. He does not say they will be spared, or that the persecution will be brief, or that it will not really hurt. He says two things instead. First, when they are hauled before authorities, they should not spend their energy preparing a defense — he himself will give them words that cannot be refuted. This is an astonishing promise, and the Acts of the Apostles is essentially a long record of it being kept: again and again, ordinary fishermen and tentmakers standing before councils and governors and finding, to everyone's surprise including their own, that they have something to say. Second, he says that "not a hair on your head will be destroyed." He has just said some of them will be put to death, so he clearly does not mean physical invulnerability. He means something deeper — that whatever is essential about them, whatever makes them them, will be held safe. The Greek word he uses for "secure your lives" at the close is the word for winning or acquiring something. By perseverance, they will come to possess the very lives that look, to the outside world, like they are being lost.

Where the sea of glass and the sword meet

What Revelation shows us and what Jesus promises are really the same thing seen from different vantage points. Luke gives us the road: arrest, betrayal, testimony, perseverance. Revelation gives us what the road leads to: a great shore, a glass sea lit with fire, and people standing on it who have been through exactly that. The victors in John's vision are not an abstract category of heroic Christians from some other era. They are the people who heard Jesus say "by your perseverance you will secure your lives" and then, one day at a time, chose to believe him. The harps in their hands were once the burdens on their backs.

We are not, most of us, facing the kind of persecution described in Luke 21. But the structure of the promise applies wherever the cost of faithfulness feels real: in the small daily choice to hold onto what is true when it would be easier to let it slide, in the relationships where following Christ has made us genuinely unwelcome. The shore is the same shore.

To carry today

Where in your life does faithfulness feel most costly right now — not dramatically, but quietly and persistently? Hold that place in mind today, and ask what it would mean to persevere there, not by straining harder, but by trusting that something worth having is being secured.
