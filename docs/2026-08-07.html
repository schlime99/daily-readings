Eighteenth Week in Ordinary Time

The city that built its power on blood will fall; the one who loses his life for Christ will find it.

First readingNahum 2:1, 3; 3:1-3, 6-7
PsalmDeuteronomy 32:35cd-36ab, 39abcd, 41
GospelMatthew 16:24-28

Two very different texts sit side by side today, and they are not obviously about the same thing. Nahum is a war oracle against a fallen empire. Matthew is a call to discipleship that sounds almost like a riddle. And yet, quietly, they are asking the same question: what are you actually betting your life on?

The first reading — Nahum 2:1, 3; 3:1-3, 6-7

Nahum is one of the short books most people have never read, tucked between the major prophets and the New Testament in a part of the Bible that gets skipped. The whole book is essentially one long poem about the destruction of Nineveh, the capital of the Assyrian Empire — and it is worth knowing who Nineveh was to the people hearing this. Assyria had been the terror of the ancient Near East for over a century. They destroyed the northern kingdom of Israel in 722 B.C. and deported its people. They besieged Jerusalem, razed cities, and were widely known for a particular cruelty: the ancient sources record that they impaled captives, stacked heads at city gates, and flayed enemies alive. When Nahum calls Nineveh "the bloody city, all lies, full of plunder," he is not exaggerating. His audience had lived under that boot.

The passage opens with a cry of relief — "See, upon the mountains there advances the bearer of good news, announcing peace!" — and there is genuine joy there that we can easily miss. After a century of domination, someone is running toward you with the message that it is over. The oppressor is destroyed. You can celebrate your feast days again. The word translated "scoundrel" has a connotation closer to something utterly base, someone with no claim to dignity at all. Nahum is announcing that this apparently invincible empire, this thing that seemed permanent and unchallengeable, is simply gone.

Then the camera pans to Nineveh itself, and the language shifts into something almost cinematic: the crack of the whip, the rumbling wheels, horses at a gallop, and then bodies — heaping corpses, the endless dead. Scholars note that this is a reversal of Assyrian boast literature, which celebrated exactly this kind of military chaos as evidence of their power. Nahum turns it around. This is not glory. This is ruin.

The psalm — drawn from the Song of Moses in Deuteronomy 32 — deepens the theology underneath the poem. "It is I who deal death and give life," God says. Not Nineveh. Not Caesar. Not any empire that has ever imagined itself eternal. The power to end things and begin them belongs to God alone. This is not a comfortable verse to sit with, but it is an honest one, and it pairs with Nahum exactly: what looked like absolute power was always borrowed.

The Gospel — Matthew 16:24-28

This passage comes directly after Peter's confession that Jesus is the Messiah, and then Jesus's first prediction of his passion — that he must suffer, be killed, and rise. Peter rebukes him, and Jesus calls Peter Satan and tells him he is thinking like a human being, not like God. Then, turning to all the disciples, he says what we hear today.

"Whoever wishes to come after me must deny himself, take up his cross, and follow me." We have heard this so often that it can slide past us, but the disciples heard it the way you hear it before you know how the story ends. Crucifixion was not a metaphor to them. It was a form of state execution that the Roman Empire used on enemies, slaves, and rebels — a method specifically designed to be public, prolonged, and humiliating. Telling someone to pick up their cross and follow you was a shocking image. It meant: come with me the way a condemned man walks through the city to the place of his execution. The crowds would have understood this instantly, and it would have sounded appalling.

Then Jesus offers what sounds like a paradox but is actually a very precise observation about how value works. If you spend your whole life protecting your life — managing your risks, protecting your reputation, avoiding loss, never surrendering anything — you will discover that you never really had a life at all. But if you hand your life over in trust to something larger than your own self-preservation, you will find it given back to you in a form you couldn't have constructed yourself. He then offers two rhetorical questions that are worth sitting with rather than rushing past: what would you trade for your own soul? The implied answer is that there is nothing to trade, because once the soul is lost, there is nothing left to make the exchange.

The cost of safety

What Nahum and Matthew share is a clear-eyed look at the strategies people use to make themselves invulnerable — and a quiet insistence that those strategies always fail. Nineveh built its security on military dominance, on the certainty that terror would keep the world in order. It seemed to work for a very long time. And then it didn't. The disciples in Matthew are being warned away from an interior version of the same error: building a self so defended, so cautious, so unwilling to spend itself, that it achieves a kind of living death before it dies. The path Jesus describes is genuinely costly. That honesty is part of what makes it trustworthy.

To carry today

Think about one thing you are protecting a little too carefully — your comfort, your standing, your sense of control over some part of your life. It does not have to be dramatic. Ask, honestly, whether that protection is serving you or slowly diminishing you, and whether there is something you already know you are being asked to put down.
