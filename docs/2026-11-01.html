Sunday · 1 November

Solemnity of

All Saints

Heaven is not a reward for the extraordinary — it is the destination of the ordinary, washed clean.

First readingRevelation 7:2-4, 9-14
PsalmPsalm 24:1-6
Second reading1 John 3:1-3
GospelMatthew 5:1-12a

All Saints is the feast of the anonymous. There are the names we know — martyrs, mystics, founders of orders, the great ones on the calendar — but this day reaches past them to the vast, uncountable company that no one canonized, whose stories were never written down, who simply lived and loved and suffered and held on. The readings together say: there are more of them than you could ever imagine, and you may be headed in the same direction.

The first reading — Revelation 7:2-4, 9-14

The Book of Revelation is one of the most misread books in the Bible, and most of the misreading comes from forgetting that it is a letter written to real people in real danger. John is writing to Christian communities in Asia Minor — present-day Turkey — who are being pressured, and in some places actively persecuted, to worship the Roman emperor. He writes in a coded, visionary style borrowed from the Jewish prophetic tradition, full of numbers and beasts and angels, precisely because he cannot afford to write plainly. The Roman authorities would intercept it.

So when John sees 144,000 marked with a seal, he is not describing a membership cap on heaven. He is describing protection. The seal on the forehead mirrors a Roman military mark — a soldier's brand, or a slave's mark that says this one belongs to someone who will come for them. The 144,000, twelve times twelve times a thousand, is a symbolic number built from the twelve tribes of Israel: it means the full and completed people of God. Not a count, but a declaration of completeness.

And then the vision opens wider, almost cinematically. Suddenly there is a crowd so large no one can even attempt to count it, from every nation and language that has ever existed. They are wearing white — the color of victory in the ancient world, not mourning — and holding palm branches, which meant the same thing then that ticker tape means now: we have won, we have survived, we have come through. The elder explains it: these are the ones who came through the great distress and washed their robes in the blood of the Lamb. The image is paradoxical, almost violent — you do not usually make something white by washing it in blood — but that is the point. The Lamb's death is the thing that cleans, not their own virtue.

Psalm 24 answers this vision directly. Who may stand in the holy place? The one whose hands are sinless, whose heart is clean. Hearing this on its own, it sounds like an entrance exam. Placed after Revelation 7, it becomes a description of what the blood of the Lamb has made possible: this is what you are, by gift, when you have been washed.

The second reading — 1 John 3:1-3

This brief passage from the First Letter of John runs on a different track from Revelation's crowds and angels, but it quietly says the same thing from the inside. John is not writing about the end of history here; he is writing about the present moment, about what is already true. We are God's children now. Not candidates, not aspirants — now. The fullness of what that means has not been revealed yet, and John is honest about that: we do not yet know what we shall be. But there is a hinge that connects the now to the then: we know that when it is revealed, we shall see him as he is. The vision of God — not a metaphor, but an actual seeing — is what sanctity finally is. And John says that living with that hope already does something to a person: it purifies. You become, gradually, more like the one you are hoping to see.

The Gospel — Matthew 5:1-12a

The Beatitudes sit at the very beginning of the Sermon on the Mount, which is itself the first and longest of Jesus' five great discourses in Matthew's Gospel. Matthew has shaped his account of Jesus deliberately — five discourses, echoing the five books of Moses, suggesting that Jesus is not replacing the law but fulfilling and deepening it. He sits down to teach, which is the posture of an authoritative rabbi. The disciples come to him. And then he begins.

Each beatitude has the same structure: a description of a kind of person, and a declaration about what belongs to them. The poor in spirit, the mourning, the meek, the hungry, the merciful, the pure of heart, the peacemakers, the persecuted. What is striking is that most of these people are not described as virtuous in any triumphant sense. They are people who are empty, or grieving, or under pressure, or in need. They are not the ones who have arrived; they are the ones who are running low. And Jesus says: the Kingdom is theirs. Not will be, someday — is. Present tense.

What the multitude and the blessed have in common

The crowd in Revelation came through great distress. The blessed in the Beatitudes are, right now, in distress — mourning, persecuted, hungry, meek in the face of power that does not respect them. The two readings are painting the same portrait from different angles. What the end of the story looks like in Revelation is exactly what the beginning of discipleship looks like in Matthew: people who have been emptied of their own self-sufficiency, who depend on something beyond themselves, who have been washed and are being washed, who are not yet what they will be but are already on the way.

The saints we celebrate today were not a separate species. They were people who started where the Beatitudes begin — poor in spirit, often, or merciful at great personal cost, or persecuted without recourse — and were carried the rest of the way.

To carry today

Think of one person you have known who has died — someone who was not famous, not officially recognized, just someone who loved well or suffered with grace or held on when it was hard. Sit with the possibility that they are among the uncountable ones in white. And then ask yourself honestly: what would it mean to live today as someone already heading there?
