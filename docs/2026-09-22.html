Twenty-fifth Week in Ordinary Time

Hearing God's word is not enough — the question is whether you act on it.

First readingProverbs 21:1-6, 10-13
PsalmPsalm 119:1, 27, 30, 34, 35, 44
GospelLuke 8:19-21

There is a word that echoes across both readings today, quietly but insistently: the way. Proverbs lays out what the way of wisdom looks like in ordinary life — in commerce, in pride, in justice, in how you treat your neighbor when he is in need. Then the Gospel arrives with something startling, a moment that seems almost harsh, and turns out to be an invitation. The question underneath both texts is the same one Psalm 119 has been singing all along: are you actually walking in the way you say you believe?

The first reading — Proverbs 21:1-6, 10-13

Proverbs is not a continuous argument; it is more like a collection of hard-won observations, the kind that get written down because someone learned them the difficult way. We are in the middle of a long stretch of sayings about how human life actually runs, and this passage pulls together several of the book's great themes into a kind of compressed moral survey.

It opens with an image that would have landed differently in an ancient monarchy: the king's heart is like a stream in the Lord's hand, directed wherever God pleases. In a world where the king was effectively absolute, this is a remarkable claim — not that kings are wicked, but that they are not finally in charge. The same God who bends rivers bends rulers. That is meant to be stabilizing, not frightening.

Then the passage pivots to something more personal. "All the ways of a man may be right in his own eyes, but it is the Lord who proves hearts." This is not a cynical observation — it is an honest one. We are all, to some degree, unreliable narrators of our own lives. We explain our choices to ourselves in the most favorable light, and Proverbs gently insists that there is another standard, and another judge. What follows is a quick inventory of what the Lord actually values: doing right and just is worth more than any liturgical gesture. Haughtiness and pride are not just unpleasant character traits; they are, the text says, tillage of sin — they cultivate the conditions in which wrongdoing grows. The person who makes a fortune through lying is not actually building something; he is chasing a bubble, and the snares underneath him are deadly.

Then it closes with the line that hits hardest: the one who shuts his ear to the cry of the poor will himself call and not be heard. This is not a threat; it is closer to a description of how the moral universe works. Mercy circulates, or it dries up. You cannot close your hand to your neighbor in need and then expect an open hand when your turn comes.

Psalm 119 answers all of this in the voice of someone who has genuinely seen the beauty of the way God describes — "Lead me in the path of your commands, for in it I delight." The psalm wants to walk this way. It is asking for the grace to keep doing what it knows is right, which is already a more honest posture than most of us maintain.

The Gospel — Luke 8:19-21

This is one of the briefest Gospel passages of the year — three verses, a handful of sentences — and it needs a little context to land properly. Luke places this scene just after Jesus has told the parable of the sower and the seed, where the seed that bears fruit is the word of God received and held fast, the word that shapes how you live. Then, immediately, this: Mary and Jesus's brothers arrive at the edge of a crowd and cannot get through. Someone passes the message along — your family is outside, they want you. And Jesus says that his mother and his brothers are those who hear the word of God and act on it.

It is not a rebuke of Mary. Anyone who has read Luke carefully knows that she is introduced in the first chapter as precisely the woman who hears, receives, and acts — "Let it be done to me according to your word." She is the model of exactly what Jesus is describing. What he is doing here is expanding, not diminishing: the circle of his family is not a biological fact, it is a spiritual one, and it is radically open. If you hear the word and do it, you are inside.

But the doing matters. Hearing alone is not the thing. The Gospel has been making this point since the parable of the sower, and the Alleluia verse before this Gospel says it plainly: blessed are those who hear the word of God and observe it.

What obedience actually costs

What ties these two readings together is the gap between knowing and doing, and what it actually takes to close it. Proverbs is cataloguing the ways we fail — the proud heart, the lying tongue, the ear shut to the poor — and most of those failures are not failures of knowledge. Nobody thinks cruelty to the poor is praiseworthy. The failure is in the doing. The Gospel makes the same point from the other direction: it is not enough to be near Jesus, to be in the crowd, to be outside the door. The relationship is built through a particular kind of action, regular and costly, the slow practice of actually obeying what you have heard.

This is not about earning anything. It is about what shapes a person over time. Proverbs knows that the diligent become reliable, that the haughty become worse, that patterns compound. And Jesus knows that a disciple is made by the repeated choice to bring life into line with the word.

To carry today

Think of one place where your knowledge of the right thing to do and your actual behavior have quietly drifted apart — not in some dramatic way, but in the ordinary friction of a day. Ask for the honesty to see it clearly, and for whatever small thing it would take to close that gap today.
