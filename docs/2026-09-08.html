Feast of the

Nativity of the Blessed Virgin Mary

Today the Church pauses to celebrate the birth of the woman who would say yes to God.

The Church does not often celebrate birthdays. Besides Christmas and Easter — the birth and rebirth of Christ — only two birthdays appear on the universal calendar: John the Baptist's and Mary's. That the Church marks Mary's birth at all is a statement of belief: this life mattered from its very beginning, before she had done anything, before she had spoken her fiat. Today is a feast of beginnings, and all three readings circle that word in their own way.

The first reading — Micah 5:1-4a

The book of Micah is a hard book, mostly. The prophet is a contemporary of Isaiah, writing in the eighth century before Christ, and he has spent most of his pages indicting Israel's leaders for crushing the poor and corrupting worship. The verse just before today's passage describes Jerusalem under siege, the ruler of Israel struck on the cheek — humiliation and defeat. So when God speaks about Bethlehem, the contrast is meant to land. Bethlehem is nothing. It is the smallest of the clans of Judah, a backwater village with a famous ancestor (David came from there) and nothing else to commend it. And yet from this nothing, God says, will come "one who is to be ruler in Israel."

This is one of the oldest patterns in Scripture, and it never stops being striking: God reaches past the obvious candidates. Not the great city, not the powerful family, not the well-positioned — the small, the overlooked, the one nobody would list first. The ruler who comes from Bethlehem will not govern by force of personality or military strength; he will "stand firm and shepherd his flock by the strength of the LORD." His greatness is not his own. And the passage ends with those three quiet words: "he shall be peace." Not "he shall bring peace" or "he shall win peace" — he shall be peace, as though peace is not something he achieves but something he is.

The psalm today is almost startlingly joyful for a psalm — it comes from the collection attributed to David, and it rings with the confidence of someone who has already seen rescue: "He has been good to me." It answers Micah the way a shout answers an announcement.

The second reading — Romans 8:28-30

Paul is writing to the Romans in the middle of one of his great sustained arguments about what God has been doing in human history. This short passage is a brief blazing summary of it: from foreknowledge to predestination to calling to justification to glory, a chain that Paul describes in the past tense even though much of it has not happened yet, because in God's intention it is already accomplished. "All things work for good for those who love God" — this is not a promise that nothing bad will happen, but rather that nothing, not even the worst of it, falls outside the reach of God's working. This passage runs on its own track from the other two readings today, and saying so is honest. But it finds the feast in its own way: if Paul is right that God's intention is to conform us "to the image of his Son," then Mary's birth is the opening of that chain in a particular and unrepeatable human life.

The Gospel — Matthew 1:1-16, 18-23

The genealogy that opens Matthew's Gospel is one of those passages people tend to skip over, and that is a shame, because it is quietly extraordinary. Matthew structures it as a list of forty-two generations, organized in three groups of fourteen: from Abraham to David, from David to the Babylonian exile, from the exile to Christ. A Jewish reader in Matthew's audience would have recognized this as highly deliberate — numbers carried meaning, and the symmetry signals that history has been building toward something.

But the names Matthew includes are the real surprise. Ancient genealogies almost never named women, and Matthew names five: Tamar, Rahab, Ruth, "the wife of Uriah" (Bathsheba, though Matthew will not write her name), and Mary. These are not the straightforward, uncomplicated mothers of the story. Tamar disguised herself to secure her rights by unconventional means. Rahab was a Canaanite and, the tradition says, a prostitute who hid the Israelite spies at Jericho. Ruth was a foreigner, a Moabite widow who clung to her mother-in-law and walked into a strange land. Bathsheba comes into the story through David's worst hour. Matthew is not embarrassed by any of this. He seems almost to insist on it — as if to say that the line leading to Jesus runs directly through the complicated, the foreign, the overlooked, the scandalized.

And then Mary. The genealogy reaches her and the grammar shifts: the list has followed the pattern "A became the father of B" all the way down, and then suddenly it reads "Joseph, the husband of Mary, of her was born Jesus who is called the Christ." The verb is passive. The subject is Mary. Something different is happening.

What Bethlehem and the genealogy both know

Micah chose the smallest village. Matthew chose the women no one would have put on the list. Both of them are making the same point by different means: God's way of entering history has a consistent preference for what human accounting would pass over. Mary herself is a young girl in an occupied territory, from a family ordinary enough that we have no record of it. And she is the one from whom the sun of justice, as the alleluia verse says, rises.

To carry today

Consider today someone whose beginning was complicated or small — maybe your own. The feast says that God's reach is longer than the resume, deeper than the circumstances of origin. Ask whether you are willing to believe that the chain running from foreknowledge to glory includes people who do not look like obvious candidates — including, on your harder days, yourself.
