Twenty-seventh Week in Ordinary Time

Paul shakes the Galatians awake; Jesus tells us to keep knocking anyway.

First readingGalatians 3:1-5
PsalmLuke 1:69-75
GospelLuke 11:5-13

There is a strange comfort in discovering that Christians have always struggled with the same temptations — the impulse to earn what was given freely, the fear that prayer isn't quite enough and we need to add something to make it work. Paul and Jesus are both addressing that impulse today, from opposite directions, and the two readings are worth sitting with separately before you notice what they share.

The first reading — Galatians 3:1-5

By the time Paul writes Galatians, he is furious, and he does not bother to hide it. This letter is unique among his writings for its almost complete absence of an opening thanksgiving — he goes from greeting to rebuke with barely a breath in between, and by chapter three he has called the Galatian community stupid twice in five verses. That word in Greek carries the sense of being bewitched, of having had one's wits stolen, and Paul runs with the image: who has bewitched you? It's a vivid, slightly dark choice, because in the ancient world people took bewitchment seriously — the evil eye, a hex, someone with enough malice and power to cloud your judgment. Paul is using language his readers would find alarming, because he thinks the situation is alarming.

The problem is this: the Galatians began their Christian life through faith, experienced the Spirit moving among them in unmistakable ways, and are now being told by certain teachers that all of that needs to be completed by taking on the full obligations of Jewish law — circumcision, dietary rules, the whole apparatus. Paul sees this as a catastrophic reversal. You started in the Spirit, he says, and now you're trying to finish in the flesh? He means "flesh" not as a synonym for sin but as shorthand for the human sphere of effort, achievement, and self-management — everything we can accomplish by our own strength and willpower.

His argument is almost embarrassingly simple: how did you receive the Spirit in the first place? Was it because your Torah observance was impeccable, or was it because you heard and believed? They know the answer. The Spirit showed up before they had done anything to earn it. So why imagine that now, going forward, God has changed his method?

The responsorial psalm here is the Benedictus from Luke's Gospel — Zechariah's great song at the birth of John the Baptist — and it fits beautifully, because the whole song is about God remembering a promise made to Abraham long before the law of Moses existed at all. The covenant came first. The promise came first. That is Paul's whole argument compressed into a canticle.

The Gospel — Luke 11:5-13

The parable of the midnight friend comes right after Jesus teaches the disciples the Our Father, which is worth keeping in mind. They've just asked him how to pray, and he has given them the words; now he gives them the spirit in which to use those words.

The scenario is very specific, and reading it with first-century Palestinian hospitality in mind makes it richer. A traveler arrives late — the Middle Eastern road system being what it was, you went when conditions allowed, not necessarily when it was convenient. Hospitality toward the traveler was not optional; it was a binding social and moral obligation, almost sacred in character. So when the man goes to his neighbor at midnight, he is not being inconsiderate. He has no choice. The question is whether the neighbor understands his own obligation.

Jesus tells us the neighbor will get up and help, not necessarily out of friendship, but because of the man's persistence — or, more accurately, because of the sheer audacity of the request, the shamelessness of it. The word Luke uses suggests someone who has dropped all pretense of dignity and is simply insisting. And then Jesus pivots from the parable to a direct promise: ask, seek, knock. The three verbs are all in a continuous tense in the original — keep asking, keep seeking, keep knocking. And then, the stunning close: if you, who are imperfect and limited and self-interested, know how to give good things to your children, how much more will the Father give the Holy Spirit to those who ask?

That ending is worth sitting with. Jesus doesn't say God will give you everything you name on your list. He says God will give the Holy Spirit — the presence and wisdom and love of God himself, poured into the one who keeps asking.

The gift you cannot manufacture

These two passages are not artificially paired — they genuinely answer each other. Paul tells the Galatians they cannot produce the Spirit by human effort; Jesus tells the disciples they can receive the Spirit by persistent, shameless, trusting prayer. The Spirit is neither earned nor automatic; it is given, freely, to those who ask and keep asking. Both readings push back against the same quiet heresy: the idea that our relationship with God is something we manage rather than something we receive.

The Benedictus ties this up with a historical sweep — this has always been how God works, promising before people deserved it, showing mercy before it was asked for, setting people free so they could worship without fear. That last phrase is worth a moment: free to worship him without fear, holy and righteous in his sight. That is what the Spirit actually accomplishes. Not performance. Not anxious striving. Worship without fear.

To carry today

Think today about whether your prayer life feels more like petition or negotiation — whether somewhere underneath the words you are trying to earn God's attention by the quality or quantity of your asking. The promise in today's Gospel is not that God responds to impressive prayer but that he responds to honest, persistent, unashamed prayer. Ask for what you actually need. Keep asking.
