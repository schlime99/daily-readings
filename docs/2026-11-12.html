Memorial of

Saint Josaphat, Bishop and Martyr

Paul asks Philemon to receive a runaway slave back as a brother — and means it.

First readingPhilemon 7-20
PsalmPsalm 146:7, 8-9a, 9bc-10
GospelLuke 17:20-25

Today the Church remembers Josaphat Kuntsevych, a bishop of the Eastern Catholic tradition in what is now Ukraine, who was killed in 1623 by a mob that opposed his efforts toward Christian unity. He died for the visible communion of the Church. And then the readings the liturgy gives us — a letter about a runaway slave and a Gospel passage about a Kingdom that cannot be pinned down or pointed to — seem, at first glance, to have little to do with one another, or with him. But stay a moment. Each of them, in its own way, is about recognizing what is already present, when you have the eyes to see it.

The first reading — Philemon 7-20

The letter to Philemon is the shortest thing Paul ever wrote, and one of the strangest documents in the New Testament. It is a personal note — four paragraphs, essentially — addressed to a man named Philemon, a Christian of some standing in the city of Colossae, who hosted a church in his house. Philemon had a slave named Onesimus. At some point, Onesimus ran away — possibly after committing some offense or taking something that wasn't his, as Paul's delicate phrasing hints. And somehow, in the course of his flight, he ended up with Paul in prison. There he was baptized, and in this passage Paul calls him "my child," saying that he has become his father through the Gospel.

Now Paul sends him back, and this letter goes with him. It is a masterpiece of gentle pressure. Paul is very careful to say that he is not commanding anything, that he wants Philemon's cooperation to be freely given and not forced, that he respects his authority as a slave owner under Roman law. But notice what he actually does: he tells Philemon that Onesimus is now "more than a slave, a brother" — and that if he regards Paul as a partner, he should welcome Onesimus as he would welcome Paul himself. He offers to cover any debt Onesimus owes, writing the pledge in his own hand, and then adds with a wry turn that Philemon owes Paul his very self. This is not a neutral letter. Paul is carefully, lovingly, and with considerable rhetorical skill, asking Philemon to see Onesimus differently — not as property that has been recovered, but as a person who has been transformed.

Psalm 146 sits here perfectly. It is a psalm about the God who "sets captives free," who "secures justice for the oppressed," who sees the ones everyone else has looked past — strangers, the fatherless, the widow. The psalm's God is a recognizer of human beings. That is exactly what Paul is asking Philemon to become.

The Gospel — Luke 17:20-25

The Pharisees ask Jesus when the Kingdom of God is coming. The question itself is reasonable, even earnest — they were waiting, as all faithful Jews were, for God to act decisively in history. Jesus's answer is startling: the Kingdom does not come "with observation," meaning it does not arrive like a comet you can chart or a campaign you can track on a map. You cannot say "here it is" or "there it is." And then he says the line that the whole passage turns on: "The Kingdom of God is among you."

The Greek word translated "among" can also mean "within," and translators have disagreed about it for centuries. But either reading points to the same truth: the Kingdom is not arriving on some future schedule you need to watch for. It is already here, present, accessible, though it does not announce itself with pomp. After this, Jesus turns to the disciples and tells them that there will be days when they long to see "one of the days of the Son of Man" — meaning, one of these ordinary days when he was present and teaching and healing, days that will seem like nothing until they are gone. And he warns them not to chase after false sightings, not to run after people who shout that he has been located. When the Son of Man comes in his fullness, they will not need a tip. It will be as obvious as lightning across the whole sky.

Then a single, sobering line: "But first he must suffer greatly and be rejected by this generation." He cannot let them have the glory without the cross. The sequence matters.

What seeing has to do with everything

Both readings are about learning to perceive what is already in front of you. Paul asks Philemon to look at Onesimus again — the same face, the same hands, the same person who was once just a category — and see a brother in Christ. Jesus tells the Pharisees that they are scanning the horizon for something that is already standing in the room with them. In both cases, the problem is not that God or grace is absent; the problem is a kind of vision that has been trained to expect something other than what it sees.

Josaphat died trying to hold together a Church that had fractured partly because each side had stopped being able to see the other as fully belonging. His martyrdom was an act of witness to a unity that he believed was real, even when it could not be observed. He saw something others had given up seeing.

To carry today

Today is a good day to ask where you have stopped looking — at a person, a situation, a relationship — because you've already decided what's there. What might you find if you gave it one more honest look? Ask for the grace to see what is already present, even when it doesn't look the way you expected.
