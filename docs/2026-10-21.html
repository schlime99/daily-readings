Twenty-ninth Week in Ordinary Time

Much will be required of the person entrusted with much.

First readingEphesians 3:2-12
PsalmIsaiah 12:2-3, 4bcd, 5-6
GospelLuke 12:39-48

Two different kinds of stewardship sit across from each other today — one cosmic, one domestic — and together they ask the same question: what have you been given, and what are you doing with it? Paul is talking about a mystery hidden since before creation. Jesus is talking about a servant who starts hitting the other servants when the boss is away. The distance between those two images is the whole of the Christian life.

The first reading — Ephesians 3:2-12

Ephesians is a letter written from prison — Paul is under house arrest in Rome — and this passage is one of the most expansive things he ever dictated. He is not writing careful theology the way he does in Romans, marshaling arguments step by step. He is marveling. The word he reaches for is mystery, and he uses it the way someone reaches for a word they know is not quite adequate. What he means is not a puzzle waiting to be solved, but something that was hidden in God from before the beginning of time and has now, through the Gospel, been let out into the open.

The hidden thing is this: the Gentiles — everyone who is not Jewish, everyone who was on the outside of the covenant — are coheirs. Not guests. Not associates. Not welcome observers. Coheirs, members of the same body, partners in the same promise. The Greek behind "coheirs" and "co-members" and "copartners" stacks the prefix together three times, as if Paul cannot say it once strongly enough. What was wrapped up in Abraham's family has turned out to be addressed to everyone who has ever lived.

Paul calls himself "the very least of all the holy ones," which is a strange thing for a man of his accomplishments to say, and probably genuine rather than performative. He was, after all, the one who had spent years hunting down and imprisoning Christians before his conversion. The grace given to him specifically — to preach to the Gentiles — was not something he earned or applied for. It was given to him precisely as the person least likely to receive it. He finds this astonishing rather than embarrassing, and that astonishment seems to be part of the point: if grace reached him, it reaches everywhere.

The canticle from Isaiah 12 that serves as today's psalm is a perfect response to this. Isaiah 12 is a short song of thanksgiving placed at the end of a long section of judgment and promise, and its image — drawing water joyfully from the springs of salvation — is exactly the mood of Ephesians 3. The covenant is not a guarded well with a short list of those who may drink. It is a spring, and the drawing is described as joyful. The psalm picks up Paul's wonder and puts it into a single physical act: someone leaning over, filling a jar, and doing it with gladness.

The Gospel — Luke 12:39-48

Jesus is still in the long middle section of Luke where he is traveling toward Jerusalem, teaching as he goes. He has just told the disciples to be like servants waiting up for a master who is out late at a wedding — ready, dressed, lamps burning. Now Peter asks a reasonable question: is this parable for us, or for everyone?

Jesus does not exactly answer. Instead he tells another story that sharpens the stakes for people in leadership. A servant is put in charge of the household while the master is away. If the master comes home and finds that servant doing the job faithfully, the servant is promoted. If the master comes home and finds the servant has decided the delay means he can do whatever he wants — drinking, abusing the other servants — the punishment is severe.

The parable ends with the line that lands hardest: "Much will be required of the person entrusted with much." It is often quoted as a general principle about talent or wealth, which it is. But in context it is first about knowledge. The servant who knew the master's will and ignored it is worse off than the servant who acted badly out of ignorance. Knowing what is right and doing otherwise is not a neutral fact — it changes the weight of the account.

There is something grimly realistic in the scenario Jesus draws. The servant who decides the master is delayed and starts helping himself to the wine and taking it out on the household staff is not a monster. He is someone who let the absence of immediate accountability do its ordinary work on his character. Jesus is not describing a spectacular villain. He is describing ordinary human drift.

What Paul's mystery costs

These two readings are on separate tracks in one sense — Paul is not reading Luke — but they press on the same point when you set them side by side. Paul's mystery is a gift of astonishing breadth: the grace of God, poured out without reserve, reaching even him, reaching the Gentiles, reaching the ends of the earth. And precisely because it is so large a gift, the question of stewardship becomes correspondingly large. The servant in the parable is not just managing a household. He has been let in on something — trusted with something — and what he does with that trust while the master is away reveals who he actually is.

Grace is not insurance. It is an entrustment. The servant who treats the master's delayed return as a vacation from accountability has already answered, in practice, the question of whether he understood what he was given.

To carry today

Think about the thing you know you ought to be doing — not some abstract moral ideal, but the specific thing you are aware of, that you have perhaps been setting aside because the moment of reckoning feels distant. Paul did not earn the grace that was given to him, but he spent himself in it. Ask yourself honestly what it would look like, today, to do the same with whatever you have been entrusted with.
