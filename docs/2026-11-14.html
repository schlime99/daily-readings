Thirty-Second Week in Ordinary Time

A widow wears down an unjust judge — and God, who is just, will not need wearing down.

First reading3 John 5-8
PsalmPsalm 112:1-2, 3-4, 5-6
GospelLuke 18:1-8

Today's two readings are on separate tracks — a brief, practical note about hospitality on one side, and one of Jesus's sharpest parables about prayer on the other — but both end up circling the same question: what does it look like to stay faithful when the outcome is not yet in sight? That is worth sitting with before the coffee goes cold.

The first reading — 3 John 5-8

Third John is the shortest book in the New Testament — shorter even than Philemon — and it is essentially a letter of commendation. The elder who writes it is praising a man named Gaius for something that might sound unremarkable: he has been welcoming traveling Christians into his home and helping them continue their journey. But in the world of the early church, this was not a small thing. These travelers were missionaries and teachers moving from community to community, and they were doing so on principle — "accepting nothing from the pagans," meaning they refused to take money or lodging from people outside the faith, so that no one could say the Gospel was for sale or that they owed favors to unbelievers. This made them entirely dependent on the network of Christian households willing to take them in.

What the elder says next is striking in its plainness: "We ought to support such persons, so that we may be co-workers in the truth." The word co-workers matters here. Gaius does not need to pack up and become an itinerant preacher to participate in the mission. By opening his door and covering someone's expenses down the road, he is in the work alongside them. The psalm assigned today fits this reading almost hand in glove — Psalm 112 is a portrait of the person who is "gracious and lends," whose "generosity shall endure forever," who "conducts his affairs with justice." It is a psalm about the person who does the unglamorous financial and practical work of holding things together, and it calls that person blessed.

There is something quietly countercultural in both passages. We tend to think of faith in terms of belief, of interior conviction, of spiritual experience. The elder and the psalmist keep redirecting attention toward the person who shows up, helps, and does not make a fuss about it.

The Gospel — Luke 18:1-8

Luke tells us before the parable begins what it is for: it is about "the necessity for them to pray always without becoming weary." He does not leave us guessing. And then Jesus tells a story that is, honestly, a little darkly funny — a judge who admits out loud that he has no interest in God or in other people, and who eventually gives a widow what she wants simply because she will not stop coming. His internal monologue amounts to: I cannot take this anymore. The word Luke uses for what she might do to him is vivid — it suggests she will come and batter him. The judge is not moved by righteousness; he is moved by exhaustion.

The argument Jesus is making is what logicians would call a from the lesser to the greater — if even this corrupt, self-absorbed judge eventually gives in to persistence, how much more will God, who actually loves his people and wants to give them justice, respond to those who call out to him? The contrast is the point. God is not the judge. The widow's relentlessness is the thing to hold onto, not the judge's character.

But then comes the last line, and it lands differently than the rest: "When the Son of Man comes, will he find faith on earth?" Jesus moves without warning from a promise about God's faithfulness to a question about ours. The parable ends with assurance, but that final sentence opens a door into something more uncomfortable. The concern is not whether God will show up — the concern is whether we will still be praying when he does. Persistent prayer is not a technique for getting what we want; it is what faith looks like across time, especially when nothing seems to be moving.

What hospitality and persistence share

These two readings do not share imagery or story, but they share a kind of posture. Both the third letter and the parable are concerned with faithfulness that does not quit — the household that keeps welcoming strangers long after the novelty is gone, the widow who goes back to that courthouse day after day. Neither of them is waiting for a dramatic sign before they act. They are simply still doing what they know to do. The psalm calls this the way of the person who fears the Lord, who is "never moved," who is in "everlasting remembrance." That phrase never moved is worth pausing on — not rigid, not armored, but simply unshaken.

To carry today

Think of somewhere in your own life where you have grown quietly weary of doing the right thing — a prayer that has gone on a long time without an obvious answer, a generosity that nobody has noticed, a faithful habit that is starting to feel like it matters to no one. The question today is not whether God has forgotten. The question is whether you are still showing up. Try naming that place honestly, and ask for the stubbornness to stay.
