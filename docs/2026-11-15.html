Thirty-third Sunday in Ordinary Time

What God entrusts is meant to be risked, not buried.

First readingProverbs 31:10-13, 19-20, 30-31
PsalmPsalm 128:1-2, 3, 4-5
Second reading1 Thessalonians 5:1-6
GospelMatthew 25:14-30

We are almost at the end of the liturgical year, and the readings know it. Next Sunday is the feast of Christ the King; after that, Advent begins again. Matthew's Gospel has been building toward this moment all year, and what we find here is not comfort — not exactly — but something better: a clear-eyed look at what it means to live as people who believe the story is going somewhere.

The first reading — Proverbs 31:10-13, 19-20, 30-31

The book of Proverbs ends with this portrait of the worthy wife, and it is worth knowing that in the Hebrew it is an acrostic: each verse begins with the next letter of the alphabet, a device that in that literary culture signaled completeness, the full range of a subject treated from A to Z. The poem is not primarily a checklist for women; it is a portrait of Wisdom herself, personified as feminine throughout Proverbs, embodied in the life of a household. This woman works with wool and flax at the distaff and spindle — the instruments of domestic production in the ancient world, nothing glamorous, everything essential. She reaches out to the poor. She does not simply organize her home; she extends it outward.

The passage builds to its punchline with something close to deliberate irony: "Charm is deceptive and beauty fleeting; the woman who fears the LORD is to be praised." The word translated "charm" carries a sense of superficial grace, the kind of attractiveness that performs well in public. The poem has just spent verses on this woman's concrete activity — the hands at the spindle, the arms reaching toward the needy — and then contrasts all that with the kind of praise that is about appearance. What God sees and what gets applauded at the city gates are, in the end, the same thing here: not how she looked but what she did with what she was given.

Psalm 128, the responsorial, echoes this directly. It opens with "Blessed are you who fear the LORD, who walk in his ways," and what follows is a vision of fruitfulness — the vine, the olive plants, the table surrounded by family. The fear of the Lord in both Proverbs and the Psalms is not terror; it is orientation, the posture of someone who knows where the center of gravity actually is. The psalm answers the first reading by confirming that this orientation bears fruit you can see and touch.

The second reading — 1 Thessalonians 5:1-6

Paul's letter to the Thessalonians is probably the earliest document in the New Testament, written around 50 A.D. to a community anxious about the end of the age. They had expected the Lord's return quickly, and some of their number had already died. Paul here addresses the question of timing directly, and his answer is essentially: the timing is not the point. The day of the Lord will come "like a thief at night" — sudden, unannounced, interrupting whatever sense of security has settled in. This is not meant to produce dread; Paul immediately pivots. His readers, he says, are "children of the light," and for them the surprise is not a threat but a homecoming. What he is asking for is not anxiety but wakefulness: not frantic watching of the sky, but the steady alertness of someone who lives as if what they believe is actually true.

This reading runs somewhat on its own track, theologically — it is not exegetically connected to the Proverbs passage. But at the level of the whole Sunday, it provides the frame: be alert, because time is not unlimited and what you do with what you have been given actually matters.

The Gospel — Matthew 25:14-30

The parable of the talents is set in Matthew's long final discourse, and it falls between the parable of the ten bridesmaids and the vision of the last judgment — the Lord is coming, and what will you have done in the meantime? A talent in the ancient world was not a skill or an aptitude but a unit of currency, enormous by any measure — a single talent was worth roughly fifteen years of a laborer's wages. The numbers in this story are almost absurd, which is part of the point. These are not small stakes.

Two servants trade with what they receive and double it. The third buries his. When the master returns, the third servant offers an explanation that reveals everything: "I knew you were a demanding person, harvesting where you did not plant and gathering where you did not scatter; so out of fear I went off and buried your talent." He had a theory about his master — severe, exacting, the kind who takes more than he gives — and that theory paralyzed him. He played it safe. And the master's response turns his logic back on him: if you thought I was that demanding, all the more reason to have done something with it.

The servant's error is not simply laziness. It is a failure of imagination rooted in a distorted picture of God. He saw the gift as a threat rather than an opportunity, and so he protected himself from the relationship it was meant to create. What the other two servants understood, apparently without being told, was that the master's trust was itself the invitation. The willingness to risk something with what they had received was the response the gift was asking for.

Fear that buries and trust that multiplies

The first reading and the Gospel are both portraits of faithfulness under exactly this pressure. The worthy woman in Proverbs does not hoard her household's resources; she extends her hands outward, to the poor, to the needy, into risk. The servants who receive five talents and two talents do not bury what they have been given. The common thread is not achievement — the master praises them in identical terms regardless of how much they started with — but the willingness to actually use what has been entrusted to them. The buried talent and the deceptive charm of Proverbs are both ways of opting out of the relationship that the gift was designed to begin.

To carry today

Think of something you have been given — an ability, a relationship, a margin of time or money or energy — that you have been holding very carefully, not quite using it, keeping it safe. Ask yourself honestly whether the caution comes from wisdom or from a quiet belief that generosity will not be met with more generosity. That is the question the third servant could not answer well. See if you can answer it differently today.
