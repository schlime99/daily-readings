Memorial of

Saint Pius of Pietrelcina, Priest

The disciples are sent with empty hands, trusting the Word that is already enough.

First readingProverbs 30:5-9
PsalmPsalm 119:29, 72, 89, 101, 104, 163
GospelLuke 9:1-6

There is a strange rhyme running through today's readings: two very different voices both arriving at the same place, which is the conviction that God's word is genuinely sufficient — not as a pious feeling, but as a practical reality that shapes what you carry and what you chase. The writer of Proverbs and the disciples on their first journey end up, from very different directions, at the same stripped-down trust.

The first reading — Proverbs 30:5-9

Proverbs is a collection assembled over centuries, and chapter 30 belongs to a section attributed to someone named Agur — an otherwise unknown figure who opens the chapter with a remarkable profession of his own ignorance before God. By verse 5, he has arrived at the one thing he is sure of: every word of God has been tested and found true, and the person who shelters in it finds real shelter. This is not mysticism; it is the language of metallurgy. Tested means refined, proven under heat, the impurities burned away. The word holds.

What follows is one of the most quietly striking prayers in the entire Old Testament — a man asking for exactly two things before he dies. Not wisdom, not long life, not victory over enemies, which is what most biblical prayers ask for. He wants to be kept from lying, and he wants neither too much nor too little. The reason he gives for the second request is almost darkly funny in its honesty: if I have too much, I will forget I need you; if I have too little, I will be tempted to steal and drag your name through the mud. It is an astonishingly clear-eyed self-assessment. He knows his own tendencies well enough to pray against them.

The prayer has no grand theological architecture. It is just a man who knows himself and knows what he actually needs to stay on the right path, and he is asking for that — not more, not less. The underlying conviction is that the word of God is enough to organize a life around, that a person does not need to accumulate defenses beyond it.

Psalm 119, the great psalm of the Torah, answers this reading directly. Its refrain — your word is a lamp for my feet — is modest and practical: a lamp for your feet gives you just enough light to take the next step. It does not flood the landscape. It does not tell you what the territory looks like three miles ahead. It shows you where to put your foot right now. The psalmist pairs this with a repeated repudiation of falsehood, exactly echoing Agur's prayer to be kept from lying. The two texts are in genuine conversation.

The Gospel — Luke 9:1-6

Luke 9 is a pivotal chapter. Jesus has been healing and teaching, the crowds are pressing in, and now he moves — he gives the Twelve his own power and authority and sends them out. This is the first mission, and it is deliberately urgent. The instructions he gives them are the opposite of how a sensible traveler would prepare: no walking stick, no bag, no food, no money, no change of clothes. In the ancient world, a walking stick was basic equipment; a bag held provisions. He is not telling them to rough it for character-building purposes. He is telling them that the mission itself will provide what they need, that they are to go in a posture of dependence rather than self-sufficiency.

The instruction to stay in whatever house first receives them, and not to upgrade to a better one later, is worth pausing on. That detail suggests that comfort and social status could creep in even on a journey like this — a better host, a nicer room — and Jesus is cutting it off before it starts. The mission is not a circuit of hospitality; it is a proclamation.

And then the bracing instruction: if a town does not welcome you, shake the dust off your feet as you leave. This is not petulance. Shaking dust from your feet was a formal gesture of separation — Jewish travelers returning from Gentile territory would shake off that dust, symbolically leaving behind what was not theirs to carry. Jesus is giving the disciples permission to not carry rejection with them. Deliver the message, do what you can, and then move. The disciples, Luke tells us simply, went and did it.

Traveling light as an act of faith

What Agur's prayer and the mission instructions share is a refusal to over-provision — a deliberate choice to depend on what God provides rather than on what you can accumulate or arrange for yourself. Agur prays to need exactly enough food, not because poverty is romantic, but because dependence keeps him honest. The disciples travel with nothing, not because goods are bad, but because clutching at security would slow them down and subtly shift what they were trusting.

Both texts are asking what you are actually relying on when you go out into the day. And both suggest that the honest answer, for people of faith, is supposed to be something other than your own stockpile of provisions, plans, or cleverness.

Today the Church also remembers Padre Pio, the twentieth-century Franciscan friar whose life was characterized by exactly this kind of stripped-down availability — decades in a small monastery, confessing for hours each day, with a simplicity that made him somehow inexhaustibly present to the people who came to him. The readings, perhaps unintentionally, suit him well.

To carry today

Where are you over-provisioning — preparing so thoroughly for a conversation, a decision, or a difficulty that you have quietly taken God out of the equation? Notice today one place where you could do a little less arranging and a little more trusting, and see what happens when you show up with slightly emptier hands.
