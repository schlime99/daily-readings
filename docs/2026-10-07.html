Memorial of

Our Lady of the Rosary

A disciple watches Jesus pray and asks to be let in on the secret.

First readingGalatians 2:1-2, 7-14
PsalmPsalm 117:1-2
GospelLuke 11:1-4

There is something quietly striking about the fact that the disciples had to ask. They had watched Jesus pray — they had seen him slip away before dawn, return from it differently — and finally one of them gathered the nerve to say, teach us. That request is the hinge of the Gospel today. And it falls on the feast of Our Lady of the Rosary, a day given over to a particular rhythm of prayer, which makes the question feel less like background and more like the whole point.

The first reading — Galatians 2:1-2, 7-14

Paul's letter to the Galatians is one of the most personal documents in the New Testament. It is essentially a defense of his apostleship and of the gospel he preaches to the Gentiles — people who were not born Jewish and had not been keeping the Jewish law. By the time this passage opens, Paul has been preaching for at least fourteen years after his conversion. He makes a second trip to Jerusalem, bringing Barnabas and a Gentile convert named Titus, and he lays his version of the gospel before the acknowledged leaders of the Jerusalem church — James, Peter (here called Cephas, his Aramaic name), and John, whom he calls "pillars."

The outcome is a handshake agreement: Paul will continue to the Gentiles, Peter to the circumcised — meaning Jewish communities — and both sides will remember the poor. This is a practical division of labor, not a division of doctrine. They are preaching the same Christ; they are just going to different audiences.

But then something goes wrong. Peter comes to Antioch, the great mixed community where Jewish and Gentile believers ate together freely, and at first he joins right in. Then representatives from James's community in Jerusalem arrive, and Peter quietly backs away from the Gentile table, apparently worried about what these visitors — "the circumcised," men who still held strictly to the Jewish dietary and social codes — would think of him. Barnabas, Paul's closest companion, gets swept along. Paul does not manage this privately. He confronts Peter to his face, in front of everyone, and his argument is pointed: you yourself have been living like a Gentile, so by this retreat you are effectively telling Gentile believers they are not fully welcome unless they become Jews. That contradicts what the gospel says about how people are made right with God.

There is a reason this passage can feel uncomfortable: Peter is wrong, and Paul says so, and Peter is Peter. The short psalm set against this reading — Psalm 117, the briefest psalm in the whole psalter, just two verses — lands with ironic precision. "Praise the Lord, all you nations, glorify him, all you peoples." All. Not some, not those who eat the right foods, not those with the right heritage. The psalm is a single shout that refuses every fence, and it is the scriptural answer to Peter's anxious retreat.

The Gospel — Luke 11:1-4

Luke is the only evangelist to give us the scene of a disciple watching Jesus pray and then asking to be taught. In Matthew's Gospel, Jesus offers the Lord's Prayer as part of the Sermon on the Mount, as instruction on how not to pray — not like the hypocrites, not like pagans who pile up words. In Luke, the prayer comes out of a moment of honest longing. One of the disciples sees something in Jesus's prayer that he wants and doesn't yet have, and he asks for it.

Luke's version of the prayer is shorter than Matthew's, which is the version most of us have memorized. There is no "on earth as it is in heaven," no "deliver us from evil," no doxology. Scholars believe Luke's version may be closer to whatever Jesus actually said in that moment — the version Matthew gives may have already been smoothed into its liturgical form for community worship. Both are right. But Luke's leanness is bracing. It is the prayer stripped to its skeleton: Father. Your name, your kingdom. Our bread, our debts, our forgiveness, our safety.

The word "Father" — Abba in Aramaic, which is also the word in the Alleluia verse from Romans — is not a formal address. It is close, familial, immediate. The disciples are not being taught to address a distant deity with elaborate protocol. They are being taught to speak the way a child speaks to someone they actually trust.

What prayer and table fellowship share

These two readings are on their own tracks in the lectionary — Paul's letter is not reading with an eye toward Luke — but they circle the same truth from different directions. What Peter's retreat at Antioch really showed was a failure of belief: he did not actually trust that what God had done in Christ was sufficient to make the Gentiles full members. He hedged. And what the disciples lack before Jesus teaches them is something similar — they do not yet know how to approach God as Father, how to believe that they are genuinely welcome at that table, how to ask without elaborate qualification.

The Rosary, which this feast celebrates, is at heart a school of just this kind of prayer — slow, returned-to, trusting repetition in the company of Mary, who had no trouble believing that what God said would be done in her was actually true.

To carry today

Think of somewhere in your life where you are hedging your trust in God — where you half-believe and then quietly back away when it costs something. Prayer has a way of exposing those retreats before we name them ourselves. Bring one of them honestly into your morning, and let the simple address — Father — do what it is designed to do.
