Twenty-seventh Week in Ordinary Time

The house swept clean but left empty is a danger, not a triumph.

First readingGalatians 3:7-14
PsalmPsalm 111:1b-2, 3-4, 5-6
GospelLuke 11:15-26

There is a particular kind of spiritual failure that looks, from the outside, like success — the life tidied up, the old habits gone, everything in order. Today's Gospel ends with a haunting image of exactly that: a house swept clean and waiting, and what moves into the vacancy. Before we get there, Paul has been laying the groundwork in Galatians for understanding why what fills us matters more than what we have cleared away.

The first reading — Galatians 3:7-14

Paul is in the middle of a fierce argument, and it is worth knowing what the fight is about. The Galatian churches have been visited by teachers who insist that Gentile converts must also take on the Mosaic Law — circumcision, dietary rules, the whole structure — in order to be fully justified before God. Paul is furious, and this passage is one of his sharpest volleys. His strategy here is to go straight to Abraham, the founding figure of the whole tradition, and point out something the teachers seem to have overlooked: Abraham was reckoned righteous before any of the Law existed. The promise came first. Faith came first. The Law came centuries later.

Paul quotes two texts in quick succession that would have been familiar to any Jewish reader. From Deuteronomy, he draws the terrifying line: cursed be everyone who does not persevere in doing all the things written in the book of the law. This is the standard the Law sets for itself — total, continuous observance. Paul is not mocking the Law; he is taking it seriously enough to say that if you are going to play that game, you have to win it completely, and no human being does. Then, from Habakkuk, he quotes the line that had already become a kind of touchstone: "the one who is righteous by faith will live." Two different logics, two different modes of standing before God. Paul is saying you cannot have it both ways.

The resolution he offers is the cross. Christ, hanging on a tree, took on the curse — that same Deuteronomic curse — so that the blessing of Abraham, the promise made long before any law, could flow outward to all the nations. This is not a comfortable theological argument; it is a claim that God's way of putting things right runs through what looks like catastrophic failure and shame. The psalm picks this up quietly, with its refrain that the Lord will remember his covenant forever. The covenant is older than the Law, older than the failure, and God does not forget it.

The Gospel — Luke 11:15-26

The scene opens in the aftermath of an exorcism, which Jesus has just performed. The crowd's response is divided: some are hostile and attribute the deed to Beelzebul, the prince of demons; others demand a sign, as if what they have just witnessed was not evidence enough. Jesus responds to the accusation first with a piece of plain logic — if Satan is casting out Satan, he is destroying his own project, which makes no sense — and then with something sharper: if the charge were true, what does that say about the Jewish exorcists in the crowd? Their own practitioners drive out demons, by whose power exactly?

Then comes the strong man parable, which is brief but vivid. A fully armed man guarding his palace keeps his possessions secure — until someone stronger comes along, overpowers him, strips away his armor, and distributes the spoils. The image is almost military in its precision. Jesus is naming what the exorcism actually means: someone stronger has entered the territory and is redistributing what the strong man thought he owned. The kingdom of God is not arriving as an idea or a program; it is arriving as a force that displaces another force.

What follows is the passage that tends to stop readers cold. An unclean spirit goes out, wanders through dry and desolate places — the wilderness, the regions associated in Jewish thought with chaos and malevolence — and finds no rest anywhere. So it returns to its old dwelling. And it finds the house swept and put in order. Empty. And so it brings seven others, and the end is worse than the beginning.

This is not a passage about spiritual hygiene in a small sense. Jesus is addressing people who have witnessed extraordinary power and responded with suspicion, or who are waiting for a tidier sort of sign before they commit. The warning is for those who might clear away the obvious debris — the gross sins, the public failures — while leaving the interior fundamentally unoccupied, uncommitted, open to anything. An empty house swept clean is more vulnerable than one that was always a mess, because at least the mess was honest about its occupant. What Luke is pressing here is the question of who or what actually lives at the center.

What fills the cleared space

These two readings are, on the surface, on separate tracks — Paul arguing about Abraham and the Law, Luke telling a story about exorcism and empty houses. But there is a real point of contact. Paul's whole argument is that the Law alone, without the Spirit that comes through faith, cannot fill the interior. You can observe every rule and still be standing in a swept, orderly, unoccupied room. The covenant God remembered was always meant to go deeper than external compliance — it was always aimed at something living inside the person. What Jesus is warning against in Luke is exactly what Paul is warning against in Galatians: the house without an inhabitant, the life reformed but not indwelt.

To carry today

Think about an area of your life where you have done the work of clearing something out — a bad habit, a resentment, a pattern you recognized and stopped. Ask honestly what has moved into that space since. Real change does not just subtract; it requires something to take up residence. Ask for the grace to let the right thing actually occupy the room you have opened.
