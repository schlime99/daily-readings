Eighteenth Week in Ordinary Time

The false prophet promises comfort; the true one walks into the storm.

First readingJeremiah 28:1-17
PsalmPsalm 119:29, 43, 79, 80, 95, 102
GospelMatthew 14:22-36

There is a strange, almost theatrical quality to today's first reading — two prophets, facing each other in the Temple, each claiming to speak for God. And then there is the Gospel, where the disciples look out from a wave-tossed boat at a figure walking toward them across the water and cannot tell whether he is real or a ghost. Both readings, in their different ways, are about the difficulty of recognizing what is actually true when fear and wishful thinking are both tugging hard at us.

The first reading — Jeremiah 28:1-17

By the time this scene takes place, Jeremiah has been prophesying for decades, and not one of his words has been pleasant. He has warned of conquest, exile, catastrophe — the consequence of a nation that has turned away from God and put its trust in alliances and idols instead. He has been mocked, imprisoned, and told repeatedly to be quiet. Now, in the fourth year of King Zedekiah's reign — probably around 594 BC, with Babylon's power already crushing and the first wave of exiles already gone — a prophet named Hananiah steps up in the Temple itself and announces that within two years, everything will be reversed. The sacred vessels taken to Babylon will come back. The king taken captive will come home. The yoke of Babylon will be shattered.

It is worth pausing here to feel the pull of Hananiah's message. He is not a fool or a cartoonish villain. He is saying what everyone desperately wants to hear. And he says it in the name of the Lord, with the right formulas, in the right place. How is anyone supposed to know he is wrong?

Jeremiah's response is one of the most quietly devastating moments in all of prophetic literature. He does not shout Hananiah down. He says, essentially, I hope you're right. Amen. May God do exactly what you have said. But here is the problem: prophets who predict peace are only confirmed as genuine when the peace actually arrives. And the pattern of the tradition runs the other way — toward warning, toward difficulty, toward the hard word rather than the comfortable one. Hananiah then does something dramatic: he takes the wooden yoke from Jeremiah's neck — Jeremiah had been walking around wearing an actual yoke as a living parable of Babylonian servitude — and smashes it. A powerful visual. A crowd-pleasing moment. And Jeremiah simply walks away.

The word that comes to Jeremiah afterward is sharp and sad: by breaking the wooden yoke, Hananiah has only guaranteed an iron one. The false mercy that tells people not to prepare for what is coming does not protect them from it; it only ensures they are caught unprepared. And Hananiah dies within the year, the text says, because he has "preached rebellion against the LORD" — not rebellion against the king, but against the God whose hard word Hananiah would not carry.

Psalm 119 sits perfectly in this context. "Remove from me the way of falsehood," it prays, "and favor me with your law." The whole psalm is a long, ardent love song to God's statutes — not because rules are pleasant in themselves, but because they are reliable. They do not shift with the mood of the crowd. The person who clings to them, the psalm says, is not put to shame. Hananiah, who did not cling to them, was.

The Gospel — Matthew 14:22-36

The disciples have just witnessed the feeding of the five thousand. You would think they would be steadied by that. Instead they are on a boat in the dark, the wind against them, terrified of a shape moving on the water — and even when Jesus identifies himself, even when Peter manages to step out onto the waves, the moment he looks at the wind, down he goes. The disciples are not failures; they are a very accurate picture of what faith actually looks like in practice: luminous one moment, floundering the next, always closer to shore than they realize.

There is something worth noticing in the detail that Jesus has gone up the mountain alone to pray. He does this after a full, exhausting day. The disciples are in the storm while Jesus is at prayer — and yet it is precisely from that prayer that he comes to them. The two things are not separate.

Peter's cry, "Lord, save me," is barely three words in Greek, and it is enough. The hand reaches out immediately. The mild rebuke — "O you of little faith, why did you doubt?" — is less a scolding than a genuine question, almost a philosophical one. What exactly was it that broke the spell? Not the wind itself, but the sight of the wind, the moment attention shifted from the person to the conditions.

Where falsehood and fear meet

These two readings are on separate tracks in terms of their narrative worlds — Jeremiah's Temple and the Sea of Galilee have nothing to do with each other historically. But they orbit the same question: what do you trust when things look impossible? Hananiah trusted the story that felt best, and it cost him everything. Peter trusted the wrong thing — the storm — for only a moment, and Christ's hand was already there before he finished sinking. The difference is not that Hananiah was a bad man and Peter was a good one. The difference is that Peter, even sinking, turned toward the right person and asked.

To carry today

Think of a place in your life where you are drawn to the comfortable explanation — the one that lets you off the hook, or keeps the situation from being as serious as it might be. It is worth asking honestly whether you are listening for Jeremiah or for Hananiah, for the true word or the welcome one. And when you feel the wind picking up and your footing goes uncertain, notice how quickly help comes when you stop arguing with the storm and simply ask.
