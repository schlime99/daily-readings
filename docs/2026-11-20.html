Thirty-Third Week in Ordinary Time

The word of God is sweet going in — and costs something on the way down.

First readingRevelation 10:8-11
PsalmPsalm 119:14, 24, 72, 103, 111, 131
GospelLuke 19:45-48

Two things happen to the word of God in today's readings: it is eaten, and it is defended. John swallows a scroll and finds that revelation is not all sweetness. Jesus drives merchants out of the temple and finds that fidelity is not without enemies. These are not unrelated observations. They are two angles on the same hard truth — that receiving the word of God and protecting the space where it lives both carry a price.

The first reading — Revelation 10:8-11

We are deep inside the Book of Revelation here, in the middle of the great vision sequence that begins in chapter four. John has already seen the heavenly throne room, the scroll with seven seals, the four horsemen, and the sounding of six of the seven trumpets. Chapter ten is a kind of pause in the action, an interlude before the seventh trumpet sounds. An enormous angel, feet planted on sea and land as though the whole world is a footstool, holds a small open scroll. A voice from heaven — not the angel's, something above even that — tells John to take it and eat it.

This scene deliberately echoes the prophet Ezekiel, who received almost identical instructions at the opening of his prophetic ministry. The scroll given to Ezekiel was written on both sides with "lamentation and wailing and woe," yet when he ate it, it was sweet as honey in his mouth. John receives the same paradox, stated even more starkly: sweet in the mouth, sour in the stomach. The word of God, fully received, works on you from the inside. It satisfies at first contact — there is genuine delight in truth, real sweetness in the promises of God — but digesting it means letting it change you, and that is where the sourness comes in. Prophecy is not a pleasant career. What John is about to receive and report will cost him something, and the sour stomach is the body's honest account of that.

The psalm falls directly in line with this image, almost as if it was chosen to comment on the scene. Psalm 119 is the great meditation on God's law and word, and the verses chosen today circle around taste: "How sweet to my palate are your promises, sweeter than honey to my mouth!" The psalmist is not naive. Throughout the full 176 verses of this psalm — the longest in the Psalter, an elaborate acrostic poem in which every letter of the Hebrew alphabet governs eight verses — the speaker is persistently in trouble, surrounded by enemies, struggling. The sweetness is not an escape from that; it is the sustenance that makes it survivable. Putting the psalm here, after John's scroll, says something worth sitting with: the same word that is sweet going in is what gets you through the souring.

The Gospel — Luke 19:45-48

Luke places the cleansing of the temple immediately after Jesus' entry into Jerusalem and his weeping over the city. He has just mourned that Jerusalem did not recognize "the time of its visitation" — the moment God came near. Now he walks into the temple and finds it cluttered with commerce. The selling of animals and exchange of currency were practical necessities in the system of temple sacrifice, and they had migrated over time from the outer courts into the Court of the Gentiles, the one space where non-Jews could come and pray. Jesus quotes two prophets at once, binding Isaiah's vision of the temple as "a house of prayer for all peoples" to Jeremiah's bitter charge that the people had turned it into "a den of robbers." He is not merely annoyed at a transaction; he is grieving a displacement. The house meant for encounter with God has become a place where encounter is crowded out.

What follows is quietly remarkable. Luke tells us that Jesus stayed, teaching in the temple every day. The authorities wanted him dead and were looking for an opening. And the reason they could not find one is given without drama: "all the people were hanging on his words." The Greek behind "hanging on" carries the sense of being suspended from something — dependent, weightless in attention. The crowd's hunger for the word was itself a protection. The temple, cleared of noise, became what it was meant to be: a place where people could actually hear.

Where the word needs room

The connection between these two readings is not about prophetic suffering on one side and temple piety on the other. It is about what has to be cleared away — from a space, from a person — before the word of God can actually land. John has to ingest the scroll; nothing about him can remain at a comfortable distance from what God is saying. Jesus has to clear the temple; nothing noisy and self-serving can remain in the space where God speaks. Both actions are disruptive. Both are loving. Both make room.

That sequence — clearing, then teaching, then the people suspended in attention — is worth holding up. The hearing follows the clearing. It is usually in that order.

To carry today

Consider today what has gotten loud in the space where you normally pray or think or simply sit still — not noise from outside, but the internal clutter of ambition, worry, or the low-grade busyness of the mind. Ask yourself whether there is something that needs to be driven out before you can actually hear. Then give yourself a few minutes of genuine quiet — not to perform calm, but to make room.
