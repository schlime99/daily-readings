Saints Martha, Mary, and Lazarus

Today the Church celebrates three people who are not apostles, not martyrs, not founders of anything — just a family in Bethany that loved Jesus and received him into their home. That is the whole reason they are here. The feast was long observed in some parts of the Church, but in 2021 it was placed on the universal calendar, which is the Church's way of saying: this kind of love is worth commemorating everywhere.

The first reading — Jeremiah 15:10, 16-21

This passage comes from what scholars call Jeremiah's "confessions" — a series of raw, almost shockingly personal complaints the prophet makes directly to God. Jeremiah is not a happy man. He is preaching doom to a people who do not want to hear it, and the cost is social isolation, hostility, and what sounds like serious spiritual exhaustion. He was called to speak before he was born, and by this point in the book he seems to regret that the deal was ever struck.

What makes the passage so striking is the way it swings between two registers. In the first movement, Jeremiah says he devoured God's words when he found them — they were his joy, his happiness, the thing that made his life feel alive and purposeful. This is not a man who came to prophecy reluctantly. He wanted it. The words were sweet. But the sweetness has curdled. Now he is sitting alone, under the weight of God's hand, filled with indignation he cannot put down, and the God who gave him the words has started to feel like a treacherous brook — a wadi that looks like a reliable stream and then, in the dry season, simply disappears.

That image of the dry wadi is one of the grimmer metaphors in the whole prophetic tradition. In a desert landscape, finding a brook that has gone dry when you were counting on it is not a minor disappointment. It is potentially lethal. Jeremiah is accusing God, gently but unmistakably, of being the thing he most needed and finding it gone when he arrived.

God's answer is not an apology. It is more like a reorientation: if you repent and come back, you will stand before me; if you can separate the precious from the worthless, you will speak for me again; and I will make you a brass wall that they cannot break. The promise is not comfort exactly — it is a commission renewed, which is what Jeremiah actually needs. The psalm chosen for the day echoes this: God is my refuge on the day of distress. Psalm 59 is the prayer of someone genuinely surrounded by enemies, and its refrain is not "everything is fine" but rather "you have been my stronghold even so."

The Gospel — John 11:19-27

The lectionary gives two options today. The first, from John 11, is the more theologically weighty, and it is the one worth sitting with longest.

Lazarus is already dead. The mourners are already at the house. Martha hears Jesus is coming and does something remarkable: she goes out to meet him on the road, before he reaches the house, before the tomb is opened, before anything has happened. It is an act of initiative that the text clearly wants us to notice, because it contrasts immediately with Mary, who stays home.

What Martha says when she reaches him is not simply complaint, though it begins there — "if you had been here, my brother would not have died." Anyone who has ever prayed urgently for something that did not happen will recognize that sentence. But Martha does not stop there. She adds, without being prompted: "But even now I know that whatever you ask of God, God will give you." She is grieving and she still believes. Those two things are sitting side by side in her, and she is not trying to resolve the tension.

When Jesus tells her "your brother will rise," she hears it the way any educated first-century Jew would: resurrection on the last day, the general resurrection that the Pharisees affirmed and the Sadducees denied. It is orthodox, it is true, and it is not quite what Jesus means. And so he corrects her — not harshly, but with precision: "I am the resurrection and the life." Not a resurrection that will happen. Not even a resurrection he will bring about. The resurrection is him, present, standing in front of her in the road.

Then the question: "Do you believe this?" Martha's answer is one of the great confessions of the Gospel of John. She does not say she fully understands it. She says she believes he is the Christ, the Son of God, the one coming into the world. This is the same formula the whole Gospel is written to produce in its readers. Martha gets there on a dusty road outside Bethany, in the middle of her grief, before the miracle.

A family and a prophet, both holding on

The connection between Jeremiah and Martha is not an obvious one, but it is real. Both of them are people who received something — the word of God, the friendship of God — and found that it cost more than they expected. Both of them continue anyway, not because the suffering has been explained to them, but because the one they are speaking to is still present. Jeremiah devoured the words and then felt abandoned; Martha's brother died while Jesus was somewhere else. Neither of them walks away. That persistence is not cheerful or tidy, but it is faith, and the readings together suggest that God takes that kind of faith seriously.

To carry today

Think of a place in your life where you prayed with genuine urgency and the answer did not come the way you hoped — where God felt, for a time, like that dry brook. Notice whether you are still standing in the road, like Martha, or have drifted back inside the house. You do not have to resolve the grief; you only have to stay close enough to speak.
