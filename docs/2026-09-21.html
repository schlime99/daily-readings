Monday · 21 September

Feast of

Saint Matthew, Apostle and Evangelist

A tax collector gets up from his table and walks into the rest of his life.

First readingEphesians 4:1-7, 11-13
PsalmPsalm 19:2-3, 4-5
GospelMatthew 9:9-13

There is a moment in Matthew's Gospel that lasts about three seconds in real time and encompasses everything the Church has ever said about vocation. A man is sitting at work. Jesus walks past. Two words: follow me. And the man gets up. Today we celebrate the feast of that man — Matthew, tax collector, apostle, and one of the four evangelists who gave us the written Gospels — and the readings chosen for his day turn out to be a quietly beautiful portrait of what it means to be called, and what you are called into.

The first reading — Ephesians 4:1-7, 11-13

Paul is writing from prison — he says so plainly, "a prisoner for the Lord" — which gives everything that follows a kind of stripped-down urgency. He is not writing from comfort; he is writing from a cell. And what he wants to say is this: hold together. The word he keeps hammering is one — one Body, one Spirit, one hope, one Lord, one faith, one baptism, one God. He is writing to a community that apparently needs the reminder, which is to say he is writing to every community that has ever existed.

But here is where it gets interesting for Matthew's feast. Paul goes on to say that Christ gave particular gifts to particular people — apostles, prophets, evangelists, pastors, teachers — not so that those people could feel important, but so that the whole Body could be built up. The offices exist for the community, not the other way around. Matthew received a specific gift: he would become an evangelist, someone whose written witness to Jesus would be read aloud at Mass in every century from his own until the end of time. That is a staggering thought. When we hear the Gospel proclaimed at Mass, Matthew's voice is in the room.

Psalm 19 is a natural response to all of this. The psalm is about proclamation — not human proclamation but cosmic proclamation, the way the heavens simply by existing declare the glory of God. "Day pours out the word to day, and night to night imparts knowledge." No sound, no speech, yet the message goes out through all the earth. The responsorial refrain chosen for today borrows that last line — their message goes out through all the earth — and applies it to the apostles. What the stars do silently, the apostles did in words, and the evangelists did in writing.

The Gospel — Matthew 9:9-13

The Gospel is the call itself, told by the man who received it. This is worth sitting with: Matthew is the one who wrote this story down. He chose to include it. He told us that he was sitting at the customs post — the toll booth, the place where a local Jewish man collected taxes on behalf of the Roman occupying power and therefore was seen by his neighbors as both a collaborator and a swindler — and that Jesus walked by and said "follow me" and that he got up and followed him.

Matthew does not record his own inner state. He does not say he was miserable or longing for something else or had secretly been following Jesus from a distance. The text is spare: Jesus spoke, Matthew stood. What we can say is that the call found him exactly where he was, not in a moment of religious seeking but in the middle of a workday, at a table, doing a job that had cut him off from his own people.

Then there is the dinner. Matthew throws what appears to be a going-away party, and his friends — the people who will still eat with him, which is to say other tax collectors and people of dubious reputation — all come. The Pharisees are scandalized. Their concern is legitimate by their own lights: ritual purity mattered, and table fellowship was not casual. To eat with someone was to say something about them and about yourself. Jesus' response is gentle but pointed. He is a physician, he says, and physicians go where the sick are. Then he quotes the prophet Hosea: "I desire mercy, not sacrifice." This was a well-known prophetic challenge to a kind of religion that had become so careful about the forms of devotion that it had forgotten what devotion was for. Jesus is not dismissing the Law; he is saying that mercy is the heart of it, and that the Pharisees' instinct to separate themselves from the unclean has confused the means with the end.

How a man at a tax booth becomes a voice for all time

The connection between the readings is not complicated but it is profound. Paul writes about the gifts given to build up the Church, and Matthew's feast is a living instance of it. Here is a man whose position made him a public sinner in the eyes of his community, and Jesus chose him to be one of the twelve, to sit with him at table, to follow him through the Passion, and eventually to write the account of his life that would be heard by more people than anyone in the ancient world could have imagined. The same mercy that brought Jesus to a dinner table full of tax collectors is the mercy that made Matthew an apostle. They are not two different moments; they are the same gesture, held open.

To carry today

Think about the work you sit down to each day — the ordinary table you return to, the task that defines you in other people's eyes and perhaps in your own. Consider that a call can arrive there, in the middle of it, without warning. Ask yourself whether you are listening for it, and whether there is something you have been holding onto that would have to be left at the table if you stood up.
