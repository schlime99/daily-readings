Twenty-second Week in Ordinary Time

Paul lists his hunger and homelessness as credentials; Jesus overrules the calendar with a name.

First reading1 Corinthians 4:6b-15
PsalmPsalm 145:17-21
GospelLuke 6:1-5

Two passages today about authority — who has it, where it comes from, and what it looks like when it is real. Paul writes from the margins, dirty and hungry, insisting that this is exactly where genuine apostolic authority lives. Jesus walks through a grain field and, with a single sentence, places himself above the most sacred institution in Jewish life. Together they make a strange kind of argument: the person who truly speaks for God rarely looks the part.

The first reading — 1 Corinthians 4:6b-15

First Corinthians is Paul's long, sometimes exasperated attempt to deal with a community that has fractured into factions. The Corinthians have sorted themselves into camps — some saying they belong to Paul, some to Apollos, some to Cephas — and they are proud about it, as if choosing the right spiritual mentor were a competitive achievement. By the time we reach chapter four, Paul has had enough of the performance. He turns the logic of status completely upside down, and the result is one of the most caustic passages he ever wrote.

The sarcasm here is pointed and deliberate: "You are already satisfied; you have already grown rich; you have become kings without us." He is not praising them. He is holding a mirror up to their self-satisfaction and letting them see how ridiculous it looks. The Corinthians have decided that spiritual success is something you can visibly accumulate — that wisdom, strength, and honor are signs that your faith is working. Paul's rebuttal is his own résumé: hungry, thirsty, poorly clad, roughed up, homeless. Not past humiliations he has since transcended, but his present reality. "To this very hour," he says. The degradation is ongoing. This is what genuine apostleship looks like.

The theological point buried in the sarcasm is worth pausing over. Paul asks, "What do you possess that you have not received?" It is a short question, but it dismantles the entire structure of spiritual pride. Every gift — intelligence, faith, eloquence, even the Gospel itself that first reached Corinth — came from somewhere else. Boasting about received gifts is like a river bragging about its water. And if everything is received, then the relevant question is not who has more, but who gave it.

He closes with something unexpectedly tender: he is not writing to shame them but to admonish them as a father would. "I became your father in Christ Jesus through the Gospel." He is the one who first preached to them; no one else can claim that. The distinction between a guide and a father is the difference between someone who offers directions and someone who is responsible for you. Paul is claiming the latter, and doing it gently, which is remarkable given how much of the passage is a controlled burn.

Psalm 145 answers this well — "The Lord is near to all who call upon him in truth." Not near to the successful or the honored, but near to the ones who call honestly. After Paul's description of what honest apostleship costs, that promise lands differently than it might otherwise.

The Gospel — Luke 6:1-5

This is an early conflict in Luke's account of Jesus's ministry. The Pharisees are watching him, not yet openly hostile but definitely keeping score. The disciples are hungry and do what people do when they are hungry and walking through a grain field — they pull a few heads, rub them in their palms to loosen the husks, and eat the grain. This was entirely legal; gleaning from another's field was explicitly permitted under Mosaic law. The issue, in the Pharisees' reading, was that rubbing the grain counted as a form of threshing, and threshing was work, and work was forbidden on the sabbath. The argument is not absurd on its own terms; it is the application of a real principle taken to its logical extreme.

Jesus's response reaches back to 1 Samuel and the story of David and his companions arriving at the sanctuary at Nob, exhausted and without provisions, and the priest giving them the bread of the presence — the consecrated loaves set out before God — because they needed to eat. Jesus is not saying rules don't matter; he is saying that David's hunger mattered to God more than strict ritual observance at that moment, and that his own disciples' hunger fits the same pattern. Then the conversation-stopper: "The Son of Man is lord of the sabbath." The sabbath belongs to him. He is not maneuvering within the law; he is identifying himself as its source.

What authority costs and what it claims

These two readings are genuinely on the same track today, which is not always the case on weekdays in Ordinary Time. Both of them are concerned with who gets to speak, and both of them locate real authority somewhere surprising. Paul's credentials are his deprivation — his hunger and homelessness and willingness to absorb insult without retaliating. Jesus's authority is not argued but named: he is the lord of the day itself. The Corinthians are trying to accumulate spiritual prestige by association; the Pharisees are trying to protect the law's integrity by surveillance. Both have mistaken the map for the territory. Paul points to what he has suffered; Jesus points to who he is. Neither answer looks impressive by ordinary measures.

To carry today

Think about the places in your life where you measure spiritual seriousness by what is visible — consistency, reputation, the company you keep in faith. Ask whether you are attending to the source of these things, or just to their appearance. Receive what you have been given as gift, and let that receiving be enough.
