Memorial of

Saint Ignatius of Loyola, Priest

The prophet speaks what he is given, and the crowd reaches for his throat.

First readingJeremiah 26:1-9
PsalmPsalm 69:5, 8-10, 14
GospelMatthew 13:54-58

There is a pattern running through today's readings that anyone who has ever said something true and unwelcome will recognize immediately. Jeremiah stands in the temple courtyard and delivers a message he did not choose, and by the time he finishes speaking, his audience is ready to kill him. Jesus returns to his own hometown and teaches in the synagogue, and the people who knew him as a child cannot get past the fact that they knew him as a child. The messenger is rejected because the message is too close, too inconvenient, too disruptive of what people have already decided to believe. On the feast of Ignatius of Loyola — a man who upended his entire life to follow a call he hadn't expected — this pattern carries a particular charge.

The first reading — Jeremiah 26:1-9

This passage opens a new section of Jeremiah, sometimes called the "temple sermon." We are at the very beginning of Jehoiakim's reign, probably around 608 BC, a moment of serious political anxiety in Judah. Babylon is rising. Egypt has just killed Jehoiakim's predecessor. The people are frightened, and the temple — the great symbol of God's presence and protection — is their reassurance. The Lord tells Jeremiah to go and stand in the temple court and speak everything he is commanded, and to omit nothing. That phrase is worth sitting with: omit nothing. Jeremiah is not given the freedom to soften the hard parts.

The substance of what he says is this: if the people do not turn back, the temple will become like Shiloh. Shiloh was where the Ark of the Covenant had been kept before the Philistines captured it — a sanctuary so thoroughly destroyed that its name had become shorthand for total devastation. To say the Jerusalem temple would become like Shiloh was to say the unthinkable out loud, in the temple itself, in front of the priests. It was roughly the equivalent of walking into a packed cathedral and announcing that God was going to reduce it to rubble. The reaction is predictable and immediate: the priests and the prophets — the professional religious class, the people whose authority and livelihood are bound up in this institution — seize Jeremiah and shout that he must die.

What makes the scene so painful is that Jeremiah is not there as an enemy. He is sent with a "perhaps" — perhaps they will listen and turn back. The message is simultaneously a threat and an appeal. God has not written these people off yet; the whole point of the prophet's errand is to create one more opening for repentance. But the messenger is grabbed before the message can land.

Psalm 69 answers this moment with an almost uncanny directness. It is the prayer of someone who is suffering unjustly for God's sake: surrounded by enemies without cause, cast out, bearing insults that were aimed at God and landed on the one who spoke for God. "Zeal for your house consumes me," the psalm says — and that line from the psalmist will later be applied to Jesus himself as he cleanses the temple. The psalm is not a tidy resolution; it is the raw cry of someone in the middle of it, still praying, still turning toward God even while the trap closes.

The Gospel — Matthew 13:54-58

Jesus comes back to Nazareth. Matthew tells us the people in the synagogue were astonished — and then immediately explains why their astonishment curdled into offense. They begin listing what they know about him: the carpenter's son, Mary's boy, brother of James and Joseph and Simon and Judas, whose sisters are right here among us. Every item on the list is true. None of it is wrong. And yet the very familiarity that should make them trust him — we know this man, we watched him grow up — becomes the reason they cannot receive him. They have already filed him away. He doesn't fit in the drawer they've opened.

Jesus responds with a saying that reads almost like a rueful observation rather than a rebuke: a prophet is without honor in his native place and in his own household. He is not surprised. He has, apparently, encountered this before. What is quietly devastating is the line that follows: he did not work many mighty deeds there because of their lack of faith. Not that he refused out of spite, but that faith and healing are not two separate events — the openness to receive is itself part of how grace works. A closed hand cannot take hold of what is being offered.

What the prophet costs

These two readings are not on separate tracks today; they are telling exactly the same story from two different moments in salvation history. The prophet speaks the word of God, the hearers cannot bear it, and the community that should have been most receptive — the one closest to the source — is the one most determined to silence him. Jeremiah's neighbors grab him in the temple court. Jesus' neighbors take offense in the synagogue. In both cases, proximity breeds not faith but a particular kind of stubbornness, the stubbornness of people who have already decided what God looks like and are not prepared to be corrected.

Ignatius of Loyola knew something about this. He was a soldier who had his leg shattered by a cannonball and spent his convalescence reading the lives of the saints — the only books on offer — and found, to his considerable surprise, that they moved him in a way that his fantasies of military glory did not. He eventually founded the Society of Jesus and sent men across the known world to preach and teach, often into communities that did not want them. He understood that the word given to you is not always the word that will be welcomed, and that this is not a reason to omit it.

To carry today

Think today about something true that you have held back because the person in front of you has already made up their mind, or because the relationship felt too close to risk. Is there something you are being called to say — or to hear — that you have been quietly filing away because you know how it will be received? Ask for the courage to neither silence the word nor harden against it.
