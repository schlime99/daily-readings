Twentieth Week in Ordinary Time

God asks Ezekiel to carry grief silently, and asks the young man to carry nothing at all.

First readingEzekiel 24:15-23
PsalmDeuteronomy 32:18-21
GospelMatthew 19:16-22

Two losses sit at the center of today's readings, and they could hardly feel more different. Ezekiel loses his wife and is commanded not to mourn. A young man in Galilee is asked to lose his possessions and walks away unable to do it. One man obeys at enormous personal cost; the other cannot, and the text doesn't condemn him — it just says he went away sad. There is room in that sadness for every one of us who has felt the pull of what God is asking and found it too heavy.

The first reading — Ezekiel 24:15-23

By the time we reach chapter 24 of Ezekiel, the prophet has already done a great deal of strange and painful things. God has used Ezekiel's body, his actions, his very home as a kind of living theater — enacted parables meant to reach a people who had stopped listening to words. The city of Jerusalem is under siege, and its fall is coming. And now God tells Ezekiel something devastating: his wife will die, the woman he calls "the delight of your eyes," and he is not to observe any of the customary mourning rites. No wailing. No uncovered head. No special bread brought by neighbors. Just silence and grief held inward.

And he does it. The text is almost unbearably spare: "That evening my wife died, and the next morning I did as I had been commanded." We don't get his feelings. We don't get a moment of interior struggle. We get the bare fact of obedience in the middle of loss.

When the people ask what it means, God gives the explanation: the Temple — the sanctuary, the delight of their eyes — is about to fall in the same way. And when it does, they too will be unable to mourn normally, not because they are commanded to silence, but because the scale of the catastrophe will be too vast for ordinary grief. They will "rot away because of sins and groan one to another" — a grief that has turned inward and gone bad, with nowhere to go.

The responsorial psalm, drawn from Moses's great canticle in Deuteronomy 32, belongs here exactly. "You have forgotten God who gave you birth" — the Rock, the one who begot them. Israel has chased after what the psalm calls "no-gods," translated sometimes as idols but meaning something closer to empty things, nothing-things. And God says he will respond with a "no-people," a foolish nation — meeting absence with absence. It is a theology of consequences that is severe and also strangely honest: what you reach for, you become like.

The Gospel — Matthew 19:16-22

The rich young man approaches Jesus with a real question, not a trick. "What good must I do to gain eternal life?" And Jesus, characteristically, turns the question back around: why are you asking about the good? There is only One who is good. It's a quiet redirection — you are looking for a thing to do, but what you need is a person to follow.

The young man has kept the commandments. He says so, and Jesus doesn't dispute it. There's something genuinely admirable here: he's not a fraud or a hypocrite. He has done what was asked and he knows, in some honest part of himself, that it isn't the whole answer. "What do I still lack?" is not a boast; it is a real hunger.

Jesus's answer is precise and personal. Not "someone in your situation ought to consider generosity." It's direct: go, sell what you have, give to the poor, then come, follow me. The sequence matters. First the letting go, then the following. And the young man finds he cannot do the first, so the second becomes impossible. He goes away sad, and the sadness is the Gospel's small act of mercy toward him — he knows what he's leaving behind, and the grief at least tells us he understood the value of what was offered.

What possession holds the place that should belong to God

The connection between these two passages is not the most obvious one, but it is real. Ezekiel is stripped of the person most precious to him — involuntarily, by loss — and is asked to carry that with extraordinary dignity. The young man is asked to release his possessions voluntarily, and finds he cannot. Both passages are asking what happens when God and the thing we love most come into conflict.

Ezekiel doesn't get to choose whether he loses his wife. But he does choose what he does with that loss — he makes it mean something, he offers it as a sign. The young man gets a choice, and his possessions win. The two readings, on separate tracks in the lectionary, arrive at the same question from opposite directions: what is the delight of your eyes, and is it holding the place that belongs to God?

To carry today

Consider today what you would find genuinely hard to release — not as an exercise in guilt, but as honest information about what actually holds your heart. It doesn't have to be money or property; it might be a plan, a reputation, a certain version of your own future. Ask whether that thing is in service of following, or quietly in the way of it.
