Memorial of

Saint Jerome, Priest and Doctor of the Church

Job cannot answer God once in a thousand times, yet still he cries out.

First readingJob 9:1-12, 14-16
PsalmPsalm 88:10-15
GospelLuke 9:57-62

Today the Church remembers Jerome, the great scholar-monk who translated the whole Bible into Latin and fought about it with nearly everyone he knew. He was brilliant, irascible, devoted, and difficult — a man whose life was proof that holiness does not require a pleasant temperament. The readings the Church offers today, whatever their own internal logic, happen to land with peculiar force on his feast: a man who dared to wrestle with God's word, and three would-be followers who hesitate at the threshold.

The first reading — Job 9:1-12, 14-16

We are deep inside the book of Job, past the opening catastrophe and into the long theological argument at the heart of the text. Job has lost everything — his children, his wealth, his health — and his three friends have arrived, as friends sometimes do, not to comfort him but to explain why he deserves it. Job's responses have a quality unlike almost anything else in scripture: they are honest to the point of being alarming. He does not deny God's power. In fact, he insists on it, and that is precisely his problem.

This passage is Job's attempt to describe what it would be like to take God to court. He imagines constructing a case, and then concedes that the exercise is hopeless — not because he is clearly in the wrong, but because the distance between a human being and the God who made the Bear and Orion and stretches out the heavens is simply too vast for argument. "He does great things past finding out," Job says, and there is wonder in it but also something close to despair. If God passed by, Job says, he would not even see him. If God seized him, no one could stop it. "Who can say to him, 'What are you doing?'"

What is stunning here is that Job is not abandoning faith. He is doing something harder — he is maintaining it in the face of an experience that makes God feel utterly inaccessible. He does not say God does not exist. He says he cannot reach him, cannot argue with him, and yet — and the psalm makes this explicit — he cries out anyway. Psalm 88 is the darkest psalm in the Psalter, a prayer that ends without resolution, without the usual turn toward trust. "Why, O LORD, do you reject me; why hide from me your face?" And the refrain is still: Let my prayer come before you. Job and the psalmist together model a faith that does not require God to feel near in order to keep speaking to him.

Jerome spent decades with these texts, handling every word. He knew Job's speeches as well as he knew anything. There is something fitting about his feast being anchored in this particular cry.

The Gospel — Luke 9:57-62

This passage sits at a hinge in Luke's Gospel. Jesus has just "set his face toward Jerusalem" — the pilgrimage toward the cross has begun, and the disciples are about to learn what following him at this stage of the journey actually costs. Three people approach him, or are approached by him, and each conversation ends the same way: with a demand that cuts through the reasonable.

The first man volunteers eagerly, and Jesus does not reward his enthusiasm but answers with a warning: the Son of Man has no permanent place to sleep. Do you understand what you are signing on for? The second is told to follow but asks to bury his father first — a duty so fundamental in Jewish culture that it could override almost anything — and Jesus says something that sounds, on first reading, almost cruel: "Let the dead bury their dead." What he means is that there are people whose lives are already oriented away from the Kingdom, and they can handle that task; this person is being called to something that cannot be deferred. The third wants to say goodbye to his family, which is what Elisha asked of Elijah and was granted, but Jesus is further along on a more urgent road: "No one who sets a hand to the plow and looks to what was left behind is fit for the Kingdom of God."

Jesus is not condemning family love or the burial of the dead. He is describing the particular quality of attention that following him requires at this moment. The plow image is practical: if you are watching where you came from, the furrow goes crooked.

The cost of full attention

These two readings are not really connected by theme — Job's situation and the three would-be disciples are quite different human moments. But they share something about what it means to face God without flinching. Job refuses to look away from his suffering or from God. The disciples are being asked not to look away from the road ahead. In both cases, the temptation is the same: to turn the head, to qualify the commitment, to manage the relationship at a safe distance.

Jerome spent his life with scripture precisely because he believed looking away was not an option. He was famously prickly, and he picked fights with people he admired, but on the central matter — that the word of God deserved the closest possible attention, at whatever personal cost — he never wavered. He is a complicated patron for this kind of day, which is perhaps why he is such a good one.

To carry today

Think about the thing you keep meaning to give your full attention — the relationship you defer, the honesty you owe someone, the prayer you shorten because the silence feels too large. Ask yourself what it would mean to stop managing from a careful distance and simply show up. That is what the day is asking.
