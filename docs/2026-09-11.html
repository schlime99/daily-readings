Twenty-third Week in Ordinary Time

Paul runs to win; Jesus asks what we can actually see.

First reading1 Corinthians 9:16-19, 22b-27
PsalmPsalm 84:3-6, 12
GospelLuke 6:39-42

Paul is training for something that will outlast him, and Jesus is asking whether we can see straight. These two are on separate tracks today — Paul's letter has its own argument running through weeks of weekday Masses, and the Gospel excerpt is a tight little cluster of sayings from the Sermon on the Plain. But taken together on a Friday morning, they make an unexpectedly honest pair: one about the discipline of the person who goes out to teach, the other about the blindness of the person who tries.

The first reading — 1 Corinthians 9:16-19, 22b-27

First Corinthians is Paul at his most argumentative, and the chapters around this passage are his defense of his own apostleship to a community that has started to question it. Corinth was a proud city, impressed by polished rhetoric and high status, and Paul had deliberately refused to perform that kind of superiority among them. He had even refused to take money for his preaching, which was unusual — teachers of his era expected payment — and some in Corinth read that refusal as a sign that his teaching wasn't worth much. Paul turns that criticism inside out. The refusal to be paid is precisely his boast, he says, because it proves the Gospel is genuinely free, not merchandise.

What follows is the famous "all things to all people" passage, which is often misread as a kind of spinelessness, as though Paul were simply telling people what they wanted to hear. But the context makes clear it is the opposite: he is describing real sacrifice, the willingness to enter someone else's world — their customs, their weakness, their specific confusion — in order to actually reach them. He is not softening the message; he is refusing to make his own cultural comfort a wall between the Gospel and someone who needs it.

Then the athletic imagery kicks in, and it is worth pausing here because Paul evidently loved it. Corinth was close to Isthmia, site of the Isthmian Games, one of the great athletic festivals of the ancient world, so this crowd would have known exactly what he was picturing: runners at full effort, a boxer working the body, years of pre-dawn discipline for a few minutes of competition. "They do it to win a perishable crown" — the victor's wreath, which was literally made of pine or celery, and wilted within days. Paul finds this almost darkly funny. All that training, all that sacrifice, for a crown that starts dying the moment you put it on. His point is not to mock the athletes but to shame himself and his readers into something sharper: if they run that hard for something that fades, how hard should we be running?

The psalm fits here beautifully, though it arrives from a completely different emotional register. Psalm 84 is a pilgrimage song, sung by someone traveling toward Jerusalem and aching to arrive. "My soul yearns and pines for the courts of the LORD" — this is not serene satisfaction but active longing. The sparrow and the swallow have found their nests near the altar; the pilgrim has not yet arrived and feels the distance keenly. It is the psalm of someone still on the road, which pairs with Paul's runner still in the race.

The Gospel — Luke 6:39-42

This short passage is part of Luke's Sermon on the Plain, the version of the Sermon on the Mount that takes place on level ground and is addressed to the disciples in the hearing of a great crowd. Jesus has just finished a long sequence on love of enemies and mercy, and now he shifts to a more pointed set of sayings about teachers and students, sight and blindness.

The opening question — can a blind person guide a blind person? — is almost too obvious to take seriously, which is of course the point. No one in the crowd would say yes. And then Jesus folds the disciples into that image: a student is not better than the teacher, and a poorly formed student leading others will simply multiply the original confusion. This is a quiet warning to anyone who has just heard the Sermon and is already itching to go correct someone.

The beam and the splinter are among the most vivid images in the Gospels, and they are funnier than we usually let them be. The Greek word for "beam" suggests a structural timber, the kind that holds up a ceiling. Jesus is painting a picture of someone walking around with a roof beam sticking out of their eye, squinting at a tiny speck of sawdust in someone else's, and offering to help. The comedy is the point. This is what self-righteousness actually looks like from the outside — enormous, obvious to everyone, invisible only to the person carrying it. The word "hypocrite" lands hard here; Jesus uses it without apology. He is not being unkind. He is being exact.

Discipline and vision

What connects Paul and Jesus today, obliquely, is the question of what serious formation actually produces. Paul trains his body and his will so that he does not become useless to the very Gospel he preaches. Jesus asks his disciples to do the harder work of examining their own sight before they attempt to correct anyone else's. Both are descriptions of what it takes to actually be a teacher rather than simply to perform the role. You can run aimlessly, Paul says. You can shadowbox. You can look busy without going anywhere. And you can guide the blind while being blind yourself, which ends the same way — everyone in the pit.

To carry today

Think today about some place in your life where you feel confident you see clearly — a conflict, a failing in someone close to you, a judgment you've held for a long time. Ask honestly whether your clarity there might be the kind that comes from having looked very hard outward and not very hard inward. You don't need to resolve the question; just hold it open for the day.
