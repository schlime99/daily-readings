Monday · 14 September

Feast of the

Exaltation of the Holy Cross

God lifted up his Son the way Moses lifted up the serpent — so that anyone who looks might live.

First readingNumbers 21:4b-9
PsalmPsalm 78:1bc-2, 34-35, 36-37, 38
Second readingPhilippians 2:6-11
GospelJohn 3:13-17

The cross is the strangest trophy in human history. Every other victory monument celebrates strength overcoming weakness; this one celebrates what looked, to everyone present, like catastrophic defeat. And yet the Church takes one whole day each year to stand in front of it and say: this is where God showed his hand. The Feast of the Exaltation of the Holy Cross does not ask us to look away from the suffering. It asks us to look straight at it, the way the Israelites in the desert looked at the bronze serpent — and live.

The first reading — Numbers 21:4b-9

This story comes near the end of forty years of wilderness wandering, and the people are exhausted. They have been here before, complaining against Moses and God, and they will be here again. What is worth noticing this time is the specific complaint: they are "disgusted with this wretched food." The food they are complaining about is the manna — the bread God sends from heaven every morning. They are sick of the miraculous provision. That detail has an almost darkly comic edge to it, until you recognize how familiar it is: the very gift that sustains us can become the thing we resent when we are tired enough.

The punishment is serpents — saraph serpents, which in Hebrew carries the sense of burning or fiery ones. People are bitten and die. The people repent and ask Moses to intercede. And God's response is not what you might expect. He does not take the serpents away. He tells Moses to make a bronze image of the very thing that is killing them and lift it up on a pole, so that anyone bitten can look at it and live. The cure requires looking directly at the source of the wound.

Psalm 78 sits here like a long sigh of recognition. It rehearses Israel's pattern: they forgot God, suffered, remembered him, sought him again, then flattered him with their lips while their hearts were elsewhere, and yet — "being merciful, he forgave their sin and destroyed them not." That refrain, "Do not forget the works of the Lord," is not a scolding. It is the psalm's attempt to break the amnesia before it sets in again.

The second reading — Philippians 2:6-11

This passage is one of the oldest pieces of Christian poetry we have. It runs on its own track — it is not a commentary on Numbers or on John; it is a hymn that the early Church was already singing when Paul quoted it in his letter. But it is perhaps the most precise theological statement of what the feast is about. Christ, who was in the form of God, did not treat that equality as something to hold onto. He emptied himself — the Greek word is kenosis, self-emptying — taking the form of a slave, becoming obedient to death, even death on a cross. And then the turn: because of this, God exalted him. The humiliation is not the prologue to the exaltation; it is the mechanism of it. The descent is itself the ascent, seen from the other side.

The Gospel — John 3:13-17

This comes from Jesus' conversation with Nicodemus, the Pharisee who came to him at night — a man who wanted to understand but was not yet ready to be seen wanting to understand. Jesus draws the thread from Numbers directly: just as Moses lifted up the serpent in the desert, so the Son of Man must be lifted up. The word "lifted up" in John's Gospel does double work. It means crucified, and it means glorified. Jesus uses it throughout the Gospel to describe his death, and every time, the lifting up onto the cross is also the lifting up in triumph. John will not let you separate them.

Then comes the sentence that may be the most quoted verse in all of scripture: "God so loved the world that he gave his only Son." It is quoted so often that it can slide past without landing. What it actually says is that the motivation behind the cross is not wrath needing satisfaction, not a ledger needing to be balanced, but love — love of the world, which in John's vocabulary means precisely the world in its brokenness and resistance and dark. God did not send his Son to condemn that world. He sent him to save it.

Looking at the wound

What connects Numbers to John is the act of looking. The Israelites had to turn toward the bronze serpent — the image of the very thing that was killing them — in order to be healed. Jesus tells Nicodemus that he will be lifted up in the same way, so that everyone who looks, everyone who believes, will not perish. The cross is not something to glance at and move on from. It is the place you look when you have been bitten, when the wound is real, when the wilderness has gone on longer than you thought you could bear. You look at the thing that should mean defeat, and somehow, looking honestly at it, you find life on the other side.

The feast does not paper over the suffering. It holds it up. That is the exaltation.

To carry today

Think of something in your life that you have been turning away from because it is painful to look at directly — a failure, a grief, a fear you have been managing around the edges. The movement this feast asks for is not to wallow in it, but to look at it honestly and bring it to God, who has already been there ahead of you. Ask for the grace to stop averting your eyes.
