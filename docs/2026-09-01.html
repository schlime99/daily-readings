Twenty-second Week in Ordinary Time

The demon knows exactly who Jesus is, and says so — which is more than anyone else manages.

First reading1 Corinthians 2:10b-16
PsalmPsalm 145:8-9, 10-11, 12-13ab, 13cd-14
GospelLuke 4:31-37

There is something almost vertiginous about today's pair of readings. Paul is explaining that real knowledge of God can only come from inside — that the Spirit alone can search the depths of God and share what it finds. Then we go to Capernaum, and the first creature to announce publicly who Jesus really is turns out to be a demon. The Spirit knows everything, Paul says. And in Luke's synagogue, we watch that claim made grotesquely literal.

The first reading — 1 Corinthians 2:10b-16

This passage falls near the opening of First Corinthians, in a section where Paul is working through a problem that is plaguing the community there: they are dividing into factions around teachers they admire, measuring wisdom by the standards of the surrounding Greek culture, which prized rhetorical sophistication and philosophical cleverness. Paul has been insisting that the Gospel does not fit those categories. And now he makes his deepest argument for why.

His starting point is a comparison drawn from ordinary human experience. Nobody knows what is going on inside you except you — your own spirit, your own inner life. You might act cheerful and be falling apart. You might seem confident and be riddled with doubt. From the outside, you are partially opaque. Paul says the same logic applies to God. What is deepest in God, what truly pertains to God, is simply not accessible by looking from the outside. No amount of human reasoning, however sophisticated, can work its way inward to the depths of divine life. The only one who knows those depths, Paul says, is the Spirit of God, who is inside that life the way your own spirit is inside yours.

This is not a counsel of despair. The point is not that God is locked away and unknowable. The point is that the only way to know what God is freely giving is to receive the Spirit who knows it. And Paul claims that is exactly what has happened: "We have not received the spirit of the world but the Spirit who is from God." The Corinthian Christians, and Paul with them, have been given from the inside what no external inquiry could have reached.

Then Paul gets careful — and honest — about what this means for how you communicate it. Spiritual realities require spiritual language; they do not translate cleanly into the vocabulary of human wisdom. Someone who does not have the Spirit will look at the Gospel and find it foolish, not because they are stupid, but because they are operating with the wrong instrument. You cannot hear a frequency you have not been tuned to receive. He closes with a quotation from Isaiah — "who has known the mind of the Lord?" — and then adds the answer the prophet did not give: we have the mind of Christ. That is a startling claim, and it is worth sitting with. Paul means that because the Spirit of Christ has been given to us, we can begin to think from inside the love and the logic of God, rather than just about it from outside.

Psalm 145 answers Paul's portrait of divine depth with a portrait of divine character. The God whose inner life the Spirit alone knows is, that Spirit reveals, "gracious and merciful, slow to anger and of great kindness." The depth of God turns out to be tenderness. That is not obvious from outside; it is a disclosure.

The Gospel — Luke 4:31-37

This is early in Luke's account of Jesus' public ministry. He has just been driven out of Nazareth, his hometown, for saying things the locals could not stomach. So he comes to Capernaum, a working fishing town on the Sea of Galilee, and begins teaching in the synagogue on the sabbath. The crowd is astonished at his teaching because he speaks "with authority" — not citing other teachers, not hedging, but as someone who knows.

Then a man with an unclean spirit begins to cry out. And what the demon says is remarkable: "I know who you are — the Holy One of God." The assembled congregation is amazed, but they have not yet said anything like that. The evil spirit has gone straight to the truth. There is a bitter irony in this that Luke clearly intends. The people around Jesus are astonished and impressed and beginning to wonder; the demon has no wondering left to do. It knows, with a precision the disciples will spend years approaching.

Jesus does not accept the testimony. He silences it — "Be quiet!" — and commands the spirit out. This is a pattern in Luke's Gospel: Jesus consistently refuses to let demons announce him. The correct recognition of who he is should not come from that source; it must be received, not merely identified. The demon knows a fact. That is not the same as the knowledge Paul is describing.

The crowd's response is the one Luke wants us to share: "What is there about his word? With authority and power he commands the unclean spirits, and they come out." They are not quite ready to answer their own question. But they are asking it, and that is how it begins.

Authority and its source

These two readings are not artificially paired. What Paul is describing — knowledge that comes from within, from the Spirit of God — is exactly what the crowd is sensing in Jesus without yet being able to name. The people in the synagogue hear someone whose word carries weight, whose command lands. The demon, operating purely on recognition, identifies the source: "the Holy One of God." Paul would say the crowd can only truly grasp that identification if the Spirit opens it to them. Not as a fact to be catalogued, but as a reality to be received.

To carry today

Consider the difference between knowing something about a person and actually knowing them — the way familiarity can sit on the surface while real understanding stays out of reach. Ask today whether your knowledge of God is the kind that has been given from inside, through prayer and quiet and attention, or whether it has stayed mostly at the level of facts and arguments you have assembled from outside. That is not a question with a tidy answer, but it is worth holding.
