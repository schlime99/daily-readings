Feast of the

Dedication of the Lateran Basilica in Rome

A river from the sanctuary's threshold makes the salt sea fresh and feeds trees that never stop bearing.

First readingEzekiel 47:1-2, 8-9, 12
PsalmPsalm 46:2-3, 5-6, 8-9
Second reading1 Corinthians 3:9c-11, 16-17
GospelJohn 2:13-22

Today the Church pauses in Ordinary Time to celebrate a building — not because stone and marble are what matter, but because stone and marble have been trying to say something for a very long time. The Lateran Basilica in Rome, the cathedral of the Bishop of Rome, was consecrated in 324, the oldest of the great Roman basilicas, and the feast of its dedication has been kept by the universal Church for centuries. What the readings do, beautifully, is refuse to let the celebration stay about architecture.

The first reading — Ezekiel 47:1-2, 8-9, 12

Ezekiel is a prophet of the exile, writing for people who had watched Jerusalem fall and the temple burn. His visions are elaborate, almost hallucinatory, full of wheels within wheels and creatures with four faces. By chapter 47, near the very end of the book, he is in the middle of a prolonged vision of a rebuilt, restored Jerusalem, and an angel is leading him around a renewed temple. What he sees flowing from beneath the threshold is water — a trickle at first, rising to a stream, then a river you have to swim across.

The vision is doing something that takes a moment to register: in the ancient world, Jerusalem was not on a river. It had no navigable waterway, no Nile, no Euphrates. The city's survival depended on cisterns and the Pool of Siloam. So when Ezekiel sees a river pouring from the sanctuary itself, he is describing something impossible by ordinary geography, which is exactly the point. The source of life, in this renewed world, is not the land's natural abundance but the presence of God in the holy place. The water flows east, into the Arabah — the desert rift valley — and then into the sea, which in Ezekiel's day meant the Dead Sea, that body of water so saturated with salt that nothing lives in it. And the river makes it fresh. Fish multiply there. Along both banks, trees fruit every month, their leaves good for healing.

The psalm picks this vision up directly. Psalm 46 is one of the great "Songs of Zion," a poem celebrating Jerusalem as God's city, and its refrain today is precisely about a river: the waters of the river gladden the city of God. The psalm's river, like Ezekiel's, is not literal — Jerusalem had no such thing — but it names a truth about what God's presence does. It makes life possible where life otherwise would not be.

The second reading — 1 Corinthians 3:9c-11, 16-17

Paul is writing to a community he founded in Corinth, a city of merchants, sailors, and competing philosophies, and by chapter 3 he is already managing factions — some people claiming Paul, others Apollos, others Cephas. He is pushing back hard: neither he nor Apollos is the point. God is building something, and Paul and Apollos are just laborers on the site. The only foundation that holds is Jesus Christ.

Then he makes a turn that is startling once you notice it: "Do you not know that you are the temple of God?" Not the building in Rome, not the Jerusalem sanctuary — the community itself, the gathered people, the body of believers in a messy port city in Greece. The Spirit lives there. Paul means this seriously enough to follow it with a warning: whoever damages that community damages what God is building, and God will not regard that lightly. This reading runs somewhat on its own track from Ezekiel and John, following its own argument about factions and foundations. But it arrives at the same destination from a different direction: holiness lives in the gathered people, not only in the walls.

The Gospel — John 2:13-22

John places the cleansing of the temple near the beginning of his Gospel, not near the end as the other evangelists do — a deliberate choice that sets the terms for everything that follows. It is early in Jesus's public ministry, and he walks into the temple courts and finds them functioning as a market. The commerce was arguably necessary — pilgrims needed animals for sacrifice, and foreign coins had to be exchanged for the temple currency — but something about what he sees breaks open a ferocity in him. He makes a whip. He overturns tables. The disciples, watching, remember a psalm: zeal for your house will consume me.

When the authorities challenge him, he gives them something they cannot parse: "Destroy this temple and in three days I will raise it up." They hear him talking about Herod's building project, forty-six years in the making. John tells us quietly that he was talking about his body. The disciples only understand this later, after the resurrection — a detail John includes to show us how his Gospel works. The meaning is not always available at first hearing. It becomes available through what comes after.

What water and body share

The movement from Ezekiel to John runs through the same image. Ezekiel's river flows from the sanctuary and brings life to dead water. In John, Jesus is the sanctuary. The life-giving presence that Ezekiel placed in a building, John places in a person — and when that person is handed over and raised up, the river begins to run in earnest. The stone temple of Jerusalem will fall again, in 70 AD, within living memory of John's writing. The temple of Christ's body does not.

The feast of a building, it turns out, is a feast about a person, and about the community that person inhabits.

To carry today

Paul's question — "Do you not know that you are the temple of God?" — is worth sitting with not as a compliment but as a genuine inquiry. Where in your daily life do you act as though the holy is somewhere else, somewhere you haven't reached yet? Consider once today what it would mean to take that question at face value, and to treat yourself, and the person next to you, accordingly.
