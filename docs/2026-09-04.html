Twenty-second Week in Ordinary Time

The bridegroom is here, and the old wineskins cannot hold what he is pouring.

First reading1 Corinthians 4:1-5
PsalmPsalm 37:3-4, 5-6, 27-28, 39-40
GospelLuke 5:33-39

There is a quiet friction running through today's readings, the kind that comes when something genuinely new arrives and the existing frameworks are not quite big enough to receive it. Paul is pressing one version of this — who gets to judge, and when — and Jesus is pressing another, more exhilarating one: the age of feasting has begun, and you cannot use the old containers.

The first reading — 1 Corinthians 4:1-5

Paul is writing to a church in Corinth that has splintered into factions, each one rallying behind a favorite preacher and dismissing the others. Some of them are judging Paul himself, measuring him against other apostles and finding him wanting. His response is one of the more striking passages in any of his letters, because he refuses to play the game on either side: he does not defend himself, but he also does not dismiss the charge by claiming clear conscience. "I am not conscious of anything against me," he says, "but I do not thereby stand acquitted."

That is worth sitting with. Paul is not saying that innocence is a feeling we can audit in ourselves. He is saying something harder — that self-knowledge has limits, that the heart's true motives are not fully available even to the person who carries them. We can be self-deceived without knowing it. We can be compromised in ways we cannot see. This is not cause for anxiety; it is cause for honesty about where final judgment belongs.

And that judgment, Paul says, belongs to God alone, and it has not happened yet. "Do not make any judgment before the appointed time." This is not a license for moral indifference — Paul elsewhere judges very plainly when genuine harm is being done. This is something narrower: a warning against rendering a verdict on another person's interior life, their motives, the ultimate state of their soul. That accounting will come, and when it does, God "will bring to light what is hidden in darkness and will manifest the motives of our hearts." What follows is startling: "everyone will receive praise from God." Not condemnation — praise. Whatever is true, and good, and faithful will finally be seen and named.

Psalm 37 fits beautifully alongside this. It is a psalm for people who are watching wrongdoers flourish and wondering why God is slow to act. Its answer is patient trust: "Commit to the LORD your way; trust in him, and he will act. He will make justice dawn for you like the light." The vindication Paul describes and the vindication the psalm promises are the same thing — real, coming, but not yet.

The Gospel — Luke 5:33-39

These two readings are genuinely on separate tracks in terms of their subject matter, but there is something in the Gospel that quietly illuminates why Paul's patience makes sense. Jesus is being questioned about fasting — the disciples of John fast regularly, the Pharisees' disciples fast regularly, but Jesus's disciples eat and drink. The implication is that Jesus's people are not serious enough, not austere enough, not properly religious.

His answer is a wedding. You do not fast at a wedding, he says, not while the bridegroom is still in the room. Fasting will come, and it has its place, but right now something else is happening: the age of arrival, the feast of presence, the thing all the fasting was preparing for. The hunger has been answered.

Then come the parables, and they carry a gentle sting. New cloth does not belong on an old garment — the patch shrinks when washed and tears the fabric worse than before. New wine does not go into old wineskins — the fermentation expands, the stiff old skins crack, and you lose both the wine and the vessel. The point is not that the old is bad. The old garment is still a garment. The old wineskins served a real purpose in their time. The problem is not their quality but their capacity. They cannot hold what is now being poured.

And then the final line, which carries a rueful honesty: "No one who has been drinking old wine desires new, for he says, 'The old is good.'" Jesus does not deny it. The old wine is good. Long religious practice, familiar forms, tested disciplines — these are good things. But comfort with the good can make us resistant to the better, especially when the better arrives in a form we did not expect. The scribes and Pharisees are not wicked men. They are people whose containers were full, and who therefore could not recognize that something larger had come.

What patient trust has to do with new wine

Paul and Jesus are circling the same human tendency from different angles: the impulse to render premature verdicts. Paul's judges in Corinth have already decided who is a real apostle and who is not. The scribes questioning Jesus have already decided what real devotion looks like. Both groups are working with old categories applied too quickly. Paul's answer is to wait on God's revealing light. Jesus's answer is to notice that the moment of revelation has already begun — the bridegroom is present, the new wine is being poured, and the only question is whether you have a vessel that can receive it.

To carry today

Think of a judgment you have made about someone — their character, their sincerity, their worth — and held quietly but firmly for some time. Ask yourself whether you are really seeing them, or whether you are working with an old container: fixed expectations, past experience, a framework you formed before all the evidence was in. The light that reveals all motives falls on yours as well as theirs. Sit with that for a moment, and let it soften something.
