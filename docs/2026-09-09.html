Memorial of

Saint Peter Claver, Priest

Peter Claver called himself the slave of the enslaved — and meant it.

First reading1 Corinthians 7:25-31
PsalmPsalm 45:11-12, 14-15, 16-17
GospelLuke 6:20-26

Today the Church remembers Peter Claver, the seventeenth-century Spanish Jesuit who spent more than four decades in the port of Cartagena — in what is now Colombia — meeting slave ships as they arrived. He would go into the holds, where conditions were almost unimaginable, and begin there. He called himself the slave of the enslaved and signed his name that way until he died. The readings the Church has set for an ordinary Wednesday in early September don't appear to be about him at all, and yet they are, in the way the liturgy often works: not by illustration but by pressure.

The first reading — 1 Corinthians 7:25-31

Paul is writing to the church at Corinth around the mid-50s, and the community is full of anxious questions about the practical shape of Christian life. This passage takes up the question of marriage and virginity, and Paul gives what he freely admits is his opinion rather than a divine commandment — a note of intellectual honesty that is itself worth pausing on. He thinks, all things considered, that people should generally stay as they are. If you're married, stay married. If you're not, don't feel compelled to seek a spouse.

His reasoning is urgent rather than romantic: the time is running out, and the world in its present form is passing away. This is the language of apocalyptic expectation — Paul genuinely believed the return of Christ was imminent, and he was counseling the Corinthians accordingly. Whatever you are, he says, hold it loosely. Those with wives should live as though they had none; those who weep or rejoice should do so without becoming captive to those states; those who buy things should carry them lightly, as tenants rather than owners.

The point is not that marriage is lesser, or that grief is forbidden, or that joy should be suppressed. The point is attachment — how thoroughly we let the present arrangement of things define us and determine us. Paul wants the Corinthians to develop a kind of inner freedom, a readiness to be moved, that the goods of this age don't foreclose. The world in its present form is passing away, which means there is a form to come, and our grip on the current one shouldn't be so tight that we can't receive it.

Psalm 45 is a royal wedding psalm — ancient, richly imagistic, and on the surface entirely about an earthly king and his bride. The tradition has long read it as pointing toward something more: the soul's relationship to God, the Church's relationship to Christ. Paired with Paul's passage about virginity and detachment, the psalm quietly redirects: the ultimate spouse is not of this world, and there is a gladness and joy in that bridal procession the psalm describes that no present affliction can finally cancel.

The Gospel — Luke 6:20-26

Luke's version of the Beatitudes is shorter and sharper than Matthew's, and where Matthew has "blessed are the poor in spirit," Luke simply says "blessed are you who are poor." He is talking to actual people in actual poverty, and the blessing is equally actual. There are no qualifiers here. And then, crucially, Luke adds the Woes — the counterpart to the Beatitudes that Matthew omits. Woe to the rich, to the satisfied, to those who laugh now, to those of whom everyone speaks well. The reversals run in both directions.

This is startling and, if we are honest, uncomfortable. The Woes are not a warning about attitude toward wealth — they are addressed to people who are, simply, wealthy, full, comfortable, and well-regarded. The problem is not that they feel bad about it. The problem is that they have already received what this present world offers, and it has cost them the hunger that opens a person to something more. Jesus is not scorning the contented; he is telling them that contentment has a price they may not have noticed.

The Alleluia before the Gospel quotes the Beatitudes directly — "Rejoice and leap for joy, your reward will be great in heaven" — and there is something slightly wild about that instruction. This is not polite satisfaction. It is the joy of people who have been stripped of every earthly reason for joy and found, to their surprise, that they are not empty.

The poverty that creates room

What Paul and Luke are circling, from entirely different angles, is the question of what we are full of. Paul wants the Corinthians to carry the world's arrangements lightly — not rejecting them, but not being defined by them, holding them with open hands. Luke's Jesus pronounces blessed those who are already empty by the world's measure, who are hungry and weeping and excluded, because they have room. Room for the Kingdom, room for satisfaction that comes from somewhere else.

Peter Claver understood this in a way most of us are still working toward. He spent his life among people from whom everything had been taken — dignity, family, freedom, country — and he met them there not as a benefactor condescending to the wretched but as one who had himself practiced a rigorous emptying. What he found in those holds, in the people the world had most thoroughly discarded, was not despair. It was the Kingdom.

To carry today

Somewhere in your life today, you are probably full of something — a certainty, a comfort, a status you're quietly protective of — that is leaving less room for what God is trying to offer. It doesn't have to be wealth in the usual sense. Ask yourself where you are most defended, most satisfied in a way that has closed something off, and whether you are willing to loosen your grip there even slightly.
