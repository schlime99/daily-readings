Memorial of

Saint John Vianney, Priest

God names the wound honestly, then promises to heal it anyway.

First readingJeremiah 30:1-2, 12-15, 18-22
PsalmPsalm 102:16-18, 19-21, 29, 22-23
GospelMatthew 14:22-36

There is a particular kind of honesty that only someone who loves you can deliver — the kind that names how bad things really are before offering any comfort. The Book of Jeremiah is full of this, and today's passage is one of its most striking examples. God speaks with a directness that could sound brutal, and then, without pausing for breath, turns entirely toward restoration. This is the shape of the day: wound named, then healed; sinking acknowledged, then caught.

The first reading — Jeremiah 30:1-2, 12-15, 18-22

We are deep in what scholars sometimes call the "Book of Consolation," chapters 30 and 31 of Jeremiah, which sit like an island of hope inside one of the bleakest prophetic books in the Bible. Jeremiah has spent most of his ministry delivering terrible news to Judah — conquest is coming, the city will fall, there is no avoiding it. And then he is told to write something down. The instruction to write is itself significant; this is a word meant to outlast the moment, a promise intended for future generations to read.

What gets written begins with frank diagnosis. The wound is "incurable," the bruise "grievous." No one is coming to advocate for Israel's case. The "lovers" who have abandoned the people are the foreign powers Judah courted for security — Egypt, Assyria — political alliances made instead of trusting God. They have vanished, as fair-weather allies do. God says plainly: this suffering came from real guilt, not from accident or bad luck. There is something almost merciful in this refusal to soften the picture. A physician who tells you exactly what is wrong, even when it is serious, is treating you as someone who can bear the truth.

Then the turn. "See! I will restore the tents of Jacob." The word "tents" reaches back to the wilderness wandering, to the portable, provisional life before Israel had cities and palaces — God is remembering the whole story of his people, not just the recent disaster. The city will be rebuilt. Songs will come back. The population will grow again. And then the most intimate promise in the passage: "When I summon him, he shall approach me" — which the text itself glosses as an almost impossible intimacy, one that ordinarily carries a mortal risk. The people will be near enough to come when called. The covenant formula that closes the reading — "You shall be my people, and I will be your God" — is one of the oldest refrains in all of Scripture, and hearing it after all that darkness carries enormous weight.

Psalm 102 follows naturally, almost as Israel's response to what God has just promised. It is the psalm of someone who has been in ruins — the psalmist speaks from exactly the condition Jeremiah described — and who lifts their eyes toward a God who "regarded the prayer of the destitute, and not despised their prayer." The refrain, "The Lord will build up Zion again, and appear in all his glory," is not wishful thinking; it is confidence built on what God has done before.

The Gospel — Matthew 14:22-36

The walking on the water is one of those stories that has been so often illustrated and summarized that we can forget how strange and particular it is. Jesus has just fed the five thousand, a moment of enormous public energy, and he sends everyone away — the crowds and then even the disciples — so that he can go up the mountain to pray. He is alone in the dark, praying, while the boat is already miles out and fighting the waves. The detail about the "fourth watch" is worth noting: that is between three and six in the morning, the last watch of the night, the darkest and longest stretch.

When the disciples see him coming toward them across the water, their first response is sheer terror. They think he is a ghost. Jesus speaks before anything else: "Take courage, it is I; do not be afraid." The phrase "it is I" is, in the Greek, literally ego eimi — I am — the same construction the Gospel of John uses for Jesus's great self-declarations. Matthew's readers would have caught the echo.

Peter's request is endearing and a little reckless, as Peter usually is. He does not wait to be invited; he wants to be commanded to come. And Jesus says simply, "Come." Peter walks on the water until he notices the wind, and then the noticing is what undoes him. He does not fail because the waves grow stronger; he fails because he looks away from the person who told him to come. Jesus catches him immediately — the word is urgent, instantaneous — and the mild rebuke, "O you of little faith, why did you doubt?" is asked by someone who is already holding him up. The question does not come before the rescue; it comes after.

Wound named and hand extended

What Jeremiah and Matthew share today is this sequence: the honest accounting of what has gone wrong, followed immediately by the act of rescue that does not wait for the person to have deserved it. Israel's wound is real, and God names it — and then restores. Peter's doubt is real, and Jesus names it — and has already caught him. The naming is not the end of the story in either case. It is part of the mercy.

On the memorial of Saint John Vianney, the patron of parish priests, who heard confessions for sixteen and eighteen hours a day and who understood better than most that people come to God already half-sunk, this pairing feels entirely right. He believed that no wound was too old or too deep for what was being offered.

To carry today

Think of something in your own life you have been reluctant to name honestly — some pattern, some failure, some fear — because naming it felt like admitting there was no way out. Consider that the naming and the rescue are not opposites. Ask for the grace to say what is true about yourself, because the hand is already extended.
