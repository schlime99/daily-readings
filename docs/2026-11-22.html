Sunday · 22 November

The Solemnity of

Our Lord Jesus Christ, King of the Universe

The King reveals himself in the hungry face, the stranger at the door, the prisoner no one visits.

First readingEzekiel 34:11-12, 15-17
PsalmPsalm 23:1-2, 2-3, 5-6
Second reading1 Corinthians 15:20-26, 28
GospelMatthew 25:31-46

Today is the last Sunday of the liturgical year, the feast that closes the whole cycle. The Church has spent twelve months walking from Advent through Christmas, through Lent and Easter and the long green stretch of Ordinary Time, and now she sets a crown on the whole thing — not a crown of gold and ceremony, but the strange crown this King actually wears. The readings are asking us to look hard at what kind of king Jesus is, and the answer is not what anyone expecting a throne would have guessed.

The first reading — Ezekiel 34:11-12, 15-17

Ezekiel was a priest and a prophet writing from exile in Babylon, probably around 590 BCE, and the chapter this passage comes from is one of the angriest in his book. What comes before these verses — the part we don't hear today — is a blistering indictment of the shepherds of Israel, meaning the kings and priests and leaders who fed themselves instead of the flock, who let the weak grow weaker while the strong grew fat. It's a remarkable piece of rhetoric, full of contempt for people in authority who forgot what authority is for.

Then God makes a turn. If the human shepherds have failed this badly, then God himself will come and do the job. "I myself will look after and tend my sheep" — the word myself is emphatic in a way that matters. No intermediary, no deputy, no second chance given to the same class of leaders who already failed. God comes personally: seeking the lost, binding the injured, healing the sick.

But the passage ends on a note that often gets glossed over: "the sleek and the strong I will destroy." This good shepherd is not sentimental. He will judge, and specifically he will judge between those who had power and used it to crowd out the vulnerable, and those who didn't. The rams and the goats of verse 17 aren't moral categories yet — that image is coming in the Gospel — but the structural point is clear. Strength used against the weak is not neutral. It will be named.

Psalm 23 answers this reading so directly it almost reads as a commentary on it. "The Lord is my shepherd" — the congregation responds to Ezekiel's promise by claiming it. The psalm moves through exactly the verbs Ezekiel listed: rest, refreshment, guidance, care at table. It's a song of trust in the shepherd who actually shows up.

The second reading — 1 Corinthians 15:20-26, 28

This passage is on its own track, not woven into the shepherd imagery of the other two readings, but it belongs on this feast for a different reason: it explains the scope of Christ's kingship. Paul is writing about resurrection, and specifically about the sequence of events at the end of time. Christ is raised first — the "firstfruits," which is a harvest image meaning the first portion that proves the rest of the crop is coming. Then those who belong to him. Then the end, when every power and sovereignty that set itself up against God is dismantled, and finally death itself, the last enemy, is destroyed.

The logic is clean and enormous. Adam brought death into the human story; Christ brings life back into it. And Christ's reign is not just over the Church, or over believers, or over history — it is over the whole created order, until every last thing that was bent by sin has been set straight. The solemnity of Christ the King is not a small claim. It is the claim that the arc of everything bends toward him.

The shepherd who is also the king

The Gospel is where the two images — shepherd and king — come together, and the result is one of the most searching passages in Matthew.

The Son of Man comes in glory and sits on his throne, and all the nations are assembled before him. And he separates them — as a shepherd separates sheep from goats, Matthew says, pulling the thread from Ezekiel straight through. The criterion turns out to be devastatingly simple and devastatingly hard: did you feed the hungry, welcome the stranger, clothe the naked, care for the sick, visit the prisoner?

What is stunning about this scene is the surprise on both sides. The righteous didn't know they were serving the king. They ask, genuinely baffled: "Lord, when did we see you?" The condemned didn't know they were failing the king. They ask the same question from the other side. Neither group had a checklist. Neither was performing for an audience. The ones who passed weren't even aware they had been tested. What they were doing was simply responding to need when they encountered it, without calculating whether the person in front of them was worth the trouble.

The king's answer is the heart of everything: "Whatever you did for one of the least of these brothers of mine, you did for me." The whole world turns out to have been saturated with his presence all along — hidden in the faces of the people nobody wanted to look at.

This is the kind of king the whole liturgical year has been preparing us to meet. Not a king who demands tribute and displays power, but a king who went into exile with his people, who was himself hungry and a stranger and sick and in prison, and who now meets us in everyone who is any of those things.

To carry today

Think of one person in your life — or just within reach of your ordinary days — who is easy to overlook: someone whose need you've noticed but filed away as not your problem. The claim of today's feast is that something is actually at stake in that moment of turning toward them or away. Ask yourself what it would take to turn toward, even once, even a little.
