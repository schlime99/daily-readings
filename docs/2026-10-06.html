Twenty-seventh Week in Ordinary Time

Paul was known before he knew himself, and Mary chose what could not be taken away.

First readingGalatians 1:13-24
PsalmPsalm 139:1b-3, 13-14ab, 14c-15
GospelLuke 10:38-42

There is a quiet question underneath both readings today, not asked aloud but present in every line: who are you, really, and who decided? Paul gives his answer in the form of a testimony. Mary gives hers by sitting down. Neither answer looks like the obvious one.

The first reading — Galatians 1:13-24

Galatians is Paul's most urgent letter, written in a state somewhere between argument and alarm. The churches in Galatia had been visited by teachers after Paul left, teachers who insisted that Gentile converts had to take on the full obligations of the Jewish law — circumcision, dietary codes, the whole inherited structure — in order to belong to the people of God. Paul writes to push back, hard, and the first chapter is his opening move: an account of where his authority comes from. The answer he gives is not what you might expect. It does not come from Jerusalem, from the other apostles, from any recognized institution. It comes from a direct encounter with the risen Christ on the road to Damascus, and from the mystery of a vocation God had determined before Paul ever drew breath.

The lines about his former life in Judaism are worth sitting with. Paul does not minimize what he did. He persecuted the Church "beyond measure" and tried to destroy it — his word is strong, almost violent. He was, by his own account, extraordinarily accomplished in his tradition, more zealous than his peers, the last person anyone would have picked for this particular role. And that is exactly the point he is making. The one who was most thoroughly opposed to the gospel became its most tireless carrier. This was not human strategy; no committee would have chosen it. Paul is at pains to say that when the revelation came, he did not consult anyone, did not go to Jerusalem, did not check his new understanding against the established authorities. He went to Arabia — where, we do not know exactly — and then returned to Damascus, and only after three years did he go up to meet Peter, spending just fifteen days with him before heading off again to Syria and Cilicia.

Why does any of this matter? Because Paul is establishing that his gospel was not handed to him secondhand, edited by others, softened by committee. He received it from the Lord. And the churches who had once been his targets, hearing that this same man was now preaching the faith he had set out to destroy, drew the only reasonable conclusion: God had done something here. "They glorified God because of me." The persecutor had become the proof.

Psalm 139 is the perfect answer to all of this. "You knit me in my mother's womb" — which is almost the same thing Paul says about himself, that God had set him apart from his mother's womb. The psalm insists that there is a self that precedes our own self-knowledge, known in the dark before there was light, formed before there was formation. Paul experienced this not as a comfortable idea but as a shattering event. The psalm turns it into prayer.

The Gospel — Luke 10:38-42

Martha and Mary are sisters, and they are hosting Jesus in their home. The scene is domestic and entirely recognizable. Martha is working — the word Luke uses carries the sense of real labor, active service, the genuine work of hospitality. Mary is sitting at Jesus's feet, listening. To sit at a teacher's feet was the posture of a disciple, and in the first century this was not a posture usually available to women. Mary has placed herself there anyway.

Martha, strained and frustrated, comes to Jesus with what is really a complaint dressed as a question: doesn't he care that she has been left to manage everything alone? It is a completely human moment. Anyone who has ever been the one making sure the dinner happens while someone else has a lovely conversation in the other room knows exactly how Martha feels. The irritation is real. The sense of unfairness is real.

Jesus does not tell Martha that her work is worthless or that she has done wrong. He says her name twice — "Martha, Martha" — which in the Gospels is always a sign of tenderness, not rebuke. He says she is anxious and worried about many things, and that there is need of only one thing. Mary has chosen it, and it will not be taken from her. He does not tell Martha to sit down. He tells her what is happening.

The word anxious here is the same word Paul will use in Philippians for the worry he instructs the community to lay down. It is not busyness itself that is the problem; it is the particular quality of scattered, driven, self-multiplying concern that cuts a person off from the present moment. Mary is entirely present to the one thing in front of her.

The place where Paul and Mary meet

These two readings are not forced together — they share genuine ground. Paul's whole point in Galatians 1 is that his vocation required him to stop consulting every other authority and simply receive what God was giving. Mary's choice is the same movement made visible in a single afternoon: she sets aside what everyone would expect her to be doing and simply sits in the presence of the one who matters most. Both of them, in their very different ways, are refusing to let the urgent crowd out the necessary.

To carry today

Think of the thing you are most anxious about managing well right now — some obligation, some responsibility, some version of yourself you are working hard to maintain. Ask whether that particular anxiety is helping you do the work better, or whether it is keeping you from being present to what is actually in front of you. The invitation is not to stop working, but to notice when worry has quietly become the point.
