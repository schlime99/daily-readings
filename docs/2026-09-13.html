Twenty-Fourth Sunday in Ordinary Time

The servant who was forgiven everything went out and forgave nothing.

First readingSirach 27:30—28:7
PsalmPsalm 103:1-2, 3-4, 9-10, 11-12
Second readingRomans 14:7-9
GospelMatthew 18:21-35

Forgiveness is the subject today, and the Church does not approach it gently. Three readings and a psalm all press on the same nerve, from three different directions, and by the end of the Gospel the pressure is nearly uncomfortable. That discomfort is worth sitting with rather than explaining away.

The first reading — Sirach 27:30—28:7

The book of Sirach sits in a part of the Old Testament that Protestants call the Apocrypha, which means many readers come to it as strangers. It is a book of wisdom literature, written by a Jerusalem scribe named Ben Sira sometime in the second century before Christ — a grandfather's book, in a way, full of hard-won practical teaching about how to live well before God and among other people. This passage comes from a section where Ben Sira is cataloguing the passions that ruin a person's life, and he lands on wrath with particular force.

His argument is logical to the point of being almost ruthless. "Could anyone nourish anger against another and expect healing from the LORD?" The question is not rhetorical in the way that sounds pious; it is rhetorical in the way that sounds obvious, like asking whether you can blow out a candle and still read by its flame. If you want mercy from God, you have to be someone who traffics in mercy — and you cannot do that while clutching a grudge. The sinner, Ben Sira says, hugs wrath tight, which is a remarkably vivid image. Wrath here is not an emotion you fall into; it is something you hold, something you refuse to put down.

The instruction to "remember your last days" is not morbid — it is clarifying. Ben Sira is saying: when you are lying on your deathbed, the wrong your neighbor did you will look very different. You will be the one begging for pardon. Think of that now, while there is still time to act on it.

Psalm 103 answers this directly, and beautifully. It is one of the great mercy psalms, and the line chosen as the refrain — The Lord is kind and merciful, slow to anger, and rich in compassion — functions almost as a rebuke by contrast. God, who has every right to keep accounts perfectly, does not. "As far as the east is from the west, so far has he put our transgressions from us." That image was chosen deliberately: east and west, unlike north and south, never meet. The distance is infinite. God's forgiveness is not amnesia, but it is final.

The second reading — Romans 14:7-9

Paul's letter to the Romans is running on a somewhat separate track from the other readings today. Chapter 14 is concerned with disputes between Christians over food laws and holy days — the kind of community friction that does not look like our friction but is, underneath, identical to it. Paul's answer to that friction, though, lifts the argument far above the particular dispute. None of us lives for ourselves. Whether we live or die, we belong to the Lord. Christ died and rose precisely to be Lord over all of us — the living and the dead. We are, all of us, already in the same situation before him.

That framing matters quietly for the other readings. If no one lives for themselves, then holding a grievance as though it were a private possession starts to look strange. The person who wronged you also belongs to the Lord. So do you. Both of you are already standing on the same ground.

The Gospel — Matthew 18:21-35

Peter's question is generous by any ordinary measure. Seven times is already far more than most people's patience will stretch. And Jesus's answer — not seven times, but seventy-seven — is not a new number. It is a way of saying the question itself has the wrong shape. You should not be counting.

The parable that follows is one of those Gospel stories where the logic is so clear that you wonder how anyone could miss it, and then you realize that you miss it constantly. The first servant owes the king what the text calls "a huge amount" — the Greek suggests something like ten thousand talents, which a laborer would have needed decades to earn. The debt is not just large; it is absurd, unpayable by any realistic effort. The king forgives it entirely, out of compassion, because the man begged him.

That same servant then goes and chokes a fellow servant over what amounts to pocket change. The disproportion is stark, and it is designed to be. The fellow servants who witness it are "deeply disturbed" — literally troubled, grieved. Something has gone wrong that is visible even to bystanders.

The hard ending — the master handing the wicked servant over to torturers — is not comfortable, and we should not make it so. Jesus is not speaking metaphorically here about mild consequences. He is saying that the mercy God extends to us is not separate from the mercy we extend to others; it is bound up with it. The offer has a shape, and we can refuse its shape while pretending to accept it.

What forgiveness actually costs

What links Sirach and the Gospel is the realism both bring to the question. Neither pretends forgiveness is easy or natural or painless. Sirach says you have to choose it, actively, against your instincts. Jesus shows through the parable what it looks like when someone is forgiven everything and the experience passes through them without changing them at all. The servant leaves the throne room still the same man. He received mercy but did not let it touch him.

The suggestion in both readings is that genuine forgiveness — the kind that flows from having understood what you yourself have been given — actually does change a person. It costs something, and it leaves a mark.

To carry today

Think of one person toward whom you are currently nursing something — resentment, distance, a quiet satisfaction that things have not gone well for them. You do not have to feel warmly toward them today. But ask yourself honestly: do you want to let it go, or have you grown comfortable holding it? Ask for the grace to want what you do not yet quite want.
