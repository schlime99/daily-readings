Thirty-fourth Week in Ordinary Time

Heaven and earth will pass away, but a new city is already coming down.

First readingRevelation 20:1-4, 11—21:2
PsalmPsalm 84:3-6, 8
GospelLuke 21:29-33

We are at the last days of the liturgical year — the final Friday before the new year begins with Advent — and the readings know exactly where they are. Everything here is oriented toward an ending that turns out to be a beginning, a destruction that clears the way for something unimaginably better. The tone is not anxious. It is almost luminous.

The first reading — Revelation 20:1-4, 11—21:2

The Book of Revelation is the strangest book in the New Testament, and it suffers badly when it is treated as a coded timeline for future catastrophes rather than what it actually is: a letter written to persecuted Christians in the late first century, full of symbols borrowed from Jewish apocalyptic literature, meant to assure people who were suffering that God was not absent and that the empire crushing them would not have the last word. The beasts, the marks, the thrones — all of this would have been recognizable shorthand to its original readers, pointing toward Rome and its demands for emperor worship.

Today's passage jumps across a gap — the lectionary skips a large middle section — and lands us at two separate but related visions. In the first, John sees those who were killed for refusing to worship the beast. They were beheaded, which is a specific Roman execution. They had refused the empire's ultimatum, and here they are, reigning with Christ. The vindication is quiet but total.

Then comes the great white throne, and this is one of the most arresting images in all of scripture: the earth and the sky flee from the presence of the one seated there, and "there was no place for them." Everything that seemed permanent and fixed simply cannot hold its ground before this presence. The dead — the great and the lowly both, notice — stand before the throne, and the scrolls are opened. Death and Hades, those two old powers that have haunted human existence from the beginning, are thrown into the pool of fire. They are not simply defeated. They are ended.

And then — this is the turn the whole passage has been building toward — John sees a new heaven and a new earth. The old order has passed. The sea is no more. (In Jewish symbolic thought, the sea often stood for chaos and threat; its absence means those things are simply gone.) And descending out of heaven is the holy city, the new Jerusalem, "prepared as a bride adorned for her husband." After all the violence and judgment, the last image is a wedding. The whole apocalypse ends in a love story.

Psalm 84 responds to this with a kind of ache. "My soul yearns and pines for the courts of the LORD." The psalmist is not yet there — not yet inside the house — but is reaching toward it. Even the sparrow finds a nest at God's altar, and the swallow a place for her young. If a bird can find a home in the courts of the Lord, so can the longing human heart. The refrain chosen for today is taken directly from Revelation itself: "Here God lives among his people." The psalm and the first reading are singing the same thing in two different registers.

The Gospel — Luke 21:29-33

The parable of the fig tree is brief and earthly, which is part of its charm after the cosmic fireworks of Revelation. Watch the trees, Jesus says. When you see the buds burst open, you do not need an expert to tell you that summer is coming — you simply know it, in your bones, from having watched seasons turn. In the same way, when you see the signs he has been describing in this chapter (turmoil, the desolation of Jerusalem, heavenly disturbances), you can know that the Kingdom of God is near.

This is not a frightening instruction. It is almost a comfort: the signs of unraveling are not evidence that things have gone terribly wrong, but that the ending is working as it should, that something new is pressing to be born. The disciples do not need to be afraid of what they see; they need to become the kind of people who can read it correctly.

The final sentence is the one that rings out: "Heaven and earth will pass away, but my words will not pass away." We just heard, in Revelation, that the earth and sky flee from the throne. Even those great constants go. But the word of Christ is not among the things that pass. It is on the other side of the ending, still standing.

What stays when everything passes

These two readings are not really on separate tracks at all. Revelation shows the architecture of the ending: judgment, the destruction of death itself, and then the new city descending. The parable offers a posture for how to live while that ending approaches — not with terror, but with the attentiveness of a farmer watching the trees. The fig tree does not explain what summer is; it just shows you the edge of it. The signs of the Kingdom's coming are like that. They are not the Kingdom, but they are its announcement.

What holds everything together is the conviction that God's word and God's presence outlast the cosmos. The new Jerusalem comes down from God, adorned as a bride. It is not something human beings construct out of their effort. It arrives.

To carry today

Think about what you are treating as permanent that probably is not — a worry, a grudge, a fixed picture of how your life is supposed to look. Consider that the things which feel most immovable are often the ones that cannot hold their ground before what is coming. Ask for the grace to hold them loosely enough to notice when the buds are beginning to open.
