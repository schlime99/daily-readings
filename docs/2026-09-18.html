Twenty-fourth Week in Ordinary Time

A company of healed women walks the roads of Galilee, and the Church begins.

First reading1 Corinthians 15:12-20
PsalmPsalm 17:1, 6-7, 8, 15
GospelLuke 8:1-3

Paul is making the most urgent argument of his life, and three women whose names we know — Mary, Joanna, Susanna — are walking dusty roads with Jesus and funding the whole enterprise. These two readings are on separate tracks, as weekday readings often are, but each carries its own weight, and together they make a quietly astonishing day.

The first reading — 1 Corinthians 15:12-20

Paul is writing to Corinth, a city that considered itself very sophisticated about spiritual things, and some of the Corinthian Christians had apparently decided they could keep the faith while quietly dropping the resurrection of the dead. Perhaps they thought resurrection was a crude, bodily idea beneath the dignity of people who had moved on to higher spiritual things. Paul will have none of it.

What he does here is build a logical chain so tight it almost clangs. If there is no resurrection of the dead as a general truth, he says, then Christ has not been raised — because Christ is one of the dead. And if Christ has not been raised, then everything falls: the preaching is hollow, the faith is hollow, the apostles are liars, and the people who have already died as Christians have simply perished. He does not soften any of this. He follows the logic all the way to its worst conclusion and leaves it sitting there.

The line "if for this life only we have hoped in Christ, we are the most pitiable people of all" is worth pausing over. Paul is not saying that Christian ethics or community or meaning are worthless — he is saying that if death is the final word, then betting everything on a crucified man who stayed dead is a genuinely terrible strategy, and the people who did so deserve our pity. That is a bracing honesty. Paul is not offering Christianity as a useful framework for living a more intentional life. He is staking everything on whether something actually happened.

Then the turn: but now Christ has been raised. The word "firstfruits" is rich and specific. In the ancient Jewish harvest, the very first portion of the crop was brought to the temple as an offering before anyone ate anything — it was the pledge that the rest was coming, that the harvest was real and the whole of it belonged to God. Paul is saying that what happened to Christ on Easter morning is not an isolated miracle but the opening event of a general resurrection still unfolding. The dead are not abandoned. The harvest has begun.

Psalm 17 answers this beautifully. It is the prayer of someone who believes he will see God's face — "on waking, I shall be content in your presence." That last phrase carries everything Paul has just argued, compressed into a single line from a psalm that was old when Jesus was born.

The Gospel — Luke 8:1-3

Luke gives us three verses that are easy to read as a transitional note — Jesus traveling, the Twelve with him, some women providing support — but it is worth sitting with what is actually being described. This group moving through the towns and villages of Galilee is the original Church on the road. And the women Luke names are not background scenery.

Mary Magdalene is identified by what she has been freed from — seven demons, which in the ancient world meant something overwhelming, a life that had been comprehensively taken over by forces she could not control. She is walking free now, and she is walking with Jesus. Joanna is the wife of Herod's household manager, which means she is a woman of some means and also a woman with something to lose — her presence here is not without risk. Susanna we know nothing else about, which somehow makes her feel more real, not less.

Luke says these women "provided for them out of their resources." The Greek word behind "provided" is the root of our word "deacon" — they were serving the mission materially, making it possible for Jesus and the Twelve to keep moving. The tradition that came to call Mary Magdalene "apostle to the apostles" because she carried the resurrection news to the disciples is rooted in exactly this kind of close, committed presence. She was not peripheral to the story. She was part of its funding, its staffing, its daily forward motion.

Gratitude as the shape of discipleship

What connects these two readings is less a theme than a posture. Paul stakes everything on a real event because he believes the real event changes everything about death, and about what life is worth spending on. The women in Luke are doing exactly that — spending their resources, their social standing, their ordinary lives on what they have experienced and now believe. They have been healed, and they are moving.

Discipleship in both readings looks the same: it comes out of something received, and it spends itself in response. Not as duty, but as overflow. The women do not seem to be following an obligation; they are following a person who gave them their lives back. That is the shape of it — gratitude that finds its legs and walks.

To carry today

Think about what you have actually received — not in the abstract, but concretely: a moment of clarity, a recovery, a relationship that pulled you back from a bad direction. Ask yourself whether you are living as though that gift was real, the way these women lived as though their healing was real. Gratitude that doesn't change how we move through the day is gratitude we haven't quite finished receiving.
